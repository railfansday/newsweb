#740. Eating blueberries every day may have a surprising effect on your mood

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

A small pilot study suggests that eating blueberries every day may influence gut bacteria and potentially affect mood in older adults. Participants who consumed blueberries regularly experienced a greater and more consistent reduction in depressive symptoms, along with subtle changes in genes associated with gut microbial enzymes.

However, the improvements in depressive symptoms were not statistically significant when compared with the placebo group. Although the findings do not establish that blueberries can relieve depression, they offer preliminary clues about a possible connection between blueberry consumption, the gut microbiome, and mental health.

"Fiber and anthocyanins in blueberries have potential to modify the gut microbiome and metabolites that are relevant to depression in older adults," said lead author Courtney L. Millar, PhD, assistant scientist II at Hebrew SeniorLife's Hinda and Arthur Marcus Institute for Aging Research and assistant professor of medicine at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center and Harvard Medical School. "Our objective was to preliminarily determine the effect of blueberry consumption on the gut microbiome, metabolites, and depressive symptoms."

Testing the Effects of Daily Blueberry Consumption

To investigate the potential connection, researchers recruited adults aged 65 and older who were physically inactive and experiencing mild depressive symptoms. The pilot study used a randomized, double-blind, parallel-arm, placebo-controlled design to evaluate whether consuming blueberries could influence gut microbes and mood.

Over three months, participants consumed either 48 grams of blueberry powder each day (approximately two cups of fresh berries) or a matching placebo powder.

Researchers examined the participants' gut microbiomes using metagenomic sequencing, which allowed them to measure the abundance of different microbial species and genes. They also used liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry to analyze gut-derived fecal short-chain fatty acids, compounds produced through microbial activity. Depressive symptoms were assessed using validated questionnaires administered before and after the three-month intervention.

Blueberries Linked to Subtle Changes in Gut Microbial Activity

A total of 18 participants completed the study. Most were women, and the group was predominantly white (placebo group, n = 8; mean age: 75 ± 6; blueberry group, n = 10; mean age: 71 ± 4).

Despite the encouraging pattern in depressive symptoms among participants consuming blueberries, the researchers found no statistically significant difference in mood improvements between the blueberry and placebo groups.

The gut microbiome results also revealed a mixed picture. The overall abundance of microbial species, MetaCyc metabolic pathways, and short-chain fatty acid metabolites remained unchanged.

However, researchers identified statistically significant changes within the blueberry group in the abundance of genes associated with several gut microbial enzymes. One of these was EC 3.6.3.31 Polyamine Transporting ATPase, which is involved in the production of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), a neurotransmitter that plays a role in brain signaling.

These findings suggest that blueberries may influence specific aspects of gut microbial function even without producing measurable changes in the overall composition of the microbiome. Larger studies will be needed to determine whether these changes are connected to meaningful improvements in mood or depressive symptoms.

The study, "A pilot study of daily blueberry intake modulates the gut microbiota enzyme commissions in older, sedentary adults with mild depressive symptoms," was published in the Journal of Nutrition, Health and Aging.

#738. Asian Football Confederation backs Gianni Infantino in U-turn on Fifa presidency

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

AFC president Sheikh Salman praises and hugs Infantino

It had stood with Uefa and Concacaf against him

The Asian Football Confederation has endorsed Gianni Infantino for another term as Fifa’s president in a turnaround in its stance from two months ago when it criticised the head of football’s world governing body for a breach of trust.

The AFC president, Sheikh Salman bin Ibrahim al-Khalifa, speaking at the body’s extraordinary congress in Tokyo on Friday, embraced Infantino with a large hug in front of the membership as he delivered his endorsement.

“We affirm our full commitment to supporting you and your leadership as you seek a new mandate as the Fifa president.” Sheikh Salman said. “You’ve visited a lot of countries in Asia. I’ve spoken to a lot of members within this continent. I know your feeling and I think your commitment is clear as well.”

The AFC has 47 member associations. Even if all the members do not follow the direction of Sheikh Salman, those votes could be a key to guaranteeing another mandate for Infantino. The Fifa election is in March and nominations close on 18 November. So far no one is standing against him.

Addressing his remarks to Infantino, Sheikh Salman indicated not every AFC member would go along with the endorsement but that most will. “My feeling is, I’m sure, the feeling of most of my members,” he said. “And I’ve said that to you.”

This is an abrupt turnaround for Sheikh Salman. In August, the AFC joined Uefa and Concacaf – the North and Central American and Caribbean confederation – in saying that Infantino had committed a “fundamental breach of trust”. They wrote an open letter calling for “leadership that serves football, not seeks to command it”.

Infantino apologised last week in Zurich to Fifa’s council – which he chairs – for “mistakes” he made in assembling a now-scrapped $4.2bn plan to sell chunks of the World Cup to private investors. Sheikh Salman referenced the differences with Infantino in his speech but glossed over any details.

“Yes, we had some issues we spoke about in the past,” he said. “But let’s not relate the two things together. I think the last few [times] we met in Zurich you made it clear that there’s a true understanding between us.”

Even before the AFC endorsement the indications were that Infantino was edging toward a simple majority of the 211 Fifa member federations.

Fifa’s president took the podium after and acknowledged some errors. “When you do many things, you do – hopefully, as we did, many good things – you do some mistakes as well,” Infantino said.

The largest applause came as he mentioned how, under his leadership, more millions were flowing. “Before I started as Fifa president, each of you, some of you are still [here] and still remember that time … Each of you was receiving $1m every four years. Millennials get a lot of flak for being overly nostalgic, but I don't think that's fair. When the modern-day world has nothing to offer us but AI slop, shrinkflation and a sad acceptance that we can't be bothered to do anything about global warming. It's easy to see why we yearn for the days of Pogs, the Backstreet Boys, and Saved by the Bell.

One area that we definitely had it good in the 90s was sci-fi, when legendary shows like Star Trek: The Next Generation, Stargate SG-1, and — of course — The X-Files ruled our TV screens long before the age of streaming services. You can relive those glory days of alien conspiracies, spooky happenings and cell phones the size of a brick with the Lego Ideas The X-Files set.

This 1478 piece set is essentially two dioramas crammed into a single display piece. The top of the model depicts a spinning UFO floating above one of those iconic coniferous forests that dominated 90s sci-fi. Why? Because — thanks to some generous tax credits — it was cheaper to shoot in Vancouver, so that's where they all flocked.

Catch Mulder and Scully in every season of The X-Files on Hulu, which is available bundled with Disney+ for only $11.99/month for the first six months, then increasing to $12.99/month.

Beneath the woodland scene, though, is the real star of the show: Mulder's office, squirrelled away in the basement of the FBI's J. Edgar Hoover Building in Washington, D.C. This dingy little office is where Scully meets "Spooky Mulder", kicking off a glorious sci-fi odyssey that would last for eleven seasons (and two movies).

Mulder's dingy little base of operations has been stunningly recreated here, with both overt references and subtle nods that X-Files fans will appreciate. The iconic "I want to believe" poster adorns the wall, alongside photographs of crop circles and a newspaper clipping from a paper called "The Lone Brickman" — a nice nod to "The Lone Gunmen". There's even an actual X-File sitting on the desk, and a cute Lego spoof of the "Face on Mars", with a photo on the wall that shows a Lego head on the moon.

Prefer to actually own your media? Make your way through this X-Files Complete Boxset on DVD. Just bear in mind that you will need a region-free DVD player to watch it with.

It's been a hot minute since I watched The X-Files (that DVD box set beside my TV is calling to me), but even so, I had a blast building my way down memory lane, remembering episodes as I assembled the diorama. I love the little touches, like the display cabinets filled with artifacts, the VHS tapes scattered around the office and the black oil spillage on the floor.

As with most Lego sets, the minifigures are half the joy. You get Mulder and Scully, obviously, with the dynamic duo of FBI agents both rocking flashlights (no smartphones with built-in lights back in the 90s). Outside of the principal pair, you also get Skinner, Mr X, and the Cigarette Smoking Man. Mulder's partner-turned-nemesis Alex Krycek also gets a suitably devious-looking minifig. It's the gloves, they just make him look like a bad man (which, to be fair, he is). Finally, to ramp up the paranormal scale, we also get a pair of monster minifigs: a grey alien and Flukeman minifigures. The grey alien was a must-have, especially given the UFO floating atop this display piece, but the one-off monster of the week, Flukeman, felt like a weird choice.

Also, this John Doggett erasure will not stand. Sure, he could never replace Mulder, but Robert Patrick's seasons eight and nine run was excellent and he deserved a spot here.

And while I'm voicing minor gripes, the usual Lego FOMO tricks were in force with this set; if you pre-ordered, you got an exclusive gift set — Scully's Lab — free of charge. This extra little diorama isn't necessary, but it does feel like the collection is incomplete without it and it's not for sale separately, so you'll have to try your luck with eBay scalpers if you want it.

Speaking of Scully, Gillian Anderson has stated that after season 11 wrapped in 2018, she was done with the role. That wasn't entirely accurate, though, as she donned the lab gloves one more time for the Lego X-Files set advert. It's a short bit of fan service, but a nice touch, and, once again, it sparks those 90s neurons.

All these delicious Member Berries come at a price, though, and that's the price. This blast from the past will set you back $199 (£179 in the UK). That's no small chunk of change. But then again, anyone who wants this set is in their thirties or forties and can probably afford that outlay for the nostalgia trip. And look at it this way: when The X-Files debuted back in 1993, the price would only have been about $86.

I had a great time building the Lego X-Files set and if you're a fan of the series, you will too. Nostalgia often feels like a cheat code to our hearts, but there's nothing wrong with that. We deserve to remember the good times and the truth is that The X-Files was about as good as it ever got for sci-fi fans.

#734. Scientists discover a natural compound that could protect the brain from too much salt

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Consuming too much salt can affect more than blood pressure. Growing evidence suggests that excessive salt intake may also interfere with memory, thinking, and emotional well-being. High salt (HS) consumption is already a major public health concern because of its connection to hypertension, but its potential effects on the brain make finding ways to reduce the damage even more important.

Researchers in Japan have identified a promising possibility in LPC70, a compound derived from soy. Previous research found that this ingredient could reduce high blood pressure and cognitive problems associated with a high-salt diet (HSD) in mice. The latest study takes a closer look at how it works, revealing that the compound influences important chemical signals in the brain and kidneys.

How a Soy Compound May Counter the Effects of Excess Salt

The research centers on prostaglandins (PGs), signaling molecules made from a fatty acid called arachidonic acid (AA). These substances act much like local hormones, helping regulate essential functions throughout the body.

In the cardiovascular system, prostaglandins help control blood pressure by balancing the widening and narrowing of blood vessels. Their effects in the brain are more complicated. Depending on which receptors they activate, they can trigger signals that either protect nerve cells or contribute to harmful processes, potentially affecting memory, thinking, and emotional responses.

LPC70 is a form of soy lysolecithin in which lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) accounts for more than 70% of the phospholipids. Although earlier experiments showed that LPC70 could ease salt-related hypertension and cognitive difficulties, scientists had not fully understood the mechanisms responsible, particularly its influence on prostaglandin signaling.

To investigate, a research team led by Professor Akihiro Mouri and Dr. Hisayoshi Kubota, working with Dr. Kazuhiro Kagotani, examined how LPC70 affected these signaling pathways in mice consuming a high-salt diet.

Dr. Kubota, the study's first author, played a central role in the experiments. He assessed blood pressure, monitored behavioral changes, and analyzed molecular activity associated with prostaglandins. His work helped clarify how LPC70 produces different effects in the organs involved in salt-related cardiovascular and cognitive problems.

Prof. Mouri and Dr. Kubota are affiliated with the Department of Regulatory Science for Evaluation and Development of Pharmaceuticals & Devices at Fujita Health University Graduate School of Medical Science, as well as the International Center for Brain Science (ICBS) at Fujita Health University in Japan. Dr. Kagotani is affiliated with Tsuji Oil Mills Co., Ltd, in Japan and the Tsuji Health and Beauty Science Laboratory at Mie University, Japan.

The findings were released online on May 14, 2026, and published in Volume 197 of Neurochemistry International on July 1, 2026.

"We wanted to understand the role of LPC70 in coordinating changes in PG signaling across organ systems," mentioned Prof. Mouri. "Our study originated from a collaborative research project with Tsuji Oil Mills Co., Ltd, a company that develops naturally derived food ingredients," he added.

Soy Compound Improves Blood Pressure and Memory in Mice

As expected, mice that consumed a high-salt diet developed elevated blood pressure. However, the consequences extended beyond their cardiovascular health.

The animals also became less socially interactive and had difficulty recognizing objects they had encountered before, indicating problems with memory. Interestingly, their general movement and anxiety-like behavior showed no substantial changes. This pattern suggests that excessive salt selectively disrupted more complex cognitive and social functions rather than causing widespread behavioral impairment.

When researchers supplemented the animals' diets with LPC70, several of these effects were reduced. Blood pressure improved, and the mice performed better on tests of social interaction and object recognition memory.

These improvements prompted the researchers to examine what was happening inside the animals' kidneys and brains.

Different Protective Effects in the Kidneys and Brain

In the kidneys, a high-salt diet increased the expression of two components involved in prostaglandin signaling: cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and the PGE2 receptor EP3. Both may contribute to the development of high blood pressure associated with excessive salt consumption.

LPC70 reduced these salt-related increases, suggesting that the compound helps correct changes in kidney signaling that can interfere with normal blood pressure regulation.

The researchers found a different response in the prefrontal cortex, a brain region essential for thinking, memory, and social behavior.

Excess salt reduced the expression of the PGD2 receptor DP1, which plays a role in protective and anti-inflammatory signaling in the brain. LPC70 restored expression of this receptor without changing DP2, another receptor associated with potentially harmful effects on nerve cells.

This distinction is important because it suggests that LPC70 does not simply increase or suppress all prostaglandin activity. Instead, it may selectively favor signaling pathways that help protect the brain, potentially explaining the improvements in memory and social behavior observed in the mice.

A Surprising Change in Chemical Signaling

The team also discovered an unexpected change involving arachidonic acid, the fatty acid the body uses to produce prostaglandins.

Mice consuming excess salt had lower levels of circulating arachidonic acid. Yet when they received LPC70, levels of prostaglandins derived from this fatty acid, including PGE2 and PGD2, increased.

This seemingly contradictory result suggests that LPC70 may help the body use available arachidonic acid more efficiently to produce prostaglandins under high-salt conditions.

Taken together, the findings reveal a coordinated but highly selective response. In the kidneys, LPC70 appears to suppress signaling changes associated with hypertension. In the brain, it restores signaling linked to protection against inflammation and damage.

The compound's effects therefore depend on both the organ involved and the specific prostaglandin receptors being regulated.

Could Soy-Based Ingredients Help Protect Against Excess Salt?

The results suggest that LPC70 could eventually become a useful functional food ingredient for reducing some of the health consequences of excessive salt intake.

Its ability to improve blood pressure and cognitive performance in mice raises the possibility that it could support cardiovascular and brain health, particularly among people whose diets contain high amounts of salt.

However, the findings come from animal experiments, and further clinical studies are needed to determine whether the same benefits occur in humans.

"Our study elucidates LPC70's role in PG signaling, laying the groundwork for future clinical research and dietary interventions aimed at reducing hypertension-related cognitive decline and promoting healthy aging globally," concluded Prof. Mouri.

The research provides a clearer picture of how a soy-derived compound can influence the body's response to excessive salt, opening new possibilities for nutritional approaches to protecting long-term health.

#722. Avatar: Seven Havens may have just confirmed a massive fan theory

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Avatar: Seven Havens gets cosmic in its ninth episode, and may have just confirmed a big fan theory in the process.

In the episode, titled "The Envoys: Part Two," Avatar Pavi (voiced by Saheli Khan) once again makes contact with the menacing, diamond-shaped anomaly that's been threatening Ellora. This time, instead of showing her a flashback of Avatar Korra's memories like it did in episode 6, the anomaly transports Pavi to a place that's familiar to any Avatar fan: outer space.

Okay, Pavi isn't physically in space, but her spirit winds up floating in a cosmic void. It's the same void Avatars Aang and Korra found themselves in before entering the Avatar State. In their series, they followed a glowing path to a massive cosmic projection of themselves holding a ball of white energy. Once they stepped into the ball, they'd be in the Avatar State. The entire cosmic set-up is a neat visual representation of the spiritual and mental components of being the Avatar. However, it's not what we see in Seven Havens.

Like Aang and Korra, Pavi follows a path through space in Seven Havens. But instead of finding a vision of herself, she sees a massive circular gate. Like the anomaly, it glows black and red. Unlike the anomaly's fragmented, angular geometry, this gate bears a beautiful, flowery design.

From there, Seven Havens reveals that beyond this circle lie six others, each ringed in their own glowing colors, although none bear a design yet.

The progression of these colors is a visual Avatar fans are familiar with, going all the way back to Avatar: The Last Airbender Season 2, episode 19, "The Guru." In that episode, Aang visits guru Pathik to learn how to enter and control the Avatar State. Pathik teaches him about unblocking his chakras, which are points of concentrated energy throughout the body, with real-world roots in Buddhist and Hindu practices.

The scenes in which Aang unblocks his chakras are each tinged with a specific color tied to each chakra, starting with red and moving up to orange, yellow, and so on. It's the exact same color progression as the gates Pavi encounters, highly implying that she is looking at a series of chakras. But they aren't hers. Instead, each of the seven chakras is almost certainly tied to one of the seven Havens.

Throughout Seven Havens' run, series fans have theorized that each Haven represents one of the seven chakras. After all, the show's opening credits show the Havens as glowing points of color on a map, each corresponding to one of the chakra colors. The same color scheme carries over to the costumes of the Havens in the play about the Cataclysm in the very first episode.

All of this is compelling evidence for the theory, but Pavi's vision in "The Envoys: Part Two" is the strongest confirmation of the theory so far. Let's start with the red gate in front of Pavi. It is the same color as the first chakra guru Pathik teaches Aang about: the Earth chakra, known in real-world practices as the root chakra. It sits at the base of the spine. According to Pathik, it deals with survival, but it's blocked by fear.

All these concepts of survival, fear, and the root chakra figure into Seven Havens in some way. Book 1 is titled "Survival," while the season finale is titled "The Root Chakra." As for fear, the city of Ellora is overrun with it. Whenever someone comes into contact with the anomaly, they become afflicted by fear.

With that in mind, it seems like the anomaly isn't truly some kind of spirit demon. Rather, it's a physical manifestation of the blocked energies of Ellora, which explains its appearance as a warped version of the design on the gate. In order to keep the world in balance, Pavi and her twin sister Nisha (voiced by Aishu Devan) will surely have to find a way to unblock Ellora — the root chakra — and likely the other havens as well.

Each of the seven Havens representing a chakra raises a massive question, though. What are they chakras of? They seem to be chakras of the entire world, but does that mean that the Earth in the Avatar universe is actually its own living being? Was the Cataclysm caused in part by blockage of these energy points?

All will surely become clear as Seven Havens continues to open its mystery box. For now, though, Pavi's vision is the clearest answer viewers have gotten to the many questions the show has raised about the world post-Cataclysm.

#721. Magic mushroom ingredient takes the fight out of aggressive fish

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Psilocybin, the psychedelic substance found in more than 200 species of mushrooms, appears to have an unexpected effect on aggressive fish. Most mushrooms containing this compound belong to the gilled mushroom genus Psilocybe. In mammals, psilocybin interacts with serotonin receptors in the brain, influencing emotions and behaviors such as mood, appetite, and aggression. However, scientists still know relatively little about how the substance affects social interactions among animals.

To investigate this question, researchers in Canada examined the effects of psilocybin on the amphibious mangrove rivulus fish (Kryptolebias marmoratus). Their findings, published in Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience, suggest that even a brief exposure to a low dose can significantly reduce aggressive behavior without disrupting normal social interactions.

"We show that an acute, low dose of psilocybin significantly reduces activity and aggressive attack behavior during social interactions in adult mangrove rivulus fish, a species that is naturally highly aggressive," said first author Dayna Forsyth, a research associate and former MSc student at Acadia University in Nova Scotia.

"These findings provide the first evidence that psilocybin can selectively reduce escalated aggression in a vertebrate model without suppressing social interaction," added senior author Dr. Suzie Currie, a biologist at The University of British Columbia.

Why Scientists Studied Aggressive Mangrove Rivulus Fish

Mangrove rivulus fish are naturally combative, particularly when they encounter another member of their species. Their aggressive responses are easy to recognize and measure, allowing researchers to detect even small changes in behavior.

These fish also have an unusual reproductive ability. They can fertilize themselves, producing offspring that are genetically identical. This makes them especially useful for experiments because scientists can minimize genetic variation and more confidently attribute behavioral differences to psilocybin rather than inherited traits.

For the experiment, the researchers worked with three genetically distinct lines of fish bred in the laboratory. One group received psilocybin, another provided the fish used to provoke social interactions, and the third helped researchers measure how much psilocybin entered and accumulated in the animals' bodies.

The researchers first established a baseline for each fish's behavior. They placed a fish into a tank already occupied by another fish, with the two separated by a fiberglass mesh barrier and an opaque cover. The mesh prevented physical contact while allowing the fish to see and smell each other once the cover was removed.

After allowing five minutes for the fish to adjust to their shared environment, the team removed the opaque cover and recorded their interactions.

The following day, 24 hours after the initial observation, the researchers placed the same fish in water containing dissolved psilocybin. Following 20 minutes of exposure, they returned it to the tank with the same fish it had encountered previously. The opaque cover was then removed again so the researchers could observe any changes in behavior.

Magic Mushroom Compound Reduces Aggressive Attacks

The results revealed a striking change. Fish exposed to psilocybin moved around less and displayed fewer bursts of aggressive swimming than fish that had not received the substance.

To assess these changes, the scientists measured overall activity (time spent moving) and several signs of aggression (including swimming bursts). These rapid bursts are particularly important because they indicate that an encounter is escalating toward an attack, even when the fish cannot make physical contact.

"Swimming bursts are high-energy attack behaviors that represent an escalation of aggression towards the stimulus fish without making physical contact," explained Currie. "Other types of aggressive behaviors, like head-on displays, are more about communication and social assessment and require very little energy."

Interestingly, psilocybin did not suppress every form of aggressive or social behavior. While the fish became less likely to engage in intense, energy-demanding attacks, their less physically demanding social displays remained largely unchanged.

"Psilocybin's calming effect appears to selectively reduce energetically costly, escalated behaviors while lower-energy social display behaviors remained largely unchanged," said Forsyth. "This suggests that this compound can selectively dampen escalated social conflict rather than shutting down behavior altogether."

The compound also reduced general activity. When placed alongside another member of their species, fish treated with psilocybin spent less time swimming than untreated controls.

Together, these findings suggest that psilocybin may influence specific aspects of aggressive behavior rather than simply making animals less responsive to their surroundings.

What the Findings Could Mean for Psychedelic Research

The researchers believe animal studies like this one could eventually help scientists better understand how psychedelic compounds influence behavior and identify possible directions for therapeutic research.

By examining which social responses are most sensitive to psilocybin, researchers may be able to uncover biological mechanisms that are difficult to investigate directly in people. Such insights could ultimately inform studies of potential clinical applications.

However, the team emphasized that the findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a treatment for aggression in humans. The experiment involved fish, did not evaluate any clinical intervention, and cannot establish whether similar effects would occur in people.

There are also important questions about how long the calming effect lasts. The researchers examined only single doses and short exposure periods. They did not investigate repeated treatments, long-term behavioral changes, or whether the fish might adapt to the substance over time.

Additional experiments will be necessary to determine whether psilocybin's ability to reduce aggressive attacks can persist beyond the brief period examined in this study.

"Future studies can build on this work to explore how psilocybin alters neural signaling, which serotonin pathways are involved, and why some aspects of social behavior are affected while others are not," concluded Currie. "These are questions that are difficult or impossible to answer directly in humans."
