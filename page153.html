#620. Apple is embracing creators for its Pro and Tell event. And its not alone.

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

An invitation to an Apple event has long been something to brag about in the tech world. Now, for some influencers, it reportedly comes with business-class travel, a hotel stay, and time with the company's top executives.

On the Oct. 15 episode of Bloomberg's Power On podcast, Mark Gurman said he had confirmed that Apple offered influencers "paid hotel, paid business class travel" to attend its "Pro and Tell" product experience in Los Angeles, calling it a "creator-focused event." For the creators attending the upcoming Los Angeles showcase, Gurman described plans that include meetings with new Apple CEO John Ternus and other executives, plus dinner on Oct. 27, ahead of the company’s Oct. 28 special event.

This isn't the first time Apple has brought creators together for an exclusive experience. In March 2025, the company invited a group of student creators to Los Angeles for a one-day event that attendees nicknamed "Apple Campus" or "Apple University." For student and creator Heaven Marley, the invitation itself was a surprise. "I got the email and thought it was spam at first," she told People.

The company followed that with a similar creator gathering in Los Angeles this January. Creator Petr Mara shared an invitation to an "Apple Experience" running Jan. 27 to 29, coinciding with the launch of Apple Creator Studio, a subscription combining software for tasks such as editing video, making music, and working with images.

And, at the September iPhone event, livestreamer IShowSpeed performed a backflip while holding an iPhone Duo in front of Ternus. His video has more than 6 million views on YouTube as of Oct. 22. (Last year, MrBeast attended the event with former CEO Tim Cook.)

Other tech companies already offer a similarly warm welcome to creators.

Bloomberg's Chris Welch also noted on the podcast episode that Samsung and Google cover travel and hotels and host dinners too, calling it "standard fare." Gurman agreed that Apple wasn’t doing anything outside its competitors' practices. What stood out to him, though, was the change from the days when an Apple invitation was enough:

"There was a time...people were begging to go," Gurman said. "It's not the other way around, typically."

So what do companies get out of all this hospitality? Well, a chance to reach consumers through creators they already love. In a September analysis, Deloitte found that about 70 percent of U.S. consumers who engage with creators regularly return to the same ones; and roughly 60 percent said they had made a purchase primarily because of a creator's recommendation or sponsorship in the previous six months.

That appeal hasn’t gone unnoticed by the tech press. Discussing Apple's 2025 invitations on MacBreak Weekly, journalist Jason Snell argued that influencers can offer companies more control over their message, repeating promotional points "instead of questioning your points as a journalist might." And as tech reviewer Ben Sin wrote on X in March, "I completely understand why Apple is doing this," but described its "pivot to favoring giving access to lifestyle and overall influencers over techies" as "still sad news for me."

Each brand has its own entry way into the creator economy. Take Samsung — Its "Team Galaxy” roster includes creators and other public figures, with model and creator Haley Baylee and musician Audrey Nuna joining this year. And for February’s Galaxy S26 launch, Samsung says it brought approximately 140 influencers from 35 countries to San Francisco for three days of product demos, content creation, and a closing celebration.

As for AI companies, they face the unique challenge of trying to win over an often-skeptical public with plenty of questions about what AI means for jobs, creative work, and the environment. Influencers provide a layer of trust that they desperately need — which is probably why they are so sought-after right now.

"That’s the goal right now: build brand love," Anthropic's head of influencer, Lexie Barnhorn, told influencer marketing company Linqia. Claude recently hosted a supper club at Wild Cherry, the trendy restaurant inside New York's A24-owned Cherry Lane Theatre.

Microsoft Copilot is trying to do the same. Last February, they brought a group of New York fashion influencers to the Super Bowl on an influencer brand trip, where they asked the AI platform questions like, "hey copilot how do you cure a hangover" and "how to get a stain out".

And in February, OpenAI sent a signal on their seriousness about influencer marketing when they hired former Instagram partnerships executive Charles Porch as its first vice president of global creative partnerships. By summer, the company was hosting creators at Wildflower Farms, a luxury resort in New York’s Hudson Valley, for a retreat featuring private cabins, monogrammed pajamas, and picturesque outdoor dinners. The company also hosted a ChatGPT influencer dinner in tandem with Brett Chody, which was held at Little Beach House Malibu, part of Soho House.

"Creators are an important part of our community and how people get information and learn about our products today," an OpenAI spokesperson told Inc in August. "We value creators who choose to engage with us, attend our events, ask tough questions, and learn alongside everyone else."

As Mashable reported in August, OpenAI's nature-filled getaway to the Hudson Valley didn’t go over quite as planned. Beneath the lavish room tours and vacation posts, social media users questioned the setting and the creators' decision to attend.

"The concept of attending an AI brand trip surrounded by nature while it actively hurts the environment is an incredible bit," one X user wrote. On Reddit, a commenter asked whether one creator was "gazing at where the new data center is gonna go," while another asked, "Did they promise you fresh water when we run out?"

From a creator's perspective, though, there are many reasons to say yes to a brand event, trip, or collab, despite the criticism. Business creator Grace McCarrick defended the retreat as a chance to hear from the people shaping a technology likely to affect her work and her audience. "If I have the opportunity to be in the room and hear from people who are at the helm of change, I can do that," she said. "It's actually well within my moral system."

There's also a real financial incentive towards working with these brands: they have money to burn.

AI and career creator Megan Lieu, who attended the OpenAI retreat, told CNBC in February that her biggest sponsorship at that point was with Anthropic. She didn’t disclose its value, but said her sponsored deals generally ranged from $5,000 to $30,000.

Even so, some creators turn the offers down: Circus performer Jack Lepiarz, known as Jack the Whipper, told CNBC he had rejected a $20,000 deal promoting generative image tools over concerns about AI’s effect on people’s livelihoods.

That puts influencers in a complicated position. The relationship with their audience is part of what makes them valuable to a company; yet, accepting the partnership can put that same relationship under scrutiny.

But for tech brands, the decision is less complicated, with lots of potential benefits and few downsides. Influencers bring an audience with them; journalists bring questions they might not want to answer.

#616. Who was Eva Mamlok? Berlin square renamed after Jewish anti-fascist resistance fighter

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

She started taking action against Hitler as a teenager and never stopped until her death in a concentration camp. Berlin's Blücherplatz is now being renamed after her.

In the history of German resistance to Nazism, some names have become symbols: Sophie Scholl, Claus von Stauffenberg, Dietrich Bonhoeffer and Georg Elser. Yet many others have remained largely forgotten.

Among them is Eva Mamlok, a young Jewish woman from Berlin whose acts of defiance began in her teenage years and continued until her murder in a Nazi concentration camp.

Now more people will get to know her story as Berlin renames Blücherplatz, a square in the district of Kreuzberg, Eva-Mamlok-Platz on October 16.

"Eva Mamlok was a very remarkable person. As a young Jewish woman from Berlin, her spirit of resistance was particularly striking," said cultural researcher Alexandra Weltz-Rombach, who has worked extensively with the historians' network "Gruppe Eva Mamlok."

Mamlok was born on May 6, 1918, into a Jewish family in Berlin. They ran a wholesale wine business, and the family belonged to the vibrant Jewish middle class that formed an important part of Berlin's social and economic life during the Weimar Republic.

But Eva came of age just as Germany was descending into dictatorship. When Hitler became chancellor in 1933, she was only 14 years old. She was also a member of the Socialist Workers' Youth (SAJ). Following the suppression of the left-wing political youth organization later that year, she became active in the underground resistance.

According to testimony from Inge Berner (1922-2012), the sole survivor of Mamlok's later resistance group, Eva climbed onto the roof of a department store on Blücherplatz as a teenager and painted the slogan "Down with Hitler."

"She was caught doing that, though she wasn't arrested at that particular moment — that didn't happen until a year later," noted Weltz-Rombach.

Mamlok first appeared in official records in November 1934, after she was arrested for taking part in commemorations for socialist leaders Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht at Berlin's Friedrichsfelde Cemetery.

Authorities determined that the 16-year-old held "hostile views toward the state" and placed her in "protective custody until further notice" — a euphemism the regime used to justify imprisonment without trial. She was sent to the women's concentration camp at Moringen in Lower Saxony, where she remained until May 1935.

After her release, she continued her anti-Nazi activities despite the increasing danger, facing persecution both as a political dissident and as a Jew.

In these underground circles, she met Pieter Siemsen (1914–2004), and they remained a couple until he emigrated to Argentina in 1937. The only known surviving photograph of Eva comes from his personal archive.

She had a daughter, Tana, in 1939. The father's identity is unknown, however. The child was placed in a Jewish orphanage in Berlin and deported in 1942 to the Auschwitz-Birkenau extermination camp, where she was murdered.

Meanwhile, because she was Jewish, Mamlok was forced to perform compulsory labor at the Berlin firm F. Butzke Schrauben-Industrie in 1940. There she met other women who shared her opposition to Nazism.

Together they formed what later became known as the "Eva Mamlok Group," an informal resistance network of Jewish and non-Jewish women. They distributed anti-Nazi leaflets and wrote slogans critical of Hitler.

They also shared banned books. "They were books that are today part of Germany's educational canon," from Thomas Mann to Lion Feuchtwanger, said Weltz-Rombach. That the Berlin Central and Regional Library (ZLB), Germany's largest public library, stands on the new Eva-Mamlok-Platz is a fitting symbol of this act of resistance and the importance of books, she added.

Mamlok and others in her group were arrested for distributing this forbidden literature. On January 13, 1942, she was deported from Berlin to the Riga Ghetto in occupied Latvia.

According to Inge Berner's testimony, Mamlok remained active in the resistance there too. For example, she arranged for a contact still in Berlin to send her a cake with a miniature camera hidden inside it to document persecution in the ghetto.

In October 1944, she was sent to the Stutthof Nazi concentration camp, where she died in December of that year.

Blücherplatz was named after the Prussian field marshal Gebhard Leberecht von Blücher, whose most renowned achievement was leading his army against Napoleon at the Battle of Leipzig in 1813 and the Battle of Waterloo in 1815.

Several places, including streets and parks, have been named after him in Germany.

"There is no shortage of Blücher memorial sites," said Weltz-Rombach. "And perhaps it's also time to put an end to this old Franco-German enmity — which is, of course, inherent in the name Blücher, given its Prussian tradition — and to establish, in its place, a different form of remembrance."

The square also happens to be the site of the Carnival of Cultures, a multicultural street festival and parade held every year to celebrate Berlin's global diversity.

Having first heard about Mamlok when a Stolperstein commemorating her was laid in 2011, Weltz-Rombach has been researching her biography for the past few years. The Berlin-based filmmaker, producer and curator has also been sharing the resistance fighter's story through various projects, including an exhibition in 2024 and a podcast series with young Berliners.

Since Mamlok was only a teenager herself when she became politically active, the workshop participants can easily relate to her fate.

These teenagers learn about Mamlok's experiences at the actual locations where she was active, helping them imagine what it would be like to be arrested and imprisoned for six months without trial for doing things that normally wouldn't be considered crimes — such as simply expressing an opinion publicly or laying flowers at a commemorative event, explained Weltz-Rombach.

"Above all, she is, of course, a role model — an example of how one would like to act oneself, and how courageous one would like to be."

"[Women's] resistance was often of a more everyday nature, rather than planting bombs," said Weltz-Rombach. Their stories offer examples of "forms of resistance that have only recently received more attention." Other examples include a 2024 exhibition and the launch of a database dedicated to female resistance fighters.

Even though many of the eyewitnesses are now dead, Weltz-Rombach believes there are still many more of these stories that deserve to be brought into the spotlight: "There are definitely many names we do not know, that are waiting to be further researched."

Don't miss the Orionid meteor shower peak tonight (Oct. 21-22) as tiny shards of Halley's Comet collide with Earth's atmosphere to carve fiery streaks through the Autumn sky! But will the light cast by the waxing gibbous moon spoil the show?

The Orionid meteor shower activity peaks when Earth travels through the densest section of the debris trail left by Comet 1P/Halley. The comet is currently racing back towards the sun ahead of its next visit to the inner solar system in 2061.

Under perfect dark sky conditions 10 to 20 shooting stars could brighten the night sky each hour as the peak unfolds, according to NASA. However, the light cast by the waxing moon — just four days from being full — will likely reduce the number of visible meteors by hiding fainter Orionids from sight.

Orionid shooting stars will appear to travel away from a point of origin, close to the red star Betelgeuse in the constellation Orion, rising above the eastern horizon an hour before midnight for viewers in the U.S.

Your best chance at spotting the fast-moving meteors will come in the hours before dawn on Oct. 22, after the moon has set and the radiant approaches its highest point in the night sky. It's best not to stare directly at the radiant, where the glowing meteor trails will be relatively short compared to those left by shooting stars that appear 45-90 degrees away from the bright star Betelgeuse, thanks to an effect known as foreshortening.

Try to travel away from city lights and allow 20-30 minutes for your eyes to adapt to the dark. Always make sure someone knows where you're going if you head somewhere remote to catch the shower and be sure to take a red light to ensure that you can find your way while also preserving your night vision!

#613. Your aging brain may be trading memory for something more valuable

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Growing older is often associated with memory loss and declining mental abilities. However, scientists are questioning whether these changes tell the whole story. Rather than simply becoming less capable, the brain may be adapting to a new stage of life in which accumulated knowledge, wisdom, and understanding become more important than remembering every detail.

Researchers at the University of Arizona have proposed a different way of understanding cognitive aging. Drawing on findings from molecular biology and brain imaging, they suggest that the changes occurring in older brains may reflect a natural stage of human development rather than deterioration alone. Their work, published in Perspectives on Psychological Science, offers a new perspective on why thinking and memory change with age.

The researchers call their proposed explanation the adaptive aging hypothesis. It suggests that the brain gradually adjusts its priorities to make better use of a lifetime of accumulated experience. This could help explain why older adults often have valuable strengths in teaching, mentoring, storytelling, and passing knowledge to younger generations.

"If we look at normative aging as a story of just decline, we are really missing the boat," said study coauthor Fabian-Xosé Fernandez, an associate professor of cognition and neural systems at the University of Arizona. "The older brain is optimized for different things than the younger brain, predominantly the sharing of stored expertise and social relationships."

How the Brain Shifts From Memory to Meaning

Two brain regions play important roles in how people remember and interpret their experiences. The hippocampus helps preserve specific information about events, including their details and circumstances. The frontal cortex, meanwhile, is more involved in extracting broader meaning, recognizing patterns, and understanding the central message of an experience, sometimes called its "gist."

According to the researchers, the balance between these systems changes during healthy aging. As people grow older, the brain tends to place less emphasis on activity in the hippocampus and rely more on the frontal cortex. This shift may allow the brain to focus more on interpreting information through existing knowledge rather than recording every new experience in precise detail.

"The fact that you see this across people and animal models suggests there is an evolutionary driving force," Fernandez said. "One could argue you are developing your entire life, and development occurs in lockstep with the needs of your brain. When you're younger, the hippocampus turns on because you don't have a knowledge base or know how the world works. A kid absorbs information like a sponge because they are building an archive of information. In early life, the balance is tilted toward recollecting declarative memories with precision, detail, and context."

Young brains need to gather enormous amounts of new information because they are still building a foundation for understanding the world. By later adulthood, however, people have already developed extensive stores of memories, concepts, and experiences. Connecting new information to what they already know may become more useful than retaining every individual detail.

The researchers suggest that this changing balance reflects a shift in how the brain processes experience. Instead of concentrating primarily on collecting detailed memories, it increasingly emphasizes understanding relationships, identifying underlying meaning, and applying previously acquired knowledge.

Why Evolution May Favor Wisdom in Older Adults

Evidence of similar changes in animal models raises the possibility that this transition has an evolutionary explanation. Fernandez suggests that the aging brain may be adapting to help individuals preserve and share the knowledge they have acquired throughout their lives.

From this perspective, the ability to draw on decades of experience could be especially valuable for making judgments, guiding others, and transferring important information between generations. Older adults may therefore play a distinct role in supporting younger people through mentorship, teaching, and storytelling.

"Given those goals, it makes sense that you aren't interested in nitty-gritty details," Fernandez said. "You are interested in safeguarding the vast knowledge you already have. Evolution may be saying that if you make it long enough, there's a need to transmit this information, so we put into motion biological changes that enforce and protect your semantic and autobiographical memories."

Under this hypothesis, changes in memory and thinking are not necessarily signs that the brain is functioning less effectively. They may instead reflect a different set of priorities, with greater emphasis on preserving meaningful knowledge and using it to understand new situations.

Why Healthy Brain Aging Is Different From Alzheimer's

The adaptive aging hypothesis could also provide scientists with another way to examine the differences between normal cognitive aging and Alzheimer's disease.

Although both involve changes in memory, Fernandez emphasizes that Alzheimer's should not be understood as ordinary aging taken to an extreme. The biological processes involved are fundamentally different.

"When I was in grad school, people thought about Alzheimer's as a more intense version of cognitive aging, but that is just not the case," Fernandez said. "They are completely different."

During typical aging, the brain's gradual shift away from relying heavily on the hippocampus and toward greater involvement of the frontal cortex generally unfolds between the fifth and seventh decades of life. In Alzheimer's disease, however, abnormal processes involving proteins such as amyloid and tau may interfere with this transition.

One important distinction involves activity in the hippocampus. While healthy aging is generally associated with reduced emphasis on this region, Alzheimer's can involve unusually high levels of hippocampal activity. Rather than supporting the normal progression of cognitive development, disease-related changes may disrupt the brain networks responsible for memory and understanding.

"In normative aging, hippocampal activation is deemphasized, which is probably what should be happening in a 70-year-old. But in many cases of Alzheimer's, it turns out the hippocampus is hyperactive," he said. "This beautiful developmental transition is completely disrupted in Alzheimer's disease by pathological processes that mess with episodic memory and interfere with circuits that allow you to think about your own life, lexicon, and vocabulary."

These disruptions can affect the ability to recall personal experiences and access stored knowledge, including words and their meanings. Understanding how these changes differ from the adaptations associated with healthy aging may help researchers develop a clearer picture of normal brain development and neurodegenerative disease.

Embracing the Hidden Strengths of the Aging Brain

The researchers argue that society may need to reconsider what successful brain aging looks like. Encouraging older adults to process information exactly as younger people do may not always be the most productive approach.

Fernandez suggests that placing excessive emphasis on remembering precise details could work against the brain's changing priorities. "For many individuals, the weight of how you process information should probably not be so hippocampal-centric by the sixth or seventh decade."

Instead of judging older adults primarily by abilities that tend to weaken with age, the researchers encourage greater recognition of the capabilities that may become especially valuable later in life.

Creating more opportunities for social interaction could be one way to support these strengths. Relationships between older and younger generations may be particularly beneficial because they provide opportunities to exchange experiences, share expertise, and preserve knowledge that might otherwise be lost.

"People see older individuals and look at them as not being on par with younger individuals across a lot of metrics," Fernandez says. "The way I look at it is that you've got someone with a brain that has been active for several decades and has seen a lot. There is tremendous value there, and we don't do a good job of unlocking it."

The central message of the adaptive aging hypothesis is that growing older may involve more than a gradual reduction in cognitive abilities. Although some mental skills become less reliable with age, the brain may also be adapting to make better use of the knowledge and experience accumulated over a lifetime. Recognizing those strengths could change how society understands aging and the contributions older adults can make.

NASA has revealed new details about its plans to return astronauts to the surface of the moon, outlining a series of upcoming milestones for SpaceX and Blue Origin as both companies race to ready their lunar landers for the agency's Artemis program.

During an Oct. 5 presentation at the Astronautical Congress (IAC) in Antalya, Türkiye, Steve Creech, acting manager of NASA's Human Landing System (HLS) Program, laid out development timelines for both spacecraft, with key demonstrations planned through early 2028 that include orbital refueling and uncrewed lunar landing tests to qualify the vehicles to fly astronauts to the surface of the moon.

A more pressing deadline, however, is NASA's Artemis III mission, which is targeted for the third quarter (Q3) of 2027. Artemis III will launch four astronauts aboard NASA's Orion spacecraft to low Earth orbit (LEO), where they will rendezvous and dock with test versions of both companies' landers for interoperability checkouts. Those demonstrations will help inform NASA's determination about which lander the agency will choose to carry astronauts to the moon's surface on Artemis IV, targeted for early 2028.

SpaceX and Blue Origin were both awarded NASA contracts to develop HLS vehicles — Starship and Blue Moon, respectively — and have spent the past several years refining their respective lunar lander designs and dealing with various delays along the way. In the face of those delays, and in an effort to better structure its mission progression for success, NASA overhauled its Artemis program architecture in February to maintain its goal of landing astronauts on the moon by the end of 2028 and establish a more regular cadence of uncrewed landings in the meantime.

As part of that shakeup, Artemis III, previously the program's first crewed lunar landing mission, was moved up a year and repurposed for the lunar lander vehicle tests the agency is preparing to fly to LEO next year. The program's first moon landing remains scheduled for 2028, but was shifted to the manifest for Artemis IV.

Between now and then, both SpaceX and Blue Origin have more than a dozen test demonstrations and vehicle milestones to achieve, including success during Artemis III and flights afterward. This will all lead up to a key decision NASA must make: Which vehicle will carry out the first crewed landing on the surface of the moon since the Apollo program ended in 1972?

Starship reached for the first time on its most recent test flight, which occurred on Sept. 28 from SpaceX's Starbase site in South Texas. NASA lists several other important goals for the vehicle to achieve in 2026, including (in the order listed during the IAC presentation) the first return and catch of the rocket's upgraded Version 3 (V3) booster at its launch pad; the first-ever return and catch of a Ship upper stage; the first Starship launch from one of SpaceX's launch pads still under construction on Florida's Space Coast; and, possibly, the first V3 booster reflight.

NASA also expects SpaceX to complete the initial build of the crew cabin for its lunar lander Starship variant. SpaceX released renders of Starship's interior last year, as well as images of the test elevator astronauts are using to train for Starship egress on the lunar surface, but little else has been made public about the progress the company has made on the cabin's design.

Should the cabin's initial build wrap up by the end of the year, as NASA's presentation suggested, SpaceX will use Q1 2027 to complete integration of the systems that Starship will need to communicate with Orion during Artemis III, and deliver a cabin simulator for astronauts to train on leading up to the mission. SpaceX plans to continue Starship test flights through Q2 of next year, complete a landing leg demonstration and begin crew cabin airlock tests.

For Artemis III, SpaceX plans to fly one of its existing V3 Starship upper stages, with some slight modifications. Starship and Orion will dock as part of Artemis III but the SpaceX vehicle won't be equipped with a crew cabin for the mission's astronauts to enter. Between the end of this year and the beginning of the next, SpaceX is expected to complete the addition of a docking system and nosecone cover to the Artemis III Starship vehicle, which, NASA documents suggest, has already completed its mechanical fit checks.

Other notches on SpaceX's checklist leading up to Artemis III include the first propellant transfer demonstration with Starship in orbit. As the first-ever launch vehicle and spacecraft designed for full reusability, Starship's fuel constraints require that its tanks be topped off in order to fly beyond LEO. For a moon landing mission, this could mean up to a dozen launches of SpaceX's Starship "tanker" spacecraft to an orbiting fuel depot, which the moon-bound Starship will visit. To pull that off, though, SpaceX has to show it can successfully transfer those cryogenic fuels on orbit, and also store them effectively enough to prevent too much boil-off.

Starship won't require any refueling flights to complete its objectives for Artemis III, but SpaceX is expected to launch its first Starship tanker vehicle within a few months of that mission as it gears up for the Artemis IV moon landing mission. To that end, a launch of an uncrewed Starship lunar lander variant is targeted sometime toward the end of Q3/beginning of Q4, with an uncrewed lunar landing mission carried out before the end of 2027.

Blue Origin, meanwhile, has its own set of milestones to meet as it develops its Blue Moon Mark 2 (MK2) lander — a 52-foot-tall (16 meters) spacecraft powered by three BE-7 engines. Unlike the bare-bones V3 Starship that SpaceX will launch on Artemis III, Blue Origin plans to fly a completed test vehicle, incorporating the crew cabin, partial life-support, flight software and other systems needed to allow astronauts to enter the lander from Orion during their time docked in LEO. Astronauts will be able to enter Blue Moon from Orion to get a feel for the lander in zero-g and test some of its systems.

NASA's timeline shows the company working to complete a series of manufacturing and testing milestones in the last few months of 2026, including continued BE-7 testing and qualification of Blue Moon's BT-7 maneuvering thrusters. The company also faces its Artemis III interim design review and a critical design review for an upcoming landing demonstration mission in 2028.

Blue Origin is also working to restore its pad at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station's Launch Complex-36 (LC-36) to operation and return its New Glenn rocket to flight, following an explosion of the rocket in May that caused extensive damage to the facility. NASA's presentation places that milestone in Q4 2026, a timeline the company has also been very vocal about maintaining. A recent Reuters report, however, suggests that may not happen until early 2027.

By early 2027, the company expects to complete the spacecraft's communications systems and finalize its flight software, with assembly, integration and testing continuing into Q2. Q1 2027 is also when Blue Origin's Blue Moon MK1 lander (a smaller, uncrewed version of its MK2 successor) will launch to the lunar surface as a part of NASA's Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) program of robotic moon missions.

Throughout next year, the company plans to continue construction and refurbishments at its launch facilities and hopes to have a new launch pad ready to support the upgraded, more powerful New Glenn 9x4 rocket l by the end of Q3. The first flight of that rocket variant is scheduled for Q4 2027, alongside a second MK1 lunar mission and the launch of an uncrewed MK2 demonstration lander.

Like SpaceX, Blue Origin will need to demonstrate that its lander can safely reach the moon's surface,operate there and return to lunar orbit before NASA clears it to carry astronauts on a future Artemis mission.

NASA's overall timeline remains the same as it has been since the February revamp, with Artemis III expected to launch in mid-2027, followed by a crewed lunar landing on Artemis IV in 2028, and possibly another on Artemis V that same year. Which company ultimately gets the green light for that first landing mission will depend on the progress each makes between now and then, and whether their landers can demonstrate the readiness that NASA requires to safely carry astronauts to the surface of the moon and back again.

The Moon is getting closer to its full phase, with more of its surface visible as the days go by. Here's what you can look for when you look up at the sky tonight.

Tonight, without the use of visual aids you should be able to spot the Copernicus Crater, the Mare Serenitatis, and Mare Fecunditatis. If you have binoculars you'll also see the Endymion Crater, the Alphonsus Crater, and the Clavius Crater. Plus, if you have a telescope, you'll also see the Linne Crater, Rima Ariadaeus, and the Apollo 17 landing spot.

The next Full Moon will take place on October 25 or 26, depending on your timezone.

The Moon takes around 29.5 days to travel around Earth, and its appearance changes throughout the month. As it moves around our planet, we see different amounts of its sunlit side, from a slim crescent to the quarter phases and eventually a Full Moon. It then begins the cycle again. As NASA explains, these phases are caused by the Moon's changing position relative to Earth and the Sun.

New Moon - The Moon is between Earth and the sun, so the side we see is dark (in other words, it's invisible to the eye).

Waxing Crescent - A small sliver of light appears on the right side (Northern Hemisphere).

First Quarter - Half of the Moon is lit on the right side. It looks like a half-Moon.

Waxing Gibbous - More than half is lit up, but it's not quite full yet.

Full Moon - The whole face of the Moon is illuminated and fully visible.

Waning Gibbous - The Moon starts losing light on the right side. (Northern Hemisphere)

Third Quarter (or Last Quarter) - Another half-Moon, but now the left side is lit.

Waning Crescent - A thin sliver of light remains on the left side before going dark again.

#601. Scientists discover a surprising link between vitamin C and cancer survival

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Vitamin C plays an important role in keeping the immune system healthy, helping wounds heal, and protecting cells from damage. However, people with precancerous blood disorders and blood cancers often have persistently low vitamin C levels. Researchers have been investigating whether correcting this deficiency could improve their health and treatment outcomes.

New findings from the phase 2 EVITA clinical trial suggest that vitamin C supplementation may offer important benefits for people with precancerous blood conditions and early-stage blood cancers. The randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study was conducted by the Van Andel Institute-Stand Up To Cancer® Epigenetics Dream Team.

The results, published in the American Cancer Society journal CANCER, revealed that patients who received 1,000 mg of oral vitamin C every day for one year experienced fewer adverse events during the study and its follow-up period. The supplement also changed the levels of certain immune signaling molecules, known as cytokines, in ways associated with more favorable outcomes. These changes suggest that vitamin C could influence inflammation-related processes involved in the growth and progression of abnormal blood cells.

Although vitamin C did not significantly affect the rate at which abnormal blood cells expanded, the researchers identified an encouraging result. An exploratory analysis showed that participants who received vitamin C were significantly more likely to be alive after approximately three years of follow-up than those who took a placebo. Scientists emphasize that this potential survival benefit must be verified in a larger phase 3 clinical trial.

Vitamin C Shows Promise for Patients at Risk of Leukemia

"We are tremendously excited and cautiously optimistic that our results could one day translate into a simple way to improve care and outcomes for people with pre-leukemia disorders," said Peter A. Jones, Ph.D., D.Sc. (hon), a distinguished professor at Van Andel Institute. Jones is also a co-corresponding author of the research and co-leader of the VAI-SU2C Epigenetics Dream Team.

"Our approach reflects a growing emphasis on cancer interception -- intervening before cancer becomes established. We are working to build on the trial findings by exploring how exactly vitamin C interacts with pre-cancerous cells, including the idea that it may help restore normal epigenetic function in blood cells at risk of becoming leukemia."

The international research effort was led by Kirsten Grønbæk, M.D., Ph.D., a leading hematologist, professor at the University of Copenhagen, and member of the VAI-SU2C Epigenetics Dream Team.

"These exciting preliminary findings lay the groundwork for a larger clinical trial to explore vitamin C as a possible way to improve patient outcomes," said Grønbæk. "It is too early to make definitive recommendations based on our results, but we are hopeful that a more expansive study will provide further insights."

First Clinical Trial Tests Daily Vitamin C for Precancerous Blood Disorders

The study enrolled 109 patients with either clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance (CCUS) or myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS). Both conditions can develop into acute myeloid leukemia (AML), an aggressive form of blood cancer that is often difficult to treat. The likelihood of developing CCUS and related blood disorders rises with age, making these conditions particularly common among older adults.

Currently, no approved treatment can prevent CCUS from progressing to leukemia. Although stem cell transplantation is an option for treating MDS, many patients cannot undergo the procedure because of age or other medical conditions. None of the people enrolled in the trial were receiving cancer treatment when the study took place.

To determine whether vitamin C might offer benefits, researchers randomly divided participants into two groups. One received 1,000 mg of vitamin C by mouth each day for 12 months. The other received a placebo designed to be indistinguishable from the supplement in appearance, taste, and smell, but containing no vitamin C.

Neither the participants nor the clinical staff knew who was receiving vitamin C until the study concluded. This double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled approach is considered the gold standard in clinical research because it reduces potential bias and allows investigators to assess treatment effects more objectively.

The research was carried out at four hospitals: Copenhagen University Hospital-Rigshospitalet, Copenhagen University Hospital-Herlev, Aalborg University Hospital, and Keck Hospital of the University of Southern California.

What makes EVITA particularly notable is that it was the first randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial to investigate oral vitamin C in patients with conditions that can precede leukemia or with lower-risk myeloid cancers. Earlier studies had primarily examined vitamin C administered intravenously and had focused on solid tumors rather than precancerous blood disorders or early-stage blood malignancies.

EVITA instead evaluated a daily oral dose comparable to that available in many over-the-counter vitamin C supplements.

More Than Half of Patients Had Low Vitamin C Levels

Blood samples collected throughout the study allowed researchers to track participants' vitamin C concentrations and determine whether supplementation corrected deficiencies.

At the beginning of the trial, more than half of the participants had insufficient vitamin C levels. Among those assigned to the supplementation group, blood concentrations returned to normal after they began taking vitamin C.

For patients living with conditions that could eventually develop into leukemia, the possibility of an accessible intervention is especially important. In many cases, physicians have little choice but to monitor the disease and watch for signs that it is progressing.

"The standard approach for patients with pre-cancer blood disorders is to monitor their blood cell counts over time. This is, understandably, very frustrating -- no one wants to hear that they have a condition that increases leukemia risk but there's nothing that can be done other than watch and wait," said Casey O'Connell, M.D., FACP, the Lawrence and Jane Kelly Chair in Hematology at the University of Southern California and lead clinician at USC's trial location.

"The EVITA trial took on this challenge by exploring vitamin C's potential to address the underlying factors that drive leukemia development."

How Vitamin C Could Influence Cancer Development

Scientists have investigated whether vitamin C could play a useful role in cancer treatment for more than seven decades. For much of that time, the evidence remained uncertain because studies produced inconsistent findings and employed different experimental methods.

During the past decade, however, improved research technologies have revealed that vitamin C has important functions beyond its familiar roles in nutrition and immunity. In particular, scientists have uncovered its involvement in controlling gene activity and guiding normal cell development.

One of the most important discoveries concerns TET2, an enzyme that helps prevent cancerous growth by controlling whether particular genes are switched "on" or "off." Vitamin C supports the activity of this enzyme, which helps maintain normal patterns of gene regulation.

Mutations affecting TET2 are frequently found in leukemia and related blood disorders. When the enzyme's normal function is disrupted, abnormal cells can gain an opportunity to multiply and potentially develop into cancer.

Experiments using cancer models have provided additional evidence of vitamin C's possible protective effects. These studies found that supplementation could interfere with cellular changes associated with cancer and slow the progression of leukemia. Notably, the strongest effects appeared when vitamin C was introduced before cancer had become established.

This research helped inspire the idea of using vitamin C as a form of early intervention, potentially targeting the biological changes that precede the development of leukemia.

Before launching EVITA, the VAI-SU2C Epigenetics Dream Team conducted a pilot clinical study in 2016 to determine whether taking vitamin C orally could meaningfully raise its concentration in the blood.

The initial findings suggested that oral supplementation was a practical approach. Encouraged by those results, the researchers launched the phase 2 EVITA trial in 2017.

"This early work was critical because it offered a foundation from which to develop the present trial," said Stine Ulrik Mikkelsen, M.D., Ph.D., the study's first author and a clinician researcher in Grønbæk's laboratory.

"Vitamin C is important because it is involved in a range of biological functions, including the activity of enzymes that regulate normal cell function."

Researchers are continuing to examine the EVITA data to better understand the biological processes that might explain why patients receiving vitamin C showed improved survival.

Although the findings offer a promising direction for future research, additional clinical studies will be essential to determine whether vitamin C supplementation can reliably improve outcomes for people with precancerous blood disorders and early-stage blood cancers.
