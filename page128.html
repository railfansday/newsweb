Bournemouth 3-0 AC Milan: Justin Kluivert, Marcus Tavernier and Amine Adil were the scorers, with Mike Maignan sent off for the visitors

Bournemouth scaled giddy new heights in their debut Europa League campaign as 10-man AC Milan were convincingly brushed aside 3-0 at the Vitality Stadium.

The Italian giants, champions of Serie A only four years ago, were wretched, unable to match Marco Rose’s team who pressed them ruthlessly and attacked breathlessly in torrential conditions on the south coast.

The first two goals were penalties scored in the first half, by Justin Kluivert then by Marcus Tavernier, before Amine Adli added a magnificent late free-kick, but the home side ought to have had even more to show for their dominance.

Mike Maignan, the Milan goalkeeper and captain, let his team and his manager Ruben Amorim down badly and inexplicably, swiping at the face of James Hill at a corner, conceding a spot-kick and leaving his side to play for more than an hour with 10 players.

Bournemouth have looked perfectly at home on their maiden European adventure but, even so, this fixture had an almost surreal sound to it, the seven-time champions of Europe taking a hammering on a pitch that was hosting League Two football as recently as 2010.

“How did we end up here?” read a huge tifo unfurled in the North Stand before kick-off, but Bournemouth’s display suggested this might be just the beginning for them in Europe.

Bournemouth dominated from the off. In the third minute, Evanilson crossed, Kluivert missed it and Ben Gannon-Doak had his shot deflected behind.

Evanilson came closer still a minute later, glancing a header a foot wide of the post, but a VAR review confirmed the ball had touched the raised hand of Koni De Winter.

It was the kind of handball decision unlikely to be given in the Premier League, but Bournemouth are learning the ropes fast in Europe. From the penalty spot, Kluivert rolled into the bottom corner.

Ryan Christie’s drive from the edge of the box was held at the second attempt in the slippery conditions by Maignan, who also had an easier save than he might have done from an Evanilson shot.

The France international had far more trouble with James Hill’s low effort which skidded off the turf and had to be scrambled around the post.

From the corner that followed, Maignan lost his cool badly, raising two hands firmly to the face of Tyler Adams, an absurd abdication of responsibility from Milan’s captain.

VAR took its time in checking the on-field decision of red card but there was little chance it would be overturned. Substitute goalkeeper Lorenzo Torriani’s first action was to pick Tavernier’s penalty out of his net.

From then on, Bournemouth cruised, though in truth even 11 against 11 had been a canter. Substitute Rayan rose well to meet a cross and ought to have kept his header down.

Adli’s free-kick gave the scoreline an incredible but thoroughly deserved gloss, though Milan were lucky to escape with just three goals against them, Rayan denied a brilliant fourth goal for the Cherries late on by Torriani.

Mitochondria are bound by a double membrane and are semiautonomous organelles, rendering them highly vulnerable to global and internal proteostatic stress1. Multiple surveillance mechanisms at proteostatic and inter-organellar levels have evolved to maintain mitochondrial integrity and cellular health2,3,4,5,6,7. However, the precise mechanisms by which proteostatic damage relays stress signals across organelles and how the quality surveillance machinery coordinates these functions remain unknown. Here we report that under proteostatic stress, damaged mitochondrial proteins are sequestered to form stress-induced mitochondrial protein aggregates (SIMPAs) that activate specific quality control mechanisms in a spatially confined manner without perturbing the mitochondrial network. Quantitative proteomic and correlative light–electron microscopy analysis revealed that SIMPA mediates inter-organellar communication via mitochondria–endoplasmic reticulum contact sites or mitochondrial-associated membranes. Mechanistically, GRP75 coordinates SIMPA biogenesis and focally activates endoplasmic reticulum unfolded protein response pathways via mitochondria–endoplasmic reticulum contact sites or mitochondrial-associated membranes for the targeted clearance of SIMPAs. Functionally, in both cellular and liver disease models, we found that a GRP75-dependent SIMPA signalling cascade attenuates mitochondrial stress and preserves mitochondrial integrity under proteotoxic stress. Overall, we show that SIMPAs, functioning as protein condensates, serve as a central cross-compartment communication hub for mitochondrial quality control, supporting cellular and organismal fitness.

Roberto De Zerbi discussed his three-at-the-back formation as if it was likely to be a one-off following Tottenham's win over Coventry on Monday night.

The tactical tweak paved the way to their first win of the season but, according to the Spurs boss, had been down to the absence of two central midfielders, Rodrigo Bentancur and Lucas Bergvall, and the direct nature of the threat posed by counter-attacking opponents.

De Zerbi had been a rock-solid devotee of the back four since his appointment in March and his tinkering hinted at the desperation for that first win and an ongoing struggle to find his best team.

His focus at the start of the season fell upon the forwards and rightly so. Spurs did not score in the first four league games. Then they scored twice against Aston Villa but not before conceding three.

Balance has been elusive. Spurs focus on solving one part of the machine only to find a gasket blows somewhere else.

And now they face all the attacking power of Chelsea at a venue where Spurs have only won once in 41 attempts, an awful run stretching over more than 36 years and questions loom for De Zerbi at the back.

Spurs boss Roberto De Zerbi switched to a back three at Coventry City on Monday night and got his first win of the season

Jan Paul van Hecke was criticised for another mistake in possession leading to the Coventry goal on Monday.

Van Hecke has made mistakes on the ball because he takes risks, because De Zerbi wants him to break lines either by running the ball out from deep or punching early passes forward. That’s the reason he made him a priority signing when he lost Cristian Romero and he has started the Dutchman in every league game.

Van Hecke is an excellent defender. Strong and aggressive, he has an old-school quality without the ball and wants to play out from the back like a modern centre-half.

Whether his partnership with Micky van de Ven has the right chemistry though is another matter. Van Hecke looked happier alongside the calming influence of Marcos Senesi at Manchester United when Van de Ven started on the bench after a heavy workload for the Netherlands.

In the modern game, centre backs can expect lots of the ball and Senesi is more composed than Van de Ven, more comfortable in possession, especially in tight areas, and has a better passing range.

The Argentine does not have the same pace but suits the way De Zerbi expects his central defenders to dictate play. He seemed to have a soothing influence on Van Hecke too. There were echoes of the understanding he forged at Brighton playing alongside Lewis Dunk.

The performance at Old Trafford was arguably the best of an underwhelming season for Spurs, but how can De Zerbi leave Van de Ven out? Not only is his recovery pace vital if he wants to push on with a high line but he is the captain and has just signed a new long-term contract.

Senesi’s reward for his performance against United was to be left out against Coventry when Tosin Adarabioyo started his first game for Spurs in the Premier League.

Elements of the system worked well for De Zerbi. Van Hecke’s physical strength, energy and willing attitude enabled him to glide across into the right back role when the back line flexed into a four and take the defensive responsibility from wing-back Mohamed Kudus.

Archie Gray was alert to help as well from the right of a midfield trio but there were times when this new system was fraught and confused, not least the goal scored by Jack Rudoni, when Van Hecke lost the ball on a high-risk dribble out of defence and Spurs were punished in the right back area.

Perhaps the system is worth another go with Tosin and Senesi both capable of playing the central role. Van de Ven’s pace and mobility make him a natural fit on the left side of a back three.

It would mirror Chelsea, who will play a similar way under Xabi Alonso, with a back three plus wing backs including the attacking Pedro Neto who would line up against Kudus, a similarly offensive option.

De Zerbi is still without Bentancur (banned) and Bergvall (injured) although the focus of his concerns will be about what happens in other areas. Can his midfield compete with Moises Caicedo? Can he keep the creative forces of Cole Palmer and Morgan Rogers at bay?

#1112. Jürgen Klopp faces myriad of issues after rough start with Germany

Jürgen Klopp has done things a little differently since becoming Germany coach. Casting a wider net for talent and trying to connect with players and country haven't made an immediate impact, but he is not bowed.

Four games, three goals, two draws, one loss and one win. Jürgen Klopp would have hoped for better results from his first international break as Germany coach. But though the comfortable win over Serbia (2-0), draws against the Netherlands (1-1) and Greece (0-0) and a shock loss to Greece (0-1) in Germany leave them third out of four in their Nations League group, Klopp saw mostly positives.

"Everyone showed what they're made of; everyone earned their place. We've shown signs that we can do some things better than people gave us credit for. I wanted to get something going, and I definitely saw that happen," he said after Sunday's 0-0 draw in Thessaloniki.

For all the positivity of the former Liverpool and Borussia Dortmund coach, the four matches have, in reality, been a mixed bag for Germany.

Having fallen in the last 32 of the 2026 World Cup and exited in the group stage in 2022, Germany's footballing stock has never been lower.

Klopp tried to address this by casting his net wider, announcing a 44-man squad that split over the matches. He then said this would be a one off, but that his strategy of trying to find a diamond in the relative rough who could adapt to the international game had given him valuable insights.

"Hard work, lots of information — these guys are brimming with talent," he said of those given a surprise opportunity.

"People were saying that German soccer was at rock bottom and that there was no talent. ... They're not lacking in talent."

While Klopp may have seen potential, and has credit in the bank from his past work, he will need to turn that into results before too long.

While much has been said about Klopp's success in imprinting his trademark Gegenpressing (counter-press) style early on, he is also a coach who places a high value on connection. Throughout the international break, he has worn a cap emblazoned with the slogan "Einer von 83 millionen” (One of 83 million) in a reference to the German population and an attempt to unify in a country divided by the rise of the far-right AfD party.

He addressed the issue in his opening press conference and then reiterated his position after some social media users branded the team "not white enough" after their loss to Greece.

"There is no reason to give these people any thought. They aren't getting any of my attention. I have little time for stupidity in real life and none on social media either," Klopp said in answer to a question posed by DW.

As well as speaking honestly about his country, Klopp has been open with his new players.

"He has a very good rapport with every player. He tries to reach out to every player and make them feel good, that they're important and that they deserve to be here," said Florian Wirtz, who went on to label his coach as a "master" at managing relationships.

With limited time together, forming bonds between player, coach and country can elevate a national team. It may prove to be more important than any tactical plans.

One of the key men in the more senior squad that started the opener against the Netherlands, Kai Havertz limped off after 29 minutes. He was replaced by Eintracht Frankfurt's Younes Ebnoutalib while Nick Woltemade (on loan at Juventus from Newcastle) and Jonathan Burkhardt, also Frankfurt, were also tested up front over the four games.

None of the replacements scored, with Woltemade guilty of a particularly bad miss against Serbia and Burkhardt having a goal disallowed against Greece on Sunday. While all have potential and have enjoyed strong patches of form in their careers, none is tried and tested at the top level, and none is playing in the Champions League. With Havertz being somewhat injury prone, Klopp needs someone to emerge as a viable backup.

Injury is also, sadly, beginning to be a major feature of Jamal Musiala's career. He picked up a thigh injury in the warmup to the Netherlands game that may keep him out for a month. He's also missed games this season due to a neurological condition and missed the first half of last season with a broken leg, which came soon after another lengthy spell out. At his best, he is probably Germany's brightest attacking spark, but it's been a while since that level has been seen.

For Wirtz, the issue is slightly different. He was excellent in the win over a limited Serbia team, scoring one and creating the other. It was a reminder of a talent that has been largely dormant at Liverpool where he's shown his creative best only in glimpses. He followed it up with a decent display against Greece but saw Burkahrdt inadvertently block his goalbound header. There were signs Klopp's system may suit him.

"He's really gotten us on board with his philosophy," said Wirtz of his coach. "I enjoy it myself, too. I get to show my strengths when we have possession."

Klopp will need more of the same from Wirtz, who captained the side in Greece, if Germany are to move forward.

#1102. Euro plummets against US dollar: Has the next financial crisis begun?

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

The euro has fallen to a 17-month low against the dollar. That will make inflation worse in the EU, where political worries and concern over French debt are already troubling investors. Is a fiscal crisis at hand?

The euro hit a 17-month low against the dollar in early trading on Monday, as concerns about eurozone debt, particularly in France, compounded existing investor concerns over global government bond yields and rising oil prices.

The euro has been sliding against the dollar for much of 2026, down about 5% since the start of the year. It reached $1.12 (€0.99) on Monday, the lowest level since early 2025.

Ricardo Amaro, lead eurozone economist at Oxford Economics, told DW that the general slide can be attributed to a repricing of investor expectations regarding US Federal Reserve policy in terms of higher interest rates in the face of rising global bond yields.

However, he said the latest sell-off was prompted in particular by concerns over France, where "investors positioned for higher fiscal risk."

The sell-off in French debt gathered pace last week as doubts grow over its long-term viability. French 10-year government bonds — the cost France must pay to borrow money within 10 years of the debt being issued — rose to a 5% yield before easing slightly.

France has long had underlying fiscal problems. Since President Emmanuel Macron came to office in May 2017, public spending has climbed while he has brought in deep tax cuts. As a result, the country's national debt has increased by well over €1 trillion ($1.12 trillion).

France's debt-to-GDP ratio now stands at almost 118%. It consistently posts unbalanced budgets. Its annual budget deficit is now regularly more than 5%, above the 3.4% rate when Macron came to office.

For many investors, wider concerns over low eurozone growth levels and surging energy prices have contributed to a renewed focus on France, with many retreating to perceived safer options, such as German government debt.

The difference between French and German government 10-year debt yields reached its highest level since the eurozone debt crisis last week, a closely watched measure of EU financial stability.

The resulting pressure on the euro and on other government bond markets, including Italy's, means there are calls for the European Central Bank (ECB) to take action to prevent concern over France turning into panic.

Deutsche Bank's Jim Reid said in a note on Monday that at one point last week, the spread between German and French bonds had become so wide that a "mini-panic" was at hand.

"The big question is whether this is the start of a new euro sovereign crisis or whether markets have already overshot," he said.

Ricardo Amaro said the situation presents a challenge for the ECB because it needs to act without making things worse. "Sounding too hawkish would also add to pressure on France's bond yields, which became an important driver of euro weakness," he added.

He expects policymakers to continue monitoring currency developments between the US dollar and the euro, but to stop short of trying to influence the market for now.

Uncertainty abounds due to both political and economic pressures. France has been beset by political crises in recent years, where passing annual budgets has become a major test of government stability.

Although the 2027 budget has been agreed, with reforms aimed at reducing the deficit, the right-wing National Rally remains well-placed ahead of the 2027 presidential election, in which Marine Le Pen is likely to be the leading candidate.

Concerns over French economic policy in the event of Le Pen winning the presidency are spooking investors, similar to how the rise of the far-right AfD in Germany has raised questions over the future direction of the bloc's key economy.

Much of the political instability has been driven by voter anger over the high cost of living. In Spain, Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez has just called a snap election after measures to deal with the country's housing crisis were voted down in parliament.

Amaro says a weakening euro could compound the underlying problem of inflation.

"Sharper euro weakness would reinforce the inflationary shock at a time when inflation is already expected to stay high into 2027," he said.

A weaker euro would increase the cost of imported goods, particularly those that are priced in US dollars. Global commodities such as oil and gas are priced in dollars, meaning soaring energy prices would likely rise further, as would US imports.

With memories of the sovereign debt crisis that afflicted the EU in the early 2010s still fresh, many observers are beginning to wonder if the moves on French debt could be indicative of a new crisis in the eurozone.

Some, such as Geoffrey Yu, a senior strategist with BNY, believe such concerns are misplaced. "Comparisons to 2012 are well off the mark," he said.

However, Amaro says the fact that further interest rate hikes are expected from the ECB this year, combined with the worsening inflation outlook, makes this a situation which needs to be managed and monitored very carefully.

"Euro weakness shouldn't be interpreted as an isolated development," he says. "If triggered by growing concerns about France's fiscal outlook, then the ECB would certainly want to thread that carefully given risks for the eurozone."
