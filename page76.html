Chalobah, who is a senior England international, joined the Italian outfit from Chelsea in the summer.

He is now a high-profile figure but was a relatively inexperienced operator when he was loaned to Ipswich Town, Huddersfield and Lorient in his youth.

The defender spent the 2019/20 season in Yorkshire, making 38 appearances for the Terriers as they successfully avoided relegation from the Championship.

He told The Athletic: “Going on loans three years in a row helped. It built my experience as a player on and off the pitch: how to live, how to carry yourself and do the extras, like investing in yourself.

“It didn’t come naturally. I had to work, whether that meant using analysis, a chef or a personal trainer. I just followed Cristiano Ronaldo’s steps - he’s one of the greatest ever in terms of how a footballer should live, how they should work. I used him to master my craft.

“After the loan, I came back to Chelsea and made my debut. It’s been an unbelievable journey, from not liking football to then being here.”

Chalobah experienced plenty during his three early spells away from Stamford Bridge. First came relegation from the second tier with Ipswich before survival with Huddersfield.

Then came his first taste of overseas football with Lorient, for whom he made 30 appearances.

He said: “The first two or three months at Ipswich were tough. I’d gone from academy football to the Championship, which is strong, physical. I had to adapt to two games per week but the players helped. I knew I was good, but I had to adapt to a game where there were long balls, second balls. I was playing in midfield, out of position. I was a centre-back in the middle of the pitch.

“At Chelsea you won every week, at Ipswich we did not. It was a test but it was needed. Men’s football. An important skipper, senior boys shouting, angry because of the game you played. You don’t see that in academy football.

“At Ipswich we needed points but we didn’t get enough. Relegation… seeing those faces in the dressing room. It was the same at Huddersfield but there we managed to stay up. I’d seen two sides of football.

“Then I went to France with Lorient, to play teams like Paris Saint-Germain and push myself further in a different type of football. I learned French, but I’ve forgotten a lot of it. I’d cycle to training, lived by the beach. Amazing. It was also Covid-19 time, with nothing else to do but concentrate on football. I really kicked on.”

There is a fine line between being a promising academy player at Celtic and becoming an established first-team regular. Dane Murray is beginning to discover just how difficult it can be to cross it.

Four consecutive starts under Martin O’Neill have given the 23-year-old centre-half a chance to stake his claim after limited senior involvement in recent years. Yet, at a club where expectations are relentless, and mistakes rarely go unnoticed, establishing a foothold can be as demanding as earning one.

Managing to withstand criticism is part and parcel of being a Celtic player. Murray is under no illusion about what comes next as he attempts to become a regular presence in the heart of Celtic’s backline.

“That's your aim to try to play as many games as you can, so it's good,” he says of his recent involvement. It is a uncomplicated expression of ambition from a defender who understands that his place in the side remains far from guaranteed. Breaking into the starting XI is one thing. Staying there is another challenge entirely.

There remains plenty to learn and Murray is conscious of how much ground he still has to cover. The immediate priority is to become accustomed to the physical, tactical and psychological demands of senior football, even if those lessons can be unforgiving.

“It's probably hard to say right now, but I think I am just trying to concentrate for 90 minutes,” Murray then explained when asked where he is improving. “I mean, I gave away a penalty last week in the third minute – it probably doesn't shine a light on it! But I think it's just concentration, that's the main thing.

“I've only made four professional starts in a row. I've not really made too many appearances, so it's just trying to get up to speed as quick as possible.”

The willingness to acknowledge his own mistakes is revealing as Murray attempts to navigate the process of turning promise into permanence. A single lapse can prove costly, while the scrutiny that accompanies every performance leaves little room for prolonged self-doubt.

The ability to move beyond such moments is essential and Murray's approach is straightforward: “I don't have a secret, to be honest. I've not got one. I just keep my head down, keep working. That's pretty much it.”

It is a mentality that should serve him well. For now, though, there are more lessons for Murray to absorb, not least from the experienced defenders around him. The likes of Kieran Tierney, Liam Scales and Cameron Carter-Vickers offer a reference point for what sustained success at Celtic requires.

“It's massive,” says the academy graduate of learning from his senior colleagues. “I think you see what they've done in their careers and what they still do. They're all international players. They've made plenty of appearances for this club, so if I can take a wee bit off them, I'll be doing alright.”

European football adds an additional pressure and with competition for places fierce, Murray believes his exposure on the continental stage is an important part of his education and will aid his development. “I feel like it gives me a lot of confidence playing in Europe,” he says. “Now in the short term I don't really think about it too much but further down the line we'll see what happens.”

“A lot of speculation was around Dundee at the time, but for me it was ultimately just to keep my head down,” he says. “I don't really listen to that stuff. I think I always want to play here, that's my main focus. My main objective is to play at Celtic and be here for as long as I can do.”

Murray is not navigating the challenge alone. His close relationship with fellow youngster Colby Donovan provides an additional source of support as both look to establish themselves in the senior squad.

“We're right good pals, so it's good playing alongside him,” Murray says. “We do a lot off the pitch together. Everyone's really close in the group. We all get on really well, so it's good to have a team full of mates.”

At Celtic, nobody is guaranteed time to grow. Murray's ambition is to ensure he earns it. The opportunity is there; the challenge is to make it last.

Former Manchester United manager Erik ten Hag has accused the club's hierarchy of fueling a 'toxic culture'.

Ten Hag, who was sacked in October 2024, has been forthright in his opinion of his former employers in a new interview with The Athletic, accusing the club of giving too much power to the players.

The 56-year-old had a number of high-profile spats with members of his squad during his two years in charge, including with the likes of Cristiano Ronaldo, Jadon Sancho and Marcus Rashford.

He has now claimed he was not supported by the club in sanctions on matters of player discipline, with Ten Hag wielding a reputation as an authoritarian during his time in charge. Rashford, for example, was left out of the Dutchman's starting line-up for a Premier League game against Wolves because he overslept and arrived late to a team meeting.

'And so, they (the club) are continuing the toxic culture; they fuel it, they give the power to the players,' Ten Hag told The Athletic.

'Rashford is an amazing player, if his mindset is right. I always showed my trust in him, played him. But when you show these standards, sometimes you have to make corrections.'

Erik ten Hag had a number of high-profile spats with members of his squad during his two years in charge, the most high-profile with Cristiano Ronaldo

In a new interview with The Athletic, the former Manchester United boss has claimed that the club's hierarchy have allowed player power to proliferate

Ten Hag's most infamous spat came with Ronaldo, who claimed he had 'no respect' for the coach in a controversial interview with Piers Morgan in November 2022, which resulted in the Portugal forward's exit.

In terms of Ronaldo's influence on the dressing room, Ten Hag said: 'Players have to act, they have a big responsibility. Football is, for some, not the highest priority. And United for some wasn’t the highest priority.'

Ten Hag's comments are timely given it has been less than a week since Lisandro Martinez accused United's hierarchy of a lack of investment in the squad over the summer and said that he expected more from 'upstairs', singling out technical director Jason Wilcox.

Despite refusing to comment on the Martinez situation, Ten Hag did accuse the former Real Madrid contingent – which included Ronaldo, Raphael Varane and Casemiro – of introducing a power imbalance.

'I can say I think that’s what the Madrid connection brought in, that players are powerful and they want to decide,' he said. 'In my opinion it didn’t suit the phase United was in. The leadership wanted attacking, dynamic football and these players wanted to play what fitted themselves.'

Casemiro came to United as the midfield strategist with 'quarterback qualities', added Ten Hag. 'Then of course it looked like he got demotivated. I don’t know what they told him.'

Player power has come under scrutiny on several occasions since Sir Alex Ferguson, an unyielding disciplinarian, departed the club in 2013.

Ten Hag's successor Ruben Amorim was forced to wield the axe on several occasions after his players challenged his authority during his time at the reins.

Ten Hag's comments come a week after Lisandro Martinez accused United's hierarchy of a lack of investment in the squad over the summer

Among them, Amorim claimed he would rather play his 63-year-old goalkeeper coach Jorge Vital in his team instead of Rashford unless the winger started to 'give the maximum every day in training and in life', a dispute which eventually led to the Englishman's departure away on loan to Aston Villa and Barcelona.

Later, another academy graduate Alejandro Garnacho was told he could join a new club after he had questioned Amorim's call to drop him from the starting line-up of their Europa League final against Tottenham last year.

Martinez, who publicly apologised for his comments last week, was handed a spot by Michael Carrick in the starting XI against Leeds at the weekend, where they could only manage a 1-1 draw.

Despite former United captain Wayne Rooney claiming that Martinez's comments could rip the dressing room apart, Carrick has insisted that there is harmony.

Ahead of the Leeds game, Carrick said: 'The squad today was in a fantastic place, better than I've seen it for a while actually.

'We're living it every day, we know what it feels like. We're emotional, we're passionate, we care a lot. Sometimes someone says something that maybe they shouldn't say at a certain time, but that doesn't distract us from what we're creating here and the environment and the spirit within the group.

'It's not affected. I'm telling you as it is. We're in a good place, and I'm really happy.'
