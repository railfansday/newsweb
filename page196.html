#4095. Play ‘Doom’ on this tiny, uterus-shaped period tracker

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Published Oct 23, 2026 3:42 PM EDT

Doom is much more than a classic, bloodsoaked first-person shooter known for its chaotic action, over-the-top weapons, and challenging boss fights. For some odd (but very enjoyable) reason, it’s served as a benchmark for testing basic computing capabilities for well over 30 years. Name a device——calculators, ATMs, digitally enabled tractors, and even biochips built with E. coli bacteria—and someone has likely coded it to run Doom.

The latest entry in the ever-growing list of Doom compatible contraptions is a double-dose of DIY ingenuity, however. The Linux software engineer known as Miss Molerat recently began tinkering with a pocket-sized medical gadget made specifically to keep track of her menstrual cycle. But unlike many similar commercial products, “Clotty” keeps all personal data strictly on the device itself. It never connects to an external cloud or offloads sensitive information to a company’s servers, thereby preventing any unwanted data harvesting. And it does all this on a custom, uterus-shaped minicomputer running on a Raspberry Pi microcontroller.

“I have one thing that has been bothering me for as long as I’ve been menstruating: despite my cycle being very regular, I somehow manage to get surprised by PMS each and every month anew,” Clotty’s inventor wrote on her website’s blog.

Clotty “does the boring stuff” for her by tracking her period via a Tamagotchi-like digital pet that requires attention and maintenance throughout each month. The pixelated creature’s mood shifts depending on the cycle phase, but it also can go on little quests and offer various minigames in the meantime.

“[It’s] basically some sort of adorably shaped mirror version of my own moods that serves as a reminder that I, too, am just some sort of creature that tries to stay alive and happy,” explained Clotty’s creator.

But why stop there? Clotty is powerful enough to run Doom on its itty-bitty LCD screen, which is thematically perfect given the project’s overarching, bloody motif. This means that, when not keeping up with personal health, a user can also blast through wave after wave of space demons using small onboard sensors.

The inventor admitted that Clotty is still in its “fetal stage,” but she plans to add more features like a menstrual cycle history archive, more games, and even some reproductive health trivia. It’s also a completely open-source project, so anyone can follow her online tutorials and download files for Clotty (and Doom).

Apple’s iPhone Duo brings iOS to a bigger tablet-like display, and these creative apps are already optimized to make the most of it.

The iPhone Duo is easily the most experimental iPhone that Apple has released. That’s both good news and bad news.

On one hand, the shape-shifting design, like on existing foldable Android phones I’ve tested, lends itself to creative use cases, such as hands-free entertainment and self-mounting for long-exposure photos.

On the other hand, the device is so new to iOS that some of your most frequently used apps may not yet be optimized to take advantage of the added screen space. There’s even a “does it work on iPhone Duo?” web tracker.

Needless to say, the next few weeks and months will be very telling about the future of apps on the iPhone Duo. Dozens, if not hundreds, of the App Store’s popular picks are already being worked on to take advantage of the flexible iPhone Duo screen. They’ll be labeled with a “Made for iPhone Duo” badge on their product page.

If you’re one of the lucky few who’s getting an iPhone Duo today (or soon), I’ve listed below some of the standouts that have already been optimized for the iPhone Duo and that I’ve personally tested.

One of the primary iPhone Duo use cases is multimedia consumption, whether it’s streaming live sports and keeping tabs on player stats or docking the phone to catch up on the latest episode of Below hands-free.

Of all the streaming services I’ve used on my iPhone Duo thus far, Netflix is the most polished, with seven (from what I’ve tallied) different orientations for watching movies and shows. There’s the standard widescreen layout when the iPhone Duo is unfolded, as well as the ability to fold it and set it down like a tent.

For something more creative, you can rotate the iPhone Duo to portrait orientation and have the video play in the upper third of the screen while you use a different app in the bottom half.

I’m no Michelin-star chef, but thanks to the dishes I’ve been able to make with the NYT Cooking app, at least my wife hasn’t completely banned me from the kitchen.

Previously, I’d look through and follow recipes on an iPad Air that’s docked on the countertop. With the iPhone Duo, I’ve been setting it down so that one half of the inner screen is lifted, toggling on Cook mode, and increasing the text size so I can comfortably scan the instructions from afar.

There’s nothing more satisfying than shutting the phone closed at the end of cooking and seeing a countertop that’s a little more tidy than before.

I’ve never been a big to-do list guy; I’m the type who just waits for a calendar event to notify me, and I prepare then and there for what’s to come. Fantastical may keep me in better order.

It’s a calendar app that doubles as a task manager and weather tracker, which just makes sense. With the iPhone Duo unfolded, you get a clear view of your day’s tasks and appointments, what the week, month, quarter or even year looks like, and the latest weather forecast. It can easily be an information overload for some people, but I’m a fan of how everything’s color-coded and organized.

Fantastical also supports enhanced Siri AI interactions, so you can add calendar events, to-dos and other happenings through voice or text prompts.

For content creators, the Edits app is one of the most popular video editing tools. It’s the one I’ve been using over the past year to produce reels and other short-form videos, and the latest update for the iPhone Duo makes my job easier.

Edits can now show a preview monitor of what you’re editing on one half of the inner screen, while the other half shows your timeline. That means you can comfortably add effects or text, set keyframes, and scrub through the timeline without having to minimize and expand the preview to see how the changes look.

That’s also my segue to give flowers to two apps I’ve spent an unreasonable amount of time on: TikTok and Instagram. In line with the iPhone Duo launch, both social media apps have been optimized for the foldable iPhone’s larger screen. You can now watch videos on the left side while reading and interacting with comments on the right.

Safe to say, it’s my new favorite way to doomscroll at night.

Toastie is easily the most whimsical “for iPhone Duo” app I’ve used, and it reminds me a lot of the early iPhone days, when chugging digital beer and swiping a paper ball based on the aerodynamics of an office cubicle were the greatest things ever.

The goal of this game is to prepare sandwiches as perfectly as possible, from the way slices of cheese are placed to how evenly spread out the butter is, to the amount of time they’re pressed in the toaster.

That’s why I highly encourage you to head to your local Apple store, experiment with app pairings on the iPhone Duo, and decide whether the two screens actually make you more productive.

#3553. Israel’s Extremist Election

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

The run-up to the vote on Tuesday is exposing the façade of Israeli democracy—and the fealty of the country’s so-called opposition when it comes to matters of Jewish supremacy.

The façade of Israeli democracy has been exposed countless times over the years, but perhaps never quite so brazenly as inside the Supreme Court in Jerusalem three weeks ago. There, an esteemed panel of justices dispensed with decades of precedent by refusing to overturn the Central Elections Committee’s decision to disqualify a Palestinian candidate—this time Balad leader Sami Abu Shehadeh—from standing in next Tuesday’s election.

The sole basis for his disqualification was an article published on October 8, 2023, in which Abu Shehadeh described the previous day’s Hamas-led attack on Israel as a “significant historical event.” The court deemed this to have constituted support for armed struggle against Israel, forcing him to drop out of the race.

Such a move would have been damning enough in isolation. But it cannot be understood without considering the fact that Israeli law also permits the disqualification of candidates for incitement to racism—yet Jewish candidates who have endorsed dropping a nuclear bomb on Gaza, called for the “total annihilation” of its cities, stated that “there should be one sentence for everyone there: death,” and demanded that “all of Lebanon must burn” remain free to run. Go figure.

This election is not, however, a story only about the racism of Israel’s extreme right. Every single Jewish party in the Knesset supported Abu Shehadeh’s disqualification, exposing the fealty of Israel’s so-called opposition when it comes to matters of Jewish supremacy. Only the Palestinian parties of the Joint List (comprising Hadash, Ta’al, and Abu Shehadeh’s Balad) offer any real resistance to the policies of apartheid, ethnic cleansing, and genocide that have accelerated dramatically under Israel’s outgoing government.

Paradoxically, it is the Joint List and the Islamist party Ra’am that hold the key to the opposition’s chances of dethroning Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. The polls could not be clearer: Without these Arab parties, the anti-Netanyahu bloc will not reach the 61 mandates required for a Knesset majority. With their support, it could topple Netanyahu—whose own bloc of loyalists looks set to fall significantly short of a majority—virtually overnight.

But on the eve of the election, the anti-Netanyahu bloc appears incapable of doing what is required to send Israel’s longest-serving prime minister to his political grave (and, perhaps, his prison cell). Racism, it turns out, is not only a moral deficiency; it is also terrible politics. And rather than win with the support of Arab parties, Israel’s opposition is preparing to lose without them.

The previous election, in late 2022, ended a political stalemate that had brought Israelis to the polls five times in less than four years—a prolonged referendum on Netanyahu that saw him briefly lose the premiership in the fourth cycle before regaining it in the fifth. Tuesday’s vote takes place on much the same terrain, pitting a pro-Netanyahu coalition of far-right and ultra-Orthodox parties against a looser alliance of centrist and right-wing parties seeking to depose him.

In addition to Netanyahu’s political future, the main issues on the ballot include the cost of living, the conscription of ultra-Orthodox Jews into the military, a state inquiry into the security failings surrounding October 7, and Israel’s deteriorating standing abroad. The policies causing that deterioration remain conspicuously absent from the conversation, save for the near-universal denunciation of the new documentary NAZA.

The pro-Netanyahu bloc comprises the parties of the current government—Likud, Otzma Yehudit, the Religious Zionist Party, Shas, and United Torah Judaism—plus two additional figures from the extreme right hoping to join them. The first is Ofer Winter, a retired senior military commander leading the new party Amcha Yisrael, whose entire election platform revolves around expelling Palestinians from Gaza. (Winter is polling on the cusp of the electoral threshold of 3.25 percent; if he falls below it, all of his votes will go to waste, giving a boost to the opposition.)

The second is Moshe Feiglin, one of the most bizarre men in Israeli politics, whose libertarian party Zehut is running on a joint ticket with Bezalel Smotrich’s Religious Zionist Party. Feiglin’s greatest hits include “Every baby in Gaza is an enemy,” “Kill everything that moves,” and, essentially, Hitler was right but about the wrong people. (Likud and the Religious Zionist Party–Zehut have signed a surplus-vote agreement, meaning votes for Netanyahu may end up strengthening Feiglin and Smotrich, and vice versa.)

The opposing bloc of anti-Netanyahu parties is led this time by former Israeli army chief Gadi Eisenkot, who heads the newcomer party Yashar. He is credited with developing the Dahiyeh Doctrine, a military strategy honed in Lebanon and premised on the wholesale destruction of civilian infrastructure in the hope of turning local residents against Hezbollah—foreshadowing the doctrine of annihilation unleashed in Gaza over the past three years.

Eisenkot was party to that too: He served in Netanyahu’s war cabinet during the deadliest months of Israel’s onslaught, joining almost immediately after October 7 before resigning in June 2024 over the prime minister’s obstruction of a hostage deal.

So what is he offering? Eisenkot has repeatedly vowed that no Palestinian state will arise under his premiership, even attacking Netanyahu for having previously supported a two-state solution. He is in favor of building new Jewish settlements in Area C, the 60 percent of the West Bank under complete Israeli control, and does not rule out construction in the particularly controversial E1 zone.

In Gaza, Lebanon, and Syria, he wants to keep at least some of the land that Israel has militarily occupied under the outgoing government. And he recently stated that National Security Minister Itamar Ben Gvir “probably did good things in prisons”—where over 100 Palestinians have died since October 7 as a result of systematic torture, medical neglect, and food deprivation.

The other leaders of Eisenkot’s would-be coalition include former settler council leader Naftali Bennett (head of Together), who wants to annex Area C of the West Bank; Avigdor Liberman (Yisrael Beitenu), Netanyahu’s former defense minister, who declared in November 2023, “There are no innocents in Gaza”; and Yair Golan (the Democrats), a former deputy military chief, who advocated starving Gaza’s entire population until Hamas released the hostages. (The Reservists–New Economic Party, a new center-right alliance focused on enforcing the draft in Haredi communities and fixing the cost of living, would also likely join the anti-Netanyahu bloc if it manages to cross the electoral threshold.)

What sets these figures apart from the current government, however, is primarily their desire to dial down the volume of global outrage against Israel.

To that end, Eisenkot has promised to crack down on “Jewish terrorism” in the West Bank (albeit because it is “harming the settlement enterprise”), and would likely seek to advance Trump’s 20-point plan for Gaza where Netanyahu has stalled it. Proposals to expel 2 million Palestinians from the Strip would no longer be on the table. Eisenkot would also halt the government’s assault on Israel’s judiciary, and may pour greater resources into tackling the mafia groups that have been allowed to run rampant within Palestinian communities inside Israel.

There is just one problem: They do not have the votes.

The most favorable polls are giving the anti-Netanyahu bloc a seat count in the low-to-mid-50s (out of the Knesset’s 120), leaving it around 10 seats short of a majority. Luckily for them, there is an easy way to make up the difference. Unluckily for them, it involves sitting with—or at least relying on the votes of —Arab parties, which the bloc’s leaders have almost unanimously ruled out.

There will be two Arab parties in the next Knesset representing Israel’s Palestinian citizens: the Joint List (polling at around seven to nine seats) and Ra’am (around five or six seats). While the Joint List is in every sense a Palestinian political force, Ra’am leader Mansour Abbas has sought to downplay his party’s Palestinian identity, focusing on the fight against organized crime in Arab communities while speaking less about the West Bank and Gaza. He has brought in a Jewish lawmaker as his number two and distanced the party from its roots in the Islamic Movement, hoping to broaden his appeal among the anti-Netanyahu bloc. It has not worked.

In 2021, Ra’am became the first independent Arab party to join an Israeli government, led by Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid. But the move was fraught with tension, and the government imploded after just one year. Upon the government’s collapse, Netanyahu—who had courted Ra’am himself during the previous election campaign—proclaimed that “the experiment failed.” Four years on, with much of Israeli society gripped by a racist fervor that has reached new levels in the wake of October 7, the chances of a repeat appear extremely slim.

Ahead of Tuesday’s vote, Golan, the anti-Netanyahu bloc’s most left-wing leader, has said he would sit with Ra’am but not the Joint List (which would not join a governing coalition anyway). Liberman voted to disqualify both parties from the race altogether, and has repeatedly ruled out sitting with either. Bennett is refusing to sit with them too, while Eisenkot told a closed meeting of Bedouin leaders that he wants to include Ra’am in his government, then denied it, and has subsequently maintained that neither Arab party will be part of his coalition.

There is, however, another option here: a minority government propped up by the votes of Arab parties from outside the coalition, known in electoral parlance as a “safety net.” Such an arrangement would be highly unstable, but it is what enabled Yitzhak Rabin to sign the Oslo Accords into law in the 1990s. The Joint List is willing to play this role if it means toppling Netanyahu, and Eisenkot has hinted that he views this option favorably. But Liberman and Bennett have both dismissed it out of hand.

Barring any major shock in Tuesday’s results, then, neither bloc appears capable of forming a viable government in its current configuration—unless several of these leaders break their promises, which would certainly not be a first for Israeli politics. If they want to avoid triggering another election, both sides will need to get creative.

The most straightforward option would be to try to poach defectors from the opposing bloc. Netanyahu is reportedly eyeing up Bennett’s faction in the event of a deadlock (though Bennett’s campaign centers on enforcing the draft in ultra-Orthodox communities, thus making a coalition with Haredi parties seem implausible), while Eisenkot hopes to lure deserters from Netanyahu’s Likud. But acquiring more than a handful of defectors would be a mammoth task.

For his part, President Isaac Herzog, who has the unenviable job of overseeing the coalition-building process, is trying to engineer another possibility: a “national unity” government bringing together Netanyahu, Eisenkot, and whichever other parties would join them. A majority of the public favors this over going back to the polls a few months later.

Should Netanyahu conclude that a unity government is his only route to remaining prime minister, even under a rotation agreement with Eisenkot, it would surely appeal to him—especially if he gets the first turn as prime minister and can then collapse the government before having to relinquish power to Eisenkot. Yet, while some members of Eisenkot’s party appear open to this scenario, Eisenkot himself has, at least for now, ruled it out unequivocally. Meanwhile, Liberman, who might seem a likely candidate to join a unity government, has declared that he won’t sit with Netanyahu “come hell or high water.”

In short, we’re in for a messy few weeks, or perhaps months, as these various scenarios are gamed out and written off. What remains, if nobody succeeds in assembling a new government, is another election early next year, with Netanyahu remaining as caretaker prime minister in the interim.

Crucially, however, there is a lot that can be done with a Knesset majority even without forming a government. If the anti-Netanyahu bloc, the Joint List, and Ra’am together control more than 60 seats after Tuesday, they could vote to replace the Knesset speaker and immediately begin passing legislation independently of the government.

They could repeal the outgoing government’s judicial overhaul laws; establish a state inquiry commission to investigate the security failings of October 7, which this government has strongly resisted; and even pass a law preventing someone under indictment from serving as prime minister, effectively ending Netanyahu’s political career.

These plans are already being drawn up, although there are doubts as to whether the bloc can maintain its unity in the face of the inevitable onslaught of pressure from Netanyahu and his acolytes. And without forming a new government, the countdown to the next election will have already begun.

#4288. Will a macaque eat a grape sitting on a pile of poo? A study on germophobia

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

One of the macaques at a Texas sanctuary, where researchers ran tests to see if the monkeys became germophobes after experiencing a viral outbreak. Stephan Kaufhold hide caption

In the very early days of COVID-19, millions who'd rarely thought twice about germ avoidance became germophobes.

Some folks wiped down groceries before stuffing them into the refrigerator. Others let mail quarantine outside for days lest it harbor stray viruses. With the hindsight that SARS-CoV-2 spread primarily through the air, these sorts of precautions seem extreme. But in the moment, they were perhaps reasonable ways of avoiding an unknown pathogen.

Around this same time, a different virus hit a macaque sanctuary in Texas. New research suggests the monkeys responded in similar ways as humans did to COVID-19 — by being extra germophobic.

Humans are far from the only animals to avoid possible sources of disease. Scientists have found that critters as diverse as monkeys and nematodes (aka roundworms) can sense and steer clear of feces, for instance, which can harbor harmful bacteria.

"Something that has been less studied is the question of how flexible this avoidance is," says Stephan Kaufhold, a cognitive scientist at UC San Diego. He wanted to see if experiencing an outbreak could make individual monkeys even more wary of picking up pathogens.

The outbreak in the Texas sanctuary, home to over 100 macaques, offered Kaufhold and his colleagues an opportunity to put that question to the test.

In 2020, macaques in an enclosure at the sanctuary started getting sick with an HIV-like virus called SRV.

Once a monkey gets infected through bodily fluids, the disease "can take a rapid progression, where about three to four weeks after you initially get infected, you might die," says Kaufhold. Many monkeys died, while others developed chronic immunodeficiency.

Sanctuary staff quickly started separating monkeys quickly, ultimately creating two groups — those who'd experienced the virus, either by getting sick themselves or witnessing others get sick, and those who hadn't experienced the outbreak.

To see if those different histories made the monkeys more germophobic, the researchers presented individuals with two tasty treats — grapes.

"They get a fantastic diet at the sanctuary, but not grapes every day, so grapes were definitely a treat," says Kaufhold.

One red grape was presented on a non-descript, clean-looking piece of wood. The other was on top of macaque poop, encased in epoxy. "Basically not to make the animals eat off actual feces," says Kaufhold. "It did look quite gross, feces with a glossy wet look. Definitely worked also in triggering disgust in humans."

From 2022 to 2024, the researchers presented monkeys with this choice in nearly 200 trials. Among monkeys who didn't experience the outbreak, 79% ate the poop-plated grape. But only 47% of the outbreak veterans ate it, researchers report in the journal Royal Society Open Science.

Essentially, those who'd been oblivious to the outbreak were more likely to throw caution to the wind and eat the poop-plated grape than those who'd lived through it.

"I was surprised," says Kaufhold. "I think it's pretty clear evidence that something is going on." Kaufhold thinks that something might be a heightened sensitivity to disgust.

"If you think about disgust, the evolutionary origins most likely are to try to protect the body from contamination because there are pathogens and parasites that we ideally would want to avoid," he says. Becoming more easily disgusted after a devastating outbreak could pay off if it means avoiding infection, of whatever cause.

Of course, scientists can't know whether macaques are actually experiencing disgust. "But the possibility that things like disgust are influencing the behavior of other species, and especially species like primates, it's definitely a possibility worth considering," says Andrew MacIntosh, a biologist at the Wilder Institute who wasn't involved in the study but has done similar experiments with macaques.

"It seems plausible that past exposure to outbreaks could make individuals more cautious or risk averse," he says, but he thinks more evidence is needed to show that. For example, the experiment was carried out several years after the outbreak, enough time for other factors to influence these behaviors, he says.

MacIntosh also points out that the fecal avoidance experiment bears little connection to how the initial outbreak spread, through bodily fluids transmitted through scratches or bites. "The mode of transmission of that virus has really nothing to do with fecal contamination," he says.

"We're not claiming that macaques had any sort of understanding of the mechanism of viruses, it just seems to be an increased caution," he says. People didn't initially know how SARS-CoV-2 spread either, he says, but many went to great lengths to avoid infection. Those impulses may have been shaped not by paranoia, but by evolution, as a way of staving off sickness in an uncertain world.

#3560. Why Trump Is Embracing Vivek Ramaswamy’s Vile Ad

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

The president is leaning into the politics of toxic parenting.

Donald Trump greets Ohio Republican gubernatorial candidate Vivek Ramaswamy on stage at the Butler-Vandalia Student Activity Center during a campaign rally on October 03, 2026, in Vandalia, Ohio.

Donald Trump’s many offenses against decency should be viewed not only as moral transgressions, but as political acts. He has become the dominant political figure of our era in part by using vulgarity and nastiness to form a lasting bond with his supporters.

This is easy enough to understand when Trump makes racist, sexist, or transphobic comments that, alas, excite voters who want to roll back egalitarian politics. But sometimes Trump’s affronts to basic morality are harder to parse at first glance—such as his decision this week to champion a vile political attack ad aired by Ohio Republican gubernatorial nominee Vivek Ramaswamy against his Democratic opponent, Amy Acton.

The ad features Acton’s biological mother, Donna Arthur, describing her daughter as a “liar” and warning Ohioans not to vote for her. The major problem with the ad, as my Nation colleague Chris Lehmann deftly covered, is the context it leaves out. In 1978, Donna Arthur’s husband, Acton’s stepfather Cledith Arthur, was arrested for raping his stepdaughter. (This was just four years after Cledith had been charged with raping his daughter from another marriage, according to reporting from HuffPost.) Donna Arthur supported her husband in the case for the rest of his life, even as he skipped a bond and fled Ohio to evade arrest. The Arthurs lost custody of Acton and her brother (who supports his sister and was also abused). Donna Arthur hasn’t spoken to her daughter in nearly half a century.

The decision of the Ramaswamy campaign to weaponize this horrific family story has disgusted not just Democrats but also a wide swath of Republicans, including Ohio luminaries such as incumbent Governor Mike DeWine, Senator Jon Husted, former governor John Kasich and former senator Rob Portman. DeWine described the ad as “deeply, deeply troubling,” though he declined to withdraw his endorsement of Ramaswamy. Outside of Ohio, the ad was condemned by an array of prominent Republicans such as Chris Christie and Nikki Haley.

However, the two most powerful Republicans in the country, Donald Trump and Vice President JD Vance, are standing firmly by Ramaswamy (who is so unrepentant about the ad that he is spending $4 million to air it even more).

Vance’s logic here is strictly partisan: Republicans should back the red team, no matter what. In a Truth Social post on Thursday, Trump echoed this factional reasoning. But he added an interesting twist, suggesting that he would welcome other ads from mothers denouncing Democrats:

Vivek Ramaswamy put up a very powerful Ad about a mother saying that her daughter, Amy Acton, who is running for Governor of Ohio, is a liar, and much worse. The Press, in a concerted and well coordinated attack, is trying to get Vivek Ramaswamy to “take down the Ad.” Frankly, anybody whose mother spoke about a daughter in that way would normally lead to the daughter being precluded from serving in Office, let alone the Office of Governor. If my mother, the legendary Mary MacLeod Trump, said anything bad about me, the Fake News would play it up. #4196. AI Can Spot Signs of Impending Death Among Cancer Patients in a Single Face Photo

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

A new facial-analysis system predicts cancer mortality risk, but its clinical usefulness remains unproven.

Doctors have long studied their patients’ faces for signs of illness. But a new AI system has taken it a step further.

In a remarkable new study, researchers developed an artificial intelligence system that could identify cancer patients at unusually high risk of dying. Of the 122 patients assigned the highest risk scores, more than half died within just 90 days of having their picture taken. Among 975 patients in the lowest-risk group, only 20 died during the same period.

Our faces may carry subtle signs of deteriorating health that even experienced doctors struggle to quantify. Trained on more than 40 million facial photographs, the AI learned to recognize patterns associated with biological aging and cancer survival.

However, it’s not like you can upload a selfie to this system and find out your life expectancy. It’s more complicated than that, and, for now, the higher-than-chance prediction rate is validated in quite ill cancer patients.

“We’re trained in medical school to walk into a room, look at the patient and make an assessment — an impression of how healthy, frail or sick they are,” Raymond Mak, a radiation oncologist at Mass General Brigham and a senior author of the study, told Scientific American. The problem, he said, is “that’s really a subjective assessment.”

An AI could do a face check more reliably than a human doctor, helping medical staff evaluate whether a patient can withstand difficult treatments.

The researchers began by training an AI model called FAHR-Face on more than 40 million facial photographs. During training, the system repeatedly hid random sections of each face and learned to reconstruct the missing details, teaching itself to recognize minute facial features and patterns.

They then developed two specialized versions. One learned to estimate a person’s biological age using more than 740,000 photographs with known ages. In an independent public-photo test, its age estimates missed the actual age by an average of 5.1 years. The other studied photographs and survival records from 34,363 cancer patients, learning which facial characteristics were associated with living longer or dying sooner.

Please check your inbox and confirm your subscription.

Among the patients treated with radiotherapy, looking older than one’s actual age was associated with poorer survival. After accounting for other clinical factors, patients whose facial age exceeded their real age by at least five years had a 21 percent higher rate of death over follow-up compared with those below that threshold.

In a separate test of FAHR-FaceSurvival involving 3,820 patients, 64 of the 122 people with the highest scores (52.5 percent) died within 90 days of their photographs. Among 975 patients with the lowest scores, 20 (2.1 percent) died in that period. Even after accounting for age, cancer diagnosis and treatment, patients in the highest-risk quarter based on their facial features had more than three times the death rate of those in the lowest-risk quarter.

To check whether their findings held up beyond the original patients, the researchers tested both AI tools on a separate group of 4,892 cancer patients in the Netherlands. The results followed the same pattern: patients whose faces looked older than their actual age tended to die sooner. The same was true for those flagged as high-risk by the survival-prediction AI.

What’s interesting here is that the two tools picked up on different warning signs. So, by using both together, doctors may offer a slightly better picture of a patient’s survival prospects than using either tool alone.

To estimate biological age, the first AI focused on the nasolabial folds — the creases running from either side of the nose toward the corners of the mouth — as well as the forehead just above the eyes and the temples. These are areas where wrinkles, changes in skin elasticity and loss of facial volume can become more noticeable as we age.

The survival-prediction AI, however, paid particular attention to the area beneath the eyes and around the bridge of the nose. But what exactly was it detecting? Was it dark circles, puffiness, hollowing beneath the eyes or something far subtler? The odd thing is that the researchers don’t yet know. Their analysis revealed which facial regions influenced the predictions, but not which specific physical changes signaled a greater risk of death. So, this black box AI won’t give us any hints that one might use by looking in the mirror.

The research extends the same team’s 2025 FaceAge study, which linked older-looking faces to shorter cancer survival. Another study published in April 2026 found that patients whose facial appearance aged faster across successive photographs also tended to have poorer outcomes.

The researchers also found links between older-looking facial age and smoking, heavy drinking and drug use. Estimates were reasonably consistent across makeup, facial expressions and lighting, though image conditions can still affect them. As we’re all painfully aware, a selfie taken during a certain time of the day or after a rough night can make you look a bit older and beaten up than you normally would.

The American cancer-patient test group was about 85 percent White, and both clinical cohorts consisted of people receiving radiotherapy. This means, these tools cannot be used confidently with the general population. The study did not attempt to assess whether AI score improves treatment decisions or outperforms doctors’ assessments of frailty. That would be a study for another time.

“Predicting an outcome is not the same as explaining it,” Miroslaw Bober, an AI researcher at the University of Surrey who was not involved, said in comments published by the Science Media Centre.

And while such AIs could be useful, there’s also the ethical risk that a wrong statistical forecast can change a patient’s fate. Matthew Liao, a New York University bioethicist, warned Scientific American that errors could result in patients being denied care. He also raised concerns about insurers or employers exploiting facial health predictions.

For now, the face offers a provocative clue in some patients — but thankfully not a medical verdict.

The findings appeared in The Lancet Digital Health.

#4098. Psychologist found the first corporate psychopath—a 17th century Dutch mutineer

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Published Oct 23, 2026 12:48 PM EDT

The concept of a corporate psychopath is eerily recognizable, depending on your work history. It’s also notoriously misunderstood. The archetype of the manipulative, self-serving manager has regularly resurfaced in popular culture for decades, but concrete evidence supporting the wider concept is debatable. For example, a research study went viral in 2016 after estimating upwards of 1-in-5 business employees fit the “psychopath” bill, only to be retracted two years later. Additionally, psychiatrists widely agree that only about 1 percent of the general population truly can be considered clinically psychopathic.

That said, many of the people determined to claw their way to the top of the company ladder display at least some of the trademark psychopathic behaviors. Many theorists have convincingly argued this has a lot to do with corporate capitalism’s foundational principles, which leave it easily exploited by the most ruthless and manipulative among us.

Clive Boddy, one of the world’s foremost investigators of corporate psychopathy, recently took this idea to its logical historical conclusion. In a study recently published in the Asian Journal of Business Ethics, Boddy reviewed one of toxic corporate leadership’s earliest documented examples—and he makes today’s nightmare coworkers sound tame in comparison.

Meet Jeronimus Cornelisz, an all-time contender for World’s Worst Boss. According to archival accounts, the 17th century Dutchman Cornelisz embodied every core corporate psychopath characteristic over 400 years ago while working for the conglomerate that started it all: the Dutch East India Company.

Established in Amsterdam in 1602, the Dutch East India Company is arguably the world’s first multinational, shareholder-owned company. It quickly amassed enough funds and power to rival most sovereign countries through the import and export of spices, tea, luxury items, coffee, wine, and enslaved populations. The Dutch East India Company eventually exerted enough influence to wage its own wars, mint its own currency, form colonies, as well as arrest and execute convicts.

All of this was made possible through a vast fleet of ships,but even the best outfitted vessels sometimes fell afoul of nature. In June 1629, a Dutch East India Company’s flagship called Batavia collided with a reef off the Western Australian coast. Nearly 300 survivors made it to the nearby island shores, but their remote location ensured they wouldn’t survive without help. The ship’s senior officers soon set off in an open longboat on a 1,900-mile journey to present-day Jakarta, then the Dutch East Indies’ capital. Before leaving, they appointed under-merchant Jeronimus Cornelisz to the top corporate position in their absence.

Unbeknownst to Batavia’s departing senior crew, Cornelisz had already spent weeks scheming on ways to incite a mutiny. The plan was to take control of the vessel then steer its copious stores of gold and silver to a new life. Cornelisz apparently viewed getting marooned as an inconvenient detour, and quickly got to work on his hostile takeover.

According to firsthand accounts, Cornelisz immediately consolidated power by swapping fellow council members with loyalists and requiring them to pledge an oath of allegiance to him. Next, he tricked suspected opponents under the direction of a soldier named Wiebbe Hayes to search a nearby island for supplies, then left them to die.

Although his revised plan required any impending rescue ship, Cornelisz’s ultimate goal remained the same—get as far away as possible, then start his own kingdom using riches plundered from Batavia. Over the ensuing months, Cornelisz directed his gang to kill more than 100 of the shipwrecked men, women, and children. Cornelisz often justified the murders under the pretense of conserving food and water, when in actuality he was continuing to eliminate perceived threats. Meanwhile, some of the surviving women were subjected to sexual abuse.

However, Cornelisz underestimated his abandoned compatriots. As the operatically psychopathic Cornelisz terrorized his group of survivors and scrounged for supplies, Hayes’ team reportedly discovered freshwater wells and hunted local wallabies, seals, and fish. The two sides subsequently squared off on multiple occasions after learning of each others’ continued existence until a rescue ship finally arrived in October 1629. After nearly three months, only about a third of Batavia’s original passengers and crew were left.

Management ultimately decided Cornelisz wasn’t internal promotion material, even by Dutch East India Company standards. Nearly everyone involved in his reign of terror was executed. As for Cornelisz and his closest loyalists, authorities eventually hanged them, but not before cutting off their hands.

The tragedy of the Batavia is an admittedly extreme case study, but Boddy argued that it illustrates how corporate structures have facilitated and encouraged psychopathic behaviors since the invention of the corporation itself.

“The same playbook we see in toxic workplaces today appears in this case from 1629,” he said. “He removed opponents, rewarded loyalists, created fear, manipulated followers and pursued power at any cost.”

In recent years, some historians have expressed doubts about details in the Batavia saga after reviewing available evidence and recontextualizing cultural dynamics. Instead of focusing on the machinations of a single ruthless corporate psychopath, they contend Batavia is more accurately a case study in group desperation. But even when viewed under this lens, the chilling event is still a cautionary tale about corporate capitalism’s darker underpinnings.

“Perhaps the most striking finding is that another leader facing the same circumstances achieved completely different outcomes,” Boddy said. “This was effectively a natural experiment in leadership. One group prospered while the other descended into murder and abuse.”

The satirical depictions of rabbits found in medieval books reveal a gallows humour for social inversion that would not feel out of place today.

Once thought to have been introduced by the Normans, archaeologists have since uncovered a rabbit bone from Roman Britain. However, documentary evidence of rabbits in the British Isles has only been found from the Norman period onwards.

One of the earliest references survives from the Scilly Isles in the form of a tithe, which was a tenth of produce given to the church as an offering. In 1176, the landowner Richard de Wyka admitted that he had withheld paying tithes on his rabbits because he had believed that “tithes were not payable on things of this kind”. His confusion suggests that rabbits were a novelty.

Rabbits continued to be sufficiently rare to warrant the king’s care and attention in the 1200s. In 1235, Henry III gave a gift of ten live rabbits to Geoffrey Despenser, the English landowner and Lord of Martley in Worcestershire, out of his park at Guildford.

A few years later, the king sent men to catch 30 or 40 rabbits in his Guildford park. They were told only to collect them if the warren was well stocked, indicating that the king didn’t want the colony to be depleted.

At the time, rabbits were kept for their meat and fur. Henry III sent out orders for the supply of venison, boars, fish, swans, peacocks, hens, eggs, and hares for various feasts in the early 13th century. It had not been until his Christmas feast of 1240 that he added rabbits to his requisition list.

Rabbits were an expensive delicacy, costing four or five times as much as chickens. Their owners guarded their warrens against poaching and predators to the point that watchtowers and stone lodges were built to protect them.

Yet rabbits did not remain the preserve of the aristocracy. The Black Death (1346 to 1353), which killed between a third and two-thirds of the European population, led to improving living standards for the survivors. Land became more freely available. Labourers could also charge more for their work. And many adopted the lifestyles of those higher up the social ladder.

Legislation was introduced in 1363 to restrict what people could wear according to their social status. Its introduction was explained because of “the excess of dress of people beyond their estate, to the very great destruction and impoverishment of the land.”

Rabbits reflected this social change. They became more commonplace during the 14th and 15th centuries as their meat was eaten by wealthier peasants and their furs worn by aspiring craftsmen.

The fur of the common grey rabbit was used for warmth, but some warrens specialised in rarer silver-grey or black rabbits. Rabbits were still fit for a king and Henry VII had night attire tailored with black rabbit fur.

As rabbit numbers increased through breeding, so too did the number of complaints about the destruction caused by them. Manorial accounts included repeated protests about the loss of crops and damage to land caused by the lord’s rabbits.

At the same time, hunting rabbits and other game was seen as potentially subversive. In 1390, following the Peasants’ Revolt of 1381, parliament introduced legislation against poaching and hunting by labourers and artisans. It was feared that, under the guise of hunting, such people would “make plots and conspiracies of rebellion” against the king.

The king consented to the legislation, extending the ban to the use of “hounds and ferrets, hays, nets, hair-pipes, cords and all other devices to take or destroy beasts of the forest, hares or rabbits, or other sport of gentlefolk”.

Rabbits and hares also reveal the satirical humour of medieval society. The Killer Rabbit of Caerbannog, the fictional and deceptively deadly rabbit from Monty Python and the Holy Grail (1975), is but a modern adaptation of this ironic imagery associated with medieval rabbits.

Rabbits adorn the margins of medieval manuscripts. Playful portrayals of rabbits jousting on the backs of snails point to a gentle self-mockery that pervades medieval culture – in this case of chivalric ideals. More in line with the killer rabbit myth, some went even further, depicting rabbits as violently attacking people and seeking retribution.

The Smithfield Decretals, a manuscript illuminated in London in the 1340s, contains one of the most elaborate of such examples. In it, rabbits are portrayed as turning the tables on their hunters, capturing them, holding them trial, and then executing their human and greyhound hunters.

Such examples fit well within the medieval culture of inverting society – the world turned upside down – for comedic relief. Similar examples include the practice of electing a boy as the bishop for the day in Durham or electing a “lord of misrule” during May Day games.

By 1555 the Swiss naturalist Conrad Gesner could write that “there are few countries wherein coneys (rabbits) do not breed, but the most plenty of all is in England”.

#4076. Prediction Markets Are Accurate

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

No surprise to me but a new paper looks at the forecast accuracy of over one million prediction questions from Polymarket, Kalshi, Manifold and the forecasting platform Metaculus. A virtue of the new analysis is that it covers many different areas from business to elections to geo-politics. An event predicted to happen p% of the time happens about p% of the time. Can’t beat that.

Using approximately one million resolved contracts and questions across four collective forecasting platforms, twelve domains, and time horizons ranging from hours to years, we evaluate the calibration and accuracy of collective forecasts in real-world settings. We found that forecast probabilities were overall highly calibrated: events assigned a probability of p% occurred approximately p% of the time. This broad pattern was evident across platforms and many domains, although fine-grained analyses revealed systematic tendencies toward over- or underestimation in highly consequential areas. While this does not mean that forecasting platforms provide certainty about individual events, our findings suggest that large-scale aggregation of judgments contains meaningful information about real-world uncertainty; when examined in aggregate, the predictions generated by the platforms provided informative estimates of event likelihood.

#4105. World’s largest wildlife crossing nears completion in California

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Published Oct 23, 2026 11:03 AM EDT

California is known for its giant, multi-laned highways bustling under bright blue skies. And while this infrastructure is useful to humans, the Golden State’s many highways represent an almost impenetrable barrier for wildlife.

Soon, habitats on either side of the 10-laned 101 Freeway in Agoura Hills, California will be reconnected thanks to the world’s largest wildlife crossing, according to the Los Angeles Times. The Wallis Annenberg Wildlife Crossing is scheduled for completion on December 2.

Wildlife crossings are structures that help animals safely cross roads. In the United States, collisions between motorists and large animals take place more than a million times a year. Experts estimate that this results in 400 human deaths every year. But the problem isn’t just road accidents. Habitat fragmentation is also a concern, especially for species that require a large range. Inbreeding can occur if a small enough group of animals becomes isolated.

In the Los Angeles area, mountain lions (Puma concolor), also known as pumas or cougars, are currently the species most vulnerable to genetic isolation caused by roads and development, according to the project’s website. And the 101 Freeway appears to be the most important obstacle, out of all the roads in the area, to the region’s ecological health.

As such, the Wallis Annenberg Wildlife Crossing aims to reconnect local plants and animals in the ecosystem of the Santa Monica Mountains.  It’s about 210 feet long and about 170 to 174 feet wide. A thick, sturdy-looking vegetation-covered bridge already straddles the busy freeway—but the weather might throw a wrench in the project’s schedule.

“We’re just hoping El Niño‘s on our side,” Beth Pratt, president of the Wildlife Crossing Fund, tells the Los Angeles Times, in reference to the climate pattern that typically brings wetter winter weather along the United States’ southern border. Flooding would put work on pause, according to the outlet. “I keep saying, ‘Mother Nature, we’re trying to help you. Let’s not throw any more complications our way.’”
