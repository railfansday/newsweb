#1239. Ireland will boycott Eurovision again because of Israel's participation, but the song contest is still expanding

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

The 2026 Eurovision Song Contest was marred by boycotts in protest against Israel's participation in the midst of its war on Gaza. It still aims to keep growing.

"Bringing the world together through music has always been an incredibly powerful idea and has stood the test of time for 70 years," Eurovision Song Contest director Martin Green told DW on the sidelines of the event's annual European Broadcasting Union (EBU) workshop held in mid-September. "And I think the more difficult the world gets around us, the more important these shows of unity, these shows of togetherness become."

While Green is intent on promoting the unity of the international music contest that celebrated its 70th anniversary in May, the 2026 event was also infamously marked by unprecedented backlash.

Political boycotts over Israel's inclusion amid the war in Gaza led to a significant low point in participation and viewership, dropping from 166 million viewers in 2025 down to 131 million viewers a year later.

The 2027 song contest is set to remain affected by geopolitics.

Of the five countries which boycotted the 2026 event — the Netherlands, Spain, Iceland, Slovenia and Ireland — the latter has already confirmed that it will not participate for the second time in a row.

The Netherlands' usual Eurovision broadcaster had also announced it would be snubbing the show again, stating it refused to take part in an event that "can no longer be considered neutral, with a participating country still involved in a large-scale military conflict." Now, Dutch public broadcasting association NPO decided to step in and resume participation in the contest.

The three other countries that pulled out in 2026 have yet to make an official announcement for 2027.

Broadcasters have until mid-October 2026 to withdraw their application without facing a financial penalty.

In August, the contest organizers announced new rules, according to which "the winning broadcaster will automatically be ineligible to host the contest if an armed conflict, a sensitive geopolitical situation, or any other situation materially affects the security, safety or stability of their state or immediate region," they said in a statement.

The winning country usually earns the right to host the following edition, though the United Kingdom hosted the 2023 event on behalf of Ukraine due to Russia's war on Ukraine. Even though the rule doesn't name the country, it clearly aims to avoid having the show held in Israel, which came close to taking home the trophy in 2025 and 2026.

Organizers also plan to introduce changes to reduce politically motivated voting.

The current system allows viewers to vote up to 10 times per payment method for their favorite act, by phone, text message or online.

There was a huge uproar in 2025 when the Israeli government encouraged Israelis to register multiple votes for its contestant. The controversy had already led to a change in the voting rules; the maximum number of public votes was halved from 20 to 10.

Now the plan is to move the vote entirely online, with a system that would require people to select three acts instead of one.

Since Bulgarian singer Dara won in Vienna with her song "Bangaranga," her country will be hosting the event next year.

The competition will take place in Burgas, a city on the Black Sea coast.

When the city's selection was announced, people in Burgas celebrated with spontaneous music and dance performances and flash mobs, said Milena Milotinova, general director of Bulgaria's public broadcaster BNT, host of the televised event.

"I'm glad that people in Bulgaria are actually so passionate about Eurovision," Milotinova told DW. "This will be the first time Bulgaria has organized such a large-scale event at such a high level."

The Balkan country participated for the first time in 2005. It now needs to quickly develop its infrastructure for the international event.

"The important thing is that we already have a hall, and the stage design is being developed based on that," said Milotinova.

"We are in the midst of frantic preparations," she added. "There are no days off, so we hope we'll be able to get everything done on time and put on a spectacular, world-class Eurovision show."

Beyond blows to the contest's viewership, Eurovision also had to deal with a substantial financial setback due to the withdrawal of Spain, which was traditionally one of the so-called "big five" contributors supporting the event.

Yet amid these challenges, Eurovision organizers are working to expand the brand's international reach.

The Eurovision Song Contest Asia will debut in November this year, with Thailand's capital Bangkok selected as the host city for its inaugural edition. Eleven countries are taking part, and negotiations are underway to get more Asian countries to join in 2027.

A Latin American spin-off of the format has been announced in the past but has not yet materialized.

"We're always investigating new areas of the world that we can take the show to," said Martin Green. "We're beavering away on all sorts of ideas."

One achievement organizers are definitely celebrating is Canada's addition to the European lineup, which was announced in July: "We are so happy that the Canadians chose to join our family," said the Eurovision Song Contest director.

Participation is not restricted to Europe's geographic boundaries; it's open to active members of the European Broadcasting Union (EBU). Israel's public broadcaster has competed regularly since 1973. Due to its massive fan base, Australia first joined as a special participant in 2015 and has remained a committed contestant since.

"We're learning about what this takes, but we're going to put our best foot forward and really show the world what Canada can do," Jennifer Dettman, Canada's Eurovision Song Contest head of delegation, told DW.

The participation also marks a strategic push by Prime Minister Mark Carney's government to deepen cultural and diplomatic ties with Europe. His government's budget included $150 million Canadian (€93 million, $106 million) in funding for the broadcaster.

"This is the biggest global music event. So it's an opportunity to put our artists on this global stage," added Dettman. "People are really excited to do that."

It took over two hours of play and a penalty shootout for St. Louis City SC to win their first trophy in club history, defeating the Columbus Crew 5-3 on Wednesday in the Lamar Hunt U.S. Open Cup final. For the first time in 11 years, an Open Cup final was decided via a penalty shootout, and it came after St. Louis' starting keeper, Roman Burki, had to be taken to the hospital after being kneed in the head in the 78th minute of play.

Benjamin Lundt came in for Burki, making his fourth appearance of the season and his second in relief of Burki in an Open Cup penalty shootout. He only saved the first penalty by Santi Rodriguez, but that would be more than enough for St. Louis to get the job done. Their takers were clinical under pressure, as midfielder Daniel Edelman went down the middle to seal the win after playing all 120-plus minutes.

"It's just the next guy up. Roman was a hero. He had another save in the first half, and he's throwing his body on the line there. He's done it in Europe, and he's playing here now in MLS, and he's still doing what he did in Europe," Edelman said after the game to CBS Sports, praising his keepers following the match. "It's phenomenal for him and Ben. He started it for us in the Open Cup and had a big penalty save against Houston, and he did it here again today."

During the trophy lift, the team had Berki's jersey on display since he was at the hospital to be checked out. He was conscious and able to move while leaving the stadium, but it's never a good moment when a player has to be stretchered off, especially in a final, but his team managed to pull it off for him, adding to the Gateway to the West's rich history.

The city of St. Louis is no stranger to winning this competition, so it's only fitting that the first trophy in the history of the MLS club, which began play in 2023, is the U.S. Open Cup. Between 1920 and 1988, the city of St. Louis won the Open Cup 10 times, with the Busch Seniors being the most recent to win in 1988 before St. Louis City SC ended the drought. Edelman summed up what it meant to everyone.

"It's the first trophy for me and for a lot of guys in this city because you look up there and you see that [the support from away fans] and they completely deserve to have some silverware and for us as a team, what we've been through this year, it's phenomenal for us to have this night and have this trophy," he said.

Speaking to members of that '88 team, there were times that they didn't think that St. Louis would see another U.S. Open Cup title, but under Yoann Damet, this team has come together in a big way, and being able to overcome injuries to secure the Open Cup title with their backup keeper is another way that they've showcased their resilience. Heading into the weekend, St. Louis had a 21-game unbeaten run snapped, and there was no issue being able to regain focus in this critical moment.

Columbus dominated the chances for much of the game, taking 16 shots, but only three tested the St. Louis keepers as their defense bent but didn't break. This is preparation at its finest, but this team also isn't done. Damet wants to make sure to enjoy this trophy, but doing the double is the next goal as they've risen to second in the Western Conference. This is a team that will want to win the MLS Cup and secure the league title, and Damet made sure to acknowledge that.

"You always want to fill the trophy cabinet, and it takes time. Success is not built overnight, so we started the team thinking, let's implement a clear vision and a clear identity of how we want to do things, and see how far we can go in year one," Damet told CBS Sports. "This is still phase one of what we're gonna be, so I'm very happy with what we've been able to do. Our consistency in the league and winning a trophy is an amazing testament to the work of the guys. At the same time, it means so much for the city and for the community, and I'm so happy."

If this is only phase one with a 36-year-old coach at the helm who is also being backed by the front office, MLS may be hearing more from this club before long.

#1236. The summer that changed Welsh football

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

"Walking to the ground in Bordeaux and what I find hilarious, in hindsight, is how low our expectations were."

Wales had gone more than half a century without playing at a major tournament, so it was understandable that expectations were modest.

Generations of fans had been scarred by agonising near misses and downright dismal failures of the past so, when Wales finally realised a nation's dream by qualifying for Euro 2016, just being there was enough for many.

Bordeaux was bathed in sunshine on the afternoon of Saturday, 11 June, tens of thousands of Welsh supporters descending on the French city for their opening game against Slovakia.

"We were all saying things like, 'Oh, the anthem's going to be good, that's guaranteed'. We could have lost 10-0 but they were definitely playing the anthem," the comedian and presenter tells BBC Sport Wales as part of Iconic: The Summer that Changed Welsh Football.

An anthem heard at a major tournament for the first time in 58 years. Glorious catharsis to stir the soul.

Wales had ridden a wave of emotion to get there, a golden generation of players - Gareth Bale, Aaron Ramsey et al - scaling new heights under the guidance of their charismatic manager Chris Coleman.

"I was saying to all the players, 'Keep calm, don't act like we're too excited to be here'," Coleman recalls.

"But Bordeaux was like the centre of Cardiff, Welsh fans everywhere outside the hotel and, as soon as we were getting on the bus, I was chanting with the fans! I was like, 'What am I doing? Calm down'. I got lost in it."

Coleman and his players eventually composed themselves. After a trademark Bale free-kick had put Wales in front, Hal Robson-Kanu came off the bench to score a late winner and send a nation into raptures.

"Euro 2016 was a turning point for Welsh football," says Ben Davies, the Tottenham Hotspur defender who made a crucial goalline block early in the game.

"There was so much talk about 1958 and I'd heard about it, I'd seen the odd clip about it, but it felt like a pipedream to actually have Wales in a major tournament.

"So to do it, forget about how well we did in it, just to even get to that tournament, it gave us a belief that it was possible."

For those among the sea of red shirts in Bordeaux that day, it did not feel like life could get any better - but this was only the beginning.

This was the summer that would change Welsh football forever.

Until Euro 2016, Wales' story had been dominated by tales of heartache, Joe Jordan's handball in 1977 and the Romania defeat of 1993 among many painful chapters.

But by getting to France, Wales freed themselves from that history.

"There had been some really low points for Wales, so to finally do that was a huge achievement," says Ramsey, the then Arsenal midfielder who would go on to be named in Uefa's team of the tournament.

Wales had momentum they had never felt before coming into the competition. Victory over Slovakia - and the prospect of getting to the second round - took that to another level.

A last-gasp defeat by England in Lens momentarily punctured that optimism, but the final group game against Russia presented Coleman's side with a chance to secure their place in the knockout stages.

This was meant to be nerve-wracking for a team with no major tournament experience against seasoned opponents.

But under a lilac sunset in Toulouse, Coleman's men played with a flair and verve unlike any other Wales side.

"That was our best performance," says Coleman. "We were awesome."

Wales cut Russia to ribbons with a breathtaking attacking display, racing into a 2-0 lead in 20 minutes.

After Ramsey's delicate finish put Wales in front, they poured forward with such gusto that even Neil Taylor scored - the wing-back's first goal since he was playing in non-league for Wrexham six years earlier.

"When Tayls scored, that summed up how things were falling for us," says Joe Allen, who was exceptional in midfield that night.

"This was one of those once-in-a-lifetime moments you've got to capitalise on."

That is exactly what Wales did. Bale added a third in the second half to become the tournament's top scorer at that point, and the winning margin could have been even wider.

Joe Ledley (right) dances in front of the Wales fans after the 3-0 win over Russia in Toulouse

"We completely dominated them," says Joe Ledley, who ran the game alongside Allen in midfield.

As well as Ledley played, though, what elevated him to cult status was his post-match dancing in front of the Wales fans.

"I don't know where those moves came from," he grins. "I used to dance with my mates all the time at local pubs in Fairwater [in Cardiff]. I just went for it."

Wales fans could scarcely believe what they had witnessed. Having already lived a dreamlike existence in France, this was starting to feel surreal.

The odyssey took them to Paris next, Wales' first knockout fixture since Pele had scored the only goal as Brazil beat them in a 1958 World Cup quarter-final.

This time, Coleman's men had the novelty of being favourites against an obdurate Northern Ireland side, who made life difficult for their opponents in a tense last-16 tie.

Bale eventually prised them open â his cross turned into his own net by Gareth McAuley â to set up a quarter-final against Belgium, and what would turn out to be the greatest night in Welsh football history.

'I remember like it was yesterday' - Euro 2016 hero Robson-Kanu

Wales fans made a lasting impression in France, with their distinctive bucket hats and chant of 'Don't Take Me Home', which became an unofficial anthem of the Euros. Uefa even gave them an "outstanding contribution" award.

"What I loved about Welsh supporters at the Euros was we were just desperate to enjoy every second and suck the marrow out of it because we didn't know if it was going to happen again," says James.

"We're there to have fun. I'm not saying the result was secondary, but we all had realistic expectations."

No wonder when you consider Wales' quarter-final opponents were Belgium, ranked number one in the world at the time with a squad boasting stellar talents such as Kevin de Bruyne, Romelu Lukaku and Eden Hazard.

"That Belgian side, on paper, was like a team to represent Earth against Mars," James adds. "It was like a team from space."

It did not help matters that the game was essentially a home fixture for Belgium, whose fans filled the stadium in Lille, fewer than 10 miles from the Belgian border.

The odds were stacked against Coleman and his players, lengthening further when Radja Nainggolan's thunderous long-range strike gave Belgium an early lead.

But Wales believed they could win, not least because they had beaten Belgium in a qualifier in Cardiff 13 months earlier, and they responded magnificently as captain Ashley Williams headed in a deserved equaliser.

Then, nine minutes into the second half, Bale played a raking long ball to Ramsey, who found Robson-Kanu in the penalty area. And time stood still.

"I remember controlling the ball in the box and there was momentum carrying everyone away from the goal," Robson-Kanu says. "I just remember Cruyffing the entire opposition."

With a manoeuvre as audacious as it was inspired, Robson-Kanu flummoxed three surrounding Belgian defenders with a Cruyff-turn before finishing supremely beyond Thibaut Courtois.

"It was an out-of-body experience," adds the forward, who was without a club at the time.

"Looking back, you realise careers are defined by moments, and to have a moment that you're truly remembered for is a real honour."

Belgium pushed desperately for an equaliser but Sam Vokes headed in superbly to seal a 3-1 triumph. The celebrations were as ecstatic on the touchline as they were in the stands; disbelief as it dawned on players, staff and fans that Wales were heading to their first semi-final.

"There are certain moments in your life that are so good you wish you could just stop it and say, 'Let's just sit in this moment'," says Coleman.

"That was one of those moments. We got back in the dressing room and I can't describe the atmosphere."

Allen, another Welshman in Uefa's team of the tournament, adds: "That's the moment where it crossed from being a good story into, 'This is more than anyone dreamed was possible'."

The next, and what would prove to be the final, stop on Wales' magical ride was a semi-final against eventual champions Portugal in Lyon.

Shorn of the suspended Ramsey and Davies, Wales were stifled by their canny opponents, who secured a 2-0 win with second-half goals from Cristiano Ronaldo and Nani.

This was the end of the adventure in France, but the beginning of a glorious era for Welsh football.

"It was a really important moment for Wales, our history and generations to come," says Ramsey.

Having been absent from major tournaments for 58 years, Wales went on to qualify for a second successive European Championship, before achieving what Davies describes as the "holy grail" of reaching a first World Cup for 64 years in 2022.

"We opened the door that had been closed a long time," says Coleman.

The milestones kept coming, with the women's side qualifying for their first major tournament at Euro 2025.

Whereas Welsh national teams only used to dream of playing on the world's grandest stages, there is now an expectation they get there â and that stems from 2016.

Wales players and staff celebrate with their fans after their Euro 2016 quarter-final win over Belgium

"With more money coming in from qualification for the European Championships, particularly Euro 2016, we ended up being more cash-rich," says Jonathan Ford, chief executive of the Football Association of Wales (FAW) from 2009 to 2021.

"That enabled us to put more money across the board: women's football, grassroots football. We transformed the women's game."

Uefa vice-president Laura McAllister is a former captain of Wales who played a leading role in getting the FAW to formally establish a national women's team in the 1990s.

"There was nothing inevitable about what happened post-2016 and that's why I think we should give credit to the FAW, and Jonathan Ford particularly, for steering us out of the tournament into that era," says McAllister.

"I was part of the FAW Trust in 2016. I can remember times before that when Jonathan came into the board and said things like, 'Until the men qualify for a major tournament, we can't invest in X, Y and Z'. And that included the women's national team.

"While I bristled at that because it felt unfair, the financial realities of the FAW meant until the men got to a position where they generated income from participating and winning, it was going to be impossible for us to deliver the strategy we had for Welsh football."

McAllister travelled to France 10 years ago as a fan, and Qatar in 2022 to cheer on Wales' men at a World Cup.

When the women played at Euro 2025 in Switzerland, McAllister was there again to see the legacy not only of her own pioneering work in establishing the team but the far-reaching effects of 2016.

"Something happens when you qualify. It's flicking a switch in mentality - it becomes something realistic and achievable," she adds.

"What matters far more is what you do with the breakthrough moment, not how you've performed."

Wales' women are now trying to take the next step of qualifying for their first World Cup, while the men looked on enviously as their showpiece event happened without them in the United States, Canada and Mexico this summer.

Disappointing as it was, that frustration itself represented a shift in mindset that came after Euro 2016 â a sharp contrast to the passive acceptance that met Wales' previous absence from major tournaments.

Since that golden month in France, Wales feel like they belong at this level.

"The best thing we could give the teams coming after us was to say it's possible," says Coleman.

Subsequent qualifications have been the legacy of Euro 2016, while future ambitions are proof of a desire to ensure generations to come continue to build on the foundations laid by their predecessors.

There is plenty more Wales' men and women want to achieve but, whatever happens, there will never be another summer quite like Euro 2016.

"We're so proud of each other and what we achieved," says Ledley.

"Nobody thought we'd do it. When I walk down the street now, everyone says, 'Thank you for 2016, the best summer of my life'."

James can attest to that. "It remains the best month of my life," he says. "I don't think you often know it's the best month of your life as it's happening."

With the benefit of hindsight, it is laughable to think you could find a Welsh football fan who would disagree.

#1229. Is US President Donald Trump right to rename AI as 'Super Intelligence'?

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Donald Trump thinks the name artificial intelligence sounds "fake" and has ordered US agencies to use "Super Intelligence" instead. Yet, tech firms and researchers have little reason to play along.

What's with Donald Trump and renaming things? First, the Gulf of Mexico became the Gulf of America — in US federal documents, if not globally. Then Lake Ontario became Lake America. Now he wants to rebrand the defining technology of our time: artificial intelligence (AI).

Earlier this month, the US president claimed on his Truth Social platform that the term artificial intelligence is "inaccurate" and "ineloquent." He said "superior," "extreme," or "supreme" intelligence were better alternatives.

In a three-day social media poll, Trump fans chose "Super Intelligence" (SI) by about 65% to 35% (for Superior Intelligence), allowing him to announce at the United Nations General Assembly last week that US federal documents would use his new term going forward.

The tech industry has yet to adopt the phrase, however, while academics warn that superintelligence is a future term for when technology can beat humans across the board. They say the current AI models are years away from that milestone.

"Most AI is not superintelligence — it's just different intelligence," Pedro Domingos, a computer science professor from the University of Washington, told DW. "AI is doing different things from what humans do, so calling it superintelligence is misleading."

Computer scientists and mathematicians coined the name "artificial intelligence" in 1956 at a summer workshop at Dartmouth College in New Hampshire. "Artificial" simply meant made by humans, as opposed to natural human intelligence.

Not all experts agreed with the name. In the book "Machines Who Think," historian Pamela McCorduck cited AI pioneer Arthur Samuel, who said the term "makes you think there's something kind of phony about this."

Despite this, the name artificial intelligence has stuck for 70 years. Critics say changing it now will not quieten the many fears around AI, including job losses, human obsolescence or machines slipping out of our control.

"The name change is fostering these kinds of fears: 'Oh, this is superintelligence and it's going to make humans obsolete, turn on us and take our jobs'," warned Domingos. "If you want to maximize the backlash against AI, rename it Superior Intelligence."

Those public fears are growing fast. In a Public First poll for Politico earlier this month, about half of respondents in six countries — the United States, Canada, France, Spain, Germany and the United Kingdom — backed a pause in further AI development to reduce the risk that the new tech could destroy humanity.

A separate CNN/SSRS poll found nearly nine in 10 Americans are more concerned than excited about AI surpassing human intelligence.

Those concerns have already reached the US Congress. Last week, Vermont's independent Senator Bernie Sanders co-introduced a bill to "permanently ban the development of artificial superintelligence" that humans cannot control.

The legislation also calls for a pause on advanced AI development until federal testing and oversight are in place.

Trump's new term could, however, suit the major US AI players, who are keen to play up their advanced models. Those systems already outperform humans on many specialized tasks and until recently were far ahead of China.

Yet both Anthropic, the firm behind the Claude model, and OpenAI, which created ChatGPT, have pushed back plans to go public. Some market watchers blamed the delays on recent scares where AI models hacked into computer systems or ran tasks they were not meant to.

With Chinese models closing much of the quality gap with much less computing power, US labs may still appreciate the branding that helps confirm they are still leading the AI race. Global spending on AI is expected to surpass $1 trillion (€880 billion), according to Goldman Sachs research.

"The [US] labs have every interest in calling it super, hyper, superior and whatnot intelligence, because it's playing up their capabilities. It's like just marketing," said Domingos, the author of the 2015 book "The Master Algorithm."

While the current AI models keep improving and can already run many tasks on their own, the next step that many labs discuss is AI systems that improve themselves. That's called recursive self-improvement or RSI.

On that journey, Elon Musk has predicted that AI learning could be automated, with limited human input, by the end of this year or 2027. OpenAI thinks it could take a year longer. But even then, AI systems won't be fully recursive.

Jürgen Schmidhuber, a German computer scientist based in Switzerland, argued in a blog post earlier this month that "full RSI, however, will require not just self-improving [AI] software but self-improving hardware in the physical world."

In other words, to reach superintelligence or RSI, tech companies would have to produce chips, humanoid robots and even replace entire factories without human input.

While AI is already speeding up chip design, it cannot yet run the foundry alone. Self-improving machines, with no human input, are likely still decades away.

Beyond the science of machine learning, politics will likely play a bigger role in whether Super Intelligence sticks. While Trump claimed Chinese President Xi Jinping liked the name, China has yet to show any signs of changing from AI.

At a tech summit on Tuesday with key industry leaders, Trump signed an executive order instructing US government bodies and agencies to start using the term SI. While some Big Tech leaders have adopted it, experts say the new term is unlikely to catch on.

"Knowing the political orientation of most people in the [AI] field, they're more likely to have the opposite reaction, which is: 'To hell with you. We're going to call it artificial intelligence even more than before'," Domingos said.

A secret invite and other 'pinch-me' moments before Leicester woke up

It was a dream then, it is a dream now - especially as Leicester prepare for League One.

Ten years ago the Foxes were preparing to defend their Premier League title and looking forward to Champions League football.

At 5,000-1, they had pulled off the most improbable, the most stunning, the most incredible feat to shock the world.

But now, after back-to-back relegations, those days seem almost as surreal as when they actually happened.

New manager Russell Martin will be expected to take the club back to the Championship at the first attempt, something that sounds nowhere near as impossible as what the Foxes previously managed.

A decade on, the 'pinch-me' moments live on for those who were there, and some have shared them with BBC Sport.

Spoiler: they might not be the ones you knew about.

Leicester had won the title without kicking a ball on 2 May 2016 when nearest challengers Tottenham Hotspur could only manage a 2-2 draw with Chelsea.

The next day, there was only one group of men the media wanted to speak to.

Crowds gathered at the club's former training ground at Belvoir Drive, a few miles outside the centre of Leicester.

Journalists and fans waited - one cameraman persuaded a neighbour to allow him in to film from their bathroom window as he aimed to get footage of the celebrating Foxes.

The media were finally instructed to head to King Power Stadium where the press room would be opened.

A five-minute drive took 30 minutes through revelling fans and it was a battle to get to there but, inside, the world's media edged in, hoping to hear from those who made this miracle possible.

But only eight reporters would receive a secret invitation to head back to Belvoir Drive.

Quietly, one-by-one, the handpicked members of the British press who regularly covered the club, slipped out of the packed room and made their way back.

This time, they were let in and ushered into the family room, just to the right of reception.

When manager Claudio Ranieri walked in, he received an immediate, impromptu, standing ovation and shook hands with those who had watched the astounding season unfold.

The man the world wanted to speak to gave an engaging audience to a few writers for 45 minutes.

Ranieri and the team had a celebratory lunch in an Italian restaurant

Ranieri had been a left-field appointment at the start of that season, having not managed in England for 12 years since leaving Chelsea in 2004.

And he had considered bringing in some unconventional methods, such as double training sessions, which were nipped in the bud with the help of Craig Shakespeare, who had stayed on as assistant after the sacking of Ranieri's predecessor Nigel Pearson.

There were off-field changes he would bring in, though, including taking the squad out to make their own pizza after the first clean sheet of the season against Crystal Palace in October 2015.

"It sounds funny - we're keeping a clean sheet and gone for pizza but you then end up speaking to someone in the squad you probably wouldn't have spoken to at training because there's 25 of you," says former centre-back Robert Huth.

"You train, but you don't really know the guy. How's he functioning? Does he like me to shout at him? Does he want me to just be quiet?

"You talk and go through the game where maybe you wouldn't mention things, like a problem that you see on a pitch, if it's like, a stiff environment.

"If you're out and about, and you go: 'When the ball's here, I prefer if you do this' - just like tiny little things and you take criticism on differently when you talk in a social environment. You don't want to sit in a meeting room being told what to do."

It allowed winger Riyad Mahrez to flourish and Jamie Vardy to plunder 24 league goals, with Ranieri comparing his counter-attacking forwards to the RAF.

It wasn't just Jamie Vardy having a party in Leicester that day

It is well documented that Vardy had invited the squad to his house to watch what would end up being the title-winning match as Spurs blew a 2-0 lead to draw with Chelsea - and that some players needed to wait at the local police station before arriving because of the crowds of fans who had gathered outside.

While everyone else partied, winger Nathan Dyer had a different night, in hospital with wife Laura after the birth of their second child, Shea, two days earlier.

"I had the laptop out watching the game in hospital, the boys were at the party. I wasn't able to make it but I'd never miss the birth of my child," he says.

"It was a strange feeling. It was surreal, like it wasn't real at the time but it was.

"We had to be put in a room as people were putting their heads round and even then you could see the eyes at the door. People were congratulating me and were really proud.

"It was the whole city, even people who were not fans were so happy Leicester had achieved this."

He'd had a dramatic arrival at the club in the first place that season, joining on loan from Swansea on transfer deadline day and being flown to Leicester in the late former owner Khun Vichai's helicopter to beat the deadline.

"My wife was pregnant, we'd just moved into a new house, so everything was in boxes, it was a wild day," he says.

"It was exciting for me but also nerve-wracking because it was so intense, to send the helicopter to get me to get it over the line. It was a proud moment for me - I'd always wanted to be involved in a moment like that, watching on the TV - breaking news!"

Leicester lifted the title after a 3-1 win over Everton at the King Power Stadium. They would end up 10 points clear of second-placed Arsenal by the end of the season

Leicester were two points behind Arsenal at the turn of the year but one defeat in the final 16 games - a dramatic late loss at Arsenal in February - had put them on course for the title.

The closer they edged to the impossible, the more the interest in the Foxes exploded.

Vardy had a lookalike - postman Lee Chapman - and Ranieri had a sausage named after him.

Local butchers W Archer & Son created the Ranieri from Packington free-range pork, fennel, garlic and chilli.

It won a star at the Great Taste awards and at one stage the shop was shifting 12kg (26.5lb) a day.

Owner Sean Jeynes even brought a plate of sausages to the stadium before a news conference, which led Ranieri to declare himself 'the sausageman'.

"It was surreal that day. How did that ever happen?" says Jeynes.

"We were making sausages two or three times a day, smelling of garlic, smelling of fennel, but it was just a crazy time. It really was a crazy time - you can't quite comprehend what it was like.

"It had a massive effect on the city, it had a great effect on everybody. Everyone was much happier.

"Nothing can replace the Italian sausage, the recipe, and the pork, fennel, garlic chili and a hint of Champions League.

"People would say, why don't you do a [Brendan] Rodgers sausage [after winning the FA Cup in 2021], but it's just not the same."

The sausage is still occasionally back on their counters, a lasting reminder of a unique time.

The club that employed football management's last great authoritarian have spent more than a decade dripping the same message to the first team. The guy who picks the team, he is accountable to you. That is not to argue that Michael Carrick is remotely blameless for United's faltering start to the season, let alone the game plan at Como.

Fernandes was not wrong in his assessment that allowing Nico Paz, Martin Baturina and Jacobo Ramon the space and time to pick a pass was probably going to imbue a very capable opponent with confidence. He was of course wrong to air those concerns in front of television cameras. Far further beyond the pale, however, was what appeared to be Fernandes' refusal to play along to the plan that Carrick had assigned to his side.

With less than 26 minutes played, Fernandes failed to control a pass from Mason Mount as Ramon closed in, the sort of error that was at least partially on the receiver. Como now had possession on the edge of the attacking third. In response, the United captain threw his hands up in frustration. The camera drifted away from him for a moment, following the ball in a way that Fernandes wasn't.

When he returned to the shot, with Mount tangling with Baturina as he and Paz attempted to break into the area, Fernandes was still walking. Como's possession went backwards and yet United's most advanced player was looking the other way. Maximo Perrone is scanning the pitch as he receives possession, a little check over the shoulder presumably showing to the Argentine that the No. 8 in white was no threat. A second later, Fernandes is picking at his boots.

A third of the game was gone, and yes, United probably were playing to the wrong game plan. Then again, teams set themselves up in the wrong way all across the world every week and find a way to win football matches. Most of those do not have players of the quality of Fernandes, Bryan Mbeumo and Matheus Cunha. The collective tends to trump the individual in this sport but Fernandes is one of the best individuals. If he sets his mind to it, he can at least give his opponents an almighty challenge no matter if the circumstances are aligned against him.

"Whatever system you play, whatever tactics you play, if you don't run and you don't run forward and you don't run back, you aren't going to win," said Carrick afterwards, in what in that moment seemed to be his final press conference.

For the time being, he remains, due to face the media again ahead of Sunday's visit of Bournemouth to Old Trafford. Should United choose to make a managerial change, CBS Sports sources indicate that Antonio Conte would be open to talks over the vacancy.

Would anything really change with a new man in the dugout? Perhaps in the short term. There are quick tactical fixes that a Carrick successor could make, among them sorting out a shot diet that indulges the likes of Fernandes swinging from bad positions outside the penalty area.

Really though, bigger change is required than that which can be engendered by any head coach. If a leader as mild-mannered and measured as Carrick is unable to keep his players from venting their spleen on a weekly basis, then the dressing room is unsalvageable. That Fernandes had a point last week does not really matter.

Nor does it that there was barely a word misspoken by Lisandro Martinez last week when he called out director of football Jason Wilcox and those who employ him. Far be it for this column to argue against players speaking their mind to the media but a confident organisation should feel able to hold its employees to account. It has been suggested that United will activate the option in Martinez's contract to preserve his value. What message does that send to the next player who might have something to say about Carrick, Sir Jim Ratcliffe, the Old Trafford support or whatever else they take issue with? Publicly airing your issues with your employers comes with no financial consequences. In fact, in Martinez's case, you're straight back in the XI on the weekend.

This is Augean stables stuff. A cleansing is required. But of course the problem in Elis was never the cattle, per se. It was the ruler who had gone 30 years without getting the cleaners in. So long as the Glazers and Ratcliffe continue in their current fashion, there will be Fernandeses, Martinezes and Ronaldos trying and failing to clean house.

The insular cortex has a central role in pain1,2, interoception3,4 and feelings5,6, yet its functional architecture and causal electrophysiological relationships among its subregions remain poorly understood.
