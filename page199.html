#3606. The Jewish Case Against AI

📅 22 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Haredi rabbis and the secular left agree: This technology is a threat to the entire social fabric.

You really can’t outsource Torah study to the machines.

The episode has striking parallels with secular debates around AI. It’s worth thinking about why the discussion within a fervently traditionalist community played out so similarly to the one on the secular left. The Lakewood rabbis don’t consider generative AI destructive simply because it is new or because it could easily supply users with blasphemous content (though there is that). They also argue that interpreting the Torah is a fundamentally communal activity that requires ameilut, or toil, a philosophy espoused by foundational scholars like Rashi. They fear a future in which Torah study is fully automated and human intellectual exertion nullified. In this, they’re surprisingly aligned with leftists who oppose data centers and big tech. “They might come up with different sources for explaining it,” anthropologist Ayala Fader told The Forward, “but they are actually articulating some of the objections to AI that you can read about in The Chronicle of Higher Education.”

There are, of course, more prosaic concerns. Haredim, like other groups that enforce strict religious or cultural observances, have long argued about when and how to accommodate the demands of the contemporary world, and whether to adopt or shun new technologies. A decade ago, a debate raged among Hasidim (a subset of Haredim) about the Internet’s potential for sacrilege. Today, there are Hasidic TikTok influencers—although they abide by strictly enforced community standards. Many Orthodox Jews today see AI as a similar gateway to pornography or other impurities, and it remains to be seen whether they will accept it as inevitable and just try to put up some guardrails, or succeed in fully eradicating it. The Satmar Rebbe, the head of the Satmar Hasidic dynasty, put out an emergency broadcast this past May: “On every phone in the home, even on a kosher phone, one can connect to…impurity in a single moment,” he said. “There are many victims—young boys, girls, and even married yeshiva students. They are falling and descending directly into hell.” He called upon Satmar Hasidim to abstain from cell phones that preload applications like Gemini or OpenAI.

Anti-AI Haredim are not alone in considering the risks of this new technology to be existential. The threats it poses to labor are obvious, and yet there’s something about it that’s still more troubling, something that can be hard to put your finger on. The debates around AI often take on a strangely religious tone—after all, the most prominent leader to condemn its encroachment on humanity is Pope Leo. The Lakewood rabbis’ objections may help us better understand why both secular and religious AI refuseniks feel that something elemental is at stake. The Haredim who met in January argued that mitzvot—good deeds performed to fulfill a Torah commandment—cannot be replaced by machines. To eschew ameilut is to forgo the sacred work of Torah study. Outsourcing interpretation of scripture can be seen as an attack on the act of exegesis itself. In this way, AI threatens to undermine their way of life more thoroughly than previous technologies.

While it may be dangerous, AI is also increasingly omnipresent, complicating Orthodox communities’ attempts to rein it in. “You have to create rules that people will follow,” Forward staff reporter Louis Keene told me over the phone. “And if a rabbi is setting rules and nobody’s following them, it doesn’t matter how strict he is because nobody’s observing it.” Yet Keene acknowledges that many people intuit that there’s just something off about AI: “A lot of people are either nervous about it or repulsed by it. I don’t think people generally like AI art. Why do we see so much slop? We’re calling it slop. Everyone agrees.” For religious as well as secular communities, that slop presents a kind of spiritual affront.

There is a remarkably religious flavor to the way even atheists and technocrats talk about AI. Advocates frequently refer to it as a god in the making. “If we ever get AI that is strong enough to basically be God and solve all of our problems, it will need to use the same techniques that the actual God uses in terms of maintaining some distance,” rationalist and AI blogger Scott Alexander told Harper’s Magazine earlier this year. I do think it’s possible that the AI will be like, Now I am God. I’ve concluded that the actual God made exactly the right decision on how much evil to permit in the universe. Therefore I refuse to change anything.” Why are self-styled rationalists leaning so hard into such an esoteric framing?

“[T]here comes a time in every ai policy professional’s life when they realize they have to read the talmud to make further progress,” Dean W. Ball, OpenAI’s head of strategic futures, posted this summer. While that may seem like a completely out-of-left-field remark to anyone not versed in Silicon Valley lore, it sparked extensive conversations among AI researchers. The thinking goes that the Talmud offers a model of interpretation and debate that could teach AI the nature of ethics. Researchers often refer to the “AI alignment problem,” which is the difficulty that human designers face in making sure an AI system’s behavior matches the goals and values humans actually want. This is both a practical and philosophical problem, so loaded that it lends itself more easily to the domain of theology than corporate governance. Judd Rosenblatt, founder of the AI consulting company AE Studios and the AI ethics nonprofit AI Alignment Foundation, told The Jewish Forward that he looks to the Talmud for inspiration as he considers the AI alignment problem, believing its tradition of evolving arguments and adaptations could help us design AI systems that can improve exponentially without going rogue. “I think a tradition that reinterprets nothing is the more fragile one,” he said. “A rule that cannot be bent, cannot adapt to a new world and dies out.” Besides, he argues, Judaism has survived multiple implosions, including the destruction of the temple.

Not all Haredi agree that AI is a modern-day Tower of Babel, tempting the wrath of God with our hubris. Users on imamother.com, an online forum for Orthodox women, discuss their use of AI with a mix of shame and excitement, with some saying it motivates them to see doctors or go to the dentist. As one poster asked, “Am I a horrible person for not listening to das torah [a rabbinic pronouncement]? I can’t believe that HaShem would rather I be completely dysfunctional than use this tool.”

Other Orthodox Jews are embracing these tools wholeheartedly, particularly those identified with Modern Orthodoxy, a broad movement that seeks to reconcile Jewish observance with modern life. Orthodox Union, a large US-based nonprofit, is developing AI-powered Torah Study apps that collate large amounts of revered texts for both professional scholars and curious laypeople. Orthodox Union’s Rabbi Simon Taylor discussed the organization’s reasoning behind developing an app: “We have to figure out how to leverage AI to teach more Torah and to spread Judaism. We knew that we weren’t going to get it right the first time and it wasn’t going to be perfect. We worked very, very hard on safeguarding and guardrails for everything that we do as well, lots and lots of testing.” Rabbi Zohar Atkins has a new venture called Yochai, a chatbot that offers AI study support for the Torah and Talmud. The study tool takes its name from the second-century sage Shimon bar Yochai and its logo is a carob tree—the plant that, according to legend, the sage sustained himself on as he hid in a cave for 13 years to escape Roman persecution.

On the other hand, Rabbi Danya Ruttenberg recently delineated a Jewish case for being exempted from using AI in the workplace. She argues that AI is theft, as large language models like ChatGPT culled their data from countless sources without permission or compensation. Halakha, Jewish law, forbids Jews from benefiting from stolen goods. Similarly, claiming authorship for something one didn’t write oneself also a form of theft—something rabbis using AI to craft sermons may find alarming. She also points to the destruction that data centers are wreaking on the environment, and invokes the ethical principle of Bal taschit, which God reveals to Moses in Deuteronomy 20:19: Do not destroy.

Still, it’s hard to imagine that the Haredim who take a hard-line stance against AI will form a political coalition with secular leftists who share their fears about the technology’s existential risks. After all, Haredim who oppose Zionism on religious grounds and occasionally protest in support of Palestine have not exactly reached across the aisle to outsiders to forge an alliance. Nevertheless, the resemblance between secular and traditionalist Orthodox oppositions to AI is uncanny: Both insist that hard work is its own reward, and abhor the way AI destroys the capacity for human cognition. Ameilut, says The Forward’s Louis Keene, is a mitzvah. Toiling is part of the process. “I’ve found that ameilut-maxxing translates pretty well to non-religious life, too. It’s an imperative to embrace the challenge,” he writes. In other words, nothing worth doing is easy.

Technology is reshaping how the devout practice their beliefs, and it’s changing how everyone interacts with the world. AI garners devotees, and seemingly alters reality—this kind of thing was once the province of religion. For some, this development is value-neutral, and for others it poses a deep threat. The collision of tech and theology brings up a myriad of uncomfortable questions. If we are made in God’s image, and AI is made in our image, where does that leave us?

Rabbinical writings describe beings called the shedim: “In three ways they are like ministering angels, and in three ways they are like humans,” according to Hagigah 16a:5 in the Talmud. They even have their own mitzvot, which are opposite from our own. They are not quite good or evil, not quite human or spirit. “They eat and drink like humans; they multiply like humans; and they die like humans,” Hagigah 16a:1 says. Yet, they also fly to the ends of the earth like angels and know the future. Perhaps this is beginning to sound familiar. While some have compared AI to the man-made Golem, it’s the shedim that strike me as the more apt comparison.

The shedim, like AI, are morally ambiguous. They are both like humans and not, with their own sense of alignment that doesn’t necessarily conform to our own lens on morality. Like AI, they claim to know the future and fly through the air to the ends of the earth. They are shadowy figures between life and death who can easily shapeshift. Their capacity is intimately tied to our own, even if their ways are not our ways. “For my plans,” God tells Isaiah (55:8), “are not your plans.” While their reasoning, like God’s, may be mysterious to us—and they may even be developing their own languages to talk with each other—they are not made in the image of HaShem like we are. Even if the shedim are not exactly demons, they lead people astray. “They are called shedim because they destroy reason,” the distinguished medieval Jewish philosopher Ibn Ezra says in his commentary on Deuteronomy. While many use AI as a tool, it can also destroy the reasoning of men and women who turn to chatbots for help a machine cannot give. Some users have developed psychosis or committed suicide. The delusions that AI has egged on seem eerily reminiscent of the shedim—like a cracked mirror struggling to reflect what makes us human.

#3612. Vivek Ramaswamy’s New Attack Ad Should Be a National Scandal

📅 22 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

The Ohio GOP gubernatorial candidate has accused his Democratic opponent, Amy Acton, of lying about being raped when she was 12 years old.

Ohio Republican gubernatorial candidate Vivek Ramaswamy speaks during a rally with President Donald Trump in Youngstown, Ohio.

There are vile and amoral political actors who will stop at nothing to promote their venal interests—and then there’s Ohio GOP gubernatorial hopeful Vivek Ramaswamy. The biotech billionaire mounted a vanity 2024 presidential run to brand himself as a Gen Z mini-Trump and went on to codirect the nihilist DOGE assault on governance until Elon Musk’s more capacious ego muscled him aside. After both these self-branding efforts flatlined, Ramaswamy decided to emulate the career path of Vice President JD Vance, leveraging his sketchy venture-capitalist fortune into a run for statewide office in his native Ohio.

Ramaswamy has been in a tight and ugly contest with former state health commissioner Amy Acton that, against all odds, became exponentially more repulsive this week with the release of a digital ad featuring Acton’s estranged mother. At the age of 12, Acton and her younger brother had been removed from their mother’s home in 1978 after her stepfather, Cletis Arthur, was arrested and charged with the rape of his stepdaughter. Their biological father, Gerald Stearns, was granted custody of Acton and her brother; her mother, Donna, and her accused rapist then skipped out on bond. Acton told the Youngstown Vindicator that her last contact with her mom came in the courtroom where the custody case was held: “I went to give her a kiss, and she just turned her cheek away. That was the last time I ever saw her.”

Gleefully mining the trauma of an abused 12-year-old, Ramaswamy’s campaign cut an ad in which Donna Arthur proclaims, after 48 years of estrangement founded on her allegiance to her daughter’s abuser, that

I love my daughter, but when I saw her lying about her opponent—and it’s not easy for a mother to say this—but Amy has a serious problem. She is a liar. She lies about her past. She says she grew up living in a tent. None of that is true. She’s lied her entire life. My daughter needs help, and she can’t help you if she needs help herself.

When the ad surfaced as part of an online poll, it met with a proper wave of outrage and disbelief. A MAGA movement that’s long dismissed and excused sexual assault, up to and including President Donald Trump’s special pleading for the defendants in the Cornell gang-rape case, was now attacking the credibility of a 12-year-old rape victim. It’s a textbook illustration of the abusers’ defense tactic known as DARVO—deny, attack, and reverse victim and offender.

Only this sleazy instance of the practice was doubling as a political attack ad. Acton’s campaign responded with a statement from her brother, Philip Stearns, pointing out the obvious: “It’s not surprising that Vivek Ramaswamy has stooped to such a despicable low as to attempt to turn the sexual abuse Amy endured at twelve years old from our birth mother’s husband into a political talking point. After he was arrested and Amy and I were removed from their custody, our birth mother stayed married to Amy’s rapist until he died.” Acton’s campaign then released an ad in which her brother explained, “I experienced a lot of abuse, but Amy got the worst of it…. Shame on Vivek. It’s the contempt he has for Amy. It’s the contempt he has for Ohio. He’s not fit to lead.” Former GOP presidential candidate Nikki Haley and conservative pundit Meghan McCain echoed the same sentiment, and a host of other political figures have denounced the gutter attack.

But Ramaswamy, being Ramaswamy, is unfazed—indeed, after his campaign initially wouldn’t avow responsibility for the spot, it announced that it was spending $4 million to broadcast it during the homestretch of the race. (Ramaswamy’s campaign has so far spent more than $100 million, more than tripling outlays from Acton’s team.) His press flack, Connie Luck, continued playing up the smear, with a disingenuous disclaimer professing that “we don’t know what happened between Amy and her mother, who first spoke out about Amy in 2020,” and then parroting the mother’s attack: “We know that Amy Acton is a serial fabulist who has some serious problems with the truth that the public needs to be aware of before deciding who to entrust as governor.”

Ramaswamy has duplicated the same sleazy rhetorical two-step, telling USA Today that Acton’s mother “has spoken out for years,” adding that “Amy Acton has been repeatedly and serially dishonest during this campaign and for much of her record in public service.” (The charge of unhinged mendacity coming from Ramaswamy is, shall we say, a rich one, given that his campaign had unleashed an earlier attack ad featuring the testimony of an Ohio resident blaming Acton’s handling of the Covid crisis for her mother’s death—only for it later to emerge that the woman had actually lived, and died, in Kentucky.)

The fallout from the ad seems likely to backfire on Ramaswamy, who clearly possesses even worse political instincts than his Buckeye State mentor, the gilded hillbilly-whisperer Vance. (Indeed, in an Ohio campaign stop, the vice president reprised the anemic bad-faith defenses Ramaswamy and his flack offered for the spot, complaining in his trademark online-bro register of rudderless grievance that “the media and the Democrats in the state of Ohio are obsessed because Vivek Ramaswamy had the audacity to run a TV commercial which calls it out like it is and call Amy Acton a liar.”) Acton had already opened a seven-point lead in the race, according to the latest Marist poll, and it’s hard to see how Ramaswamy’s desperate smear job will bring independents, suburban women, and anyone still maintaining a shred of common human decency into the GOP camp. Any honest assessment of Ramaswamy’s prospects in the race’s final week and half should begin and end with the 2012 saga of Todd Aiken, the Missouri GOP Senate candidate brought down by his demented comments on how “legitimate rape” rarely produces pregnancies—and therefore its victims should be denied access to abortion.

But to judge by the media coverage thus far, there’s a good chance that the Ramaswamy rape ad will escape Midwest containment, and come to symbolize the depravity and corruption of today’s Republican Party in the minds of many voters. There is a striking precedent for such a development—the impact of Florida GOP Representative Mark Foley’s resignation from Congress just prior to the 2006 midterms. Foley, the longtime director of the House page program, had long been rumored to have abused that post for sexually predatory ends. After a series of damning messages proving out those charges in unmistakable intimate detail surfaced, Foley resigned from Congress on September 29.

The scandal crystallized many impressions of rampant GOP corruption then percolating through the mediasphere, from the crass cynicism and greed dramatized in the downfall of Republican superlobbyist Jack Abramoff to the epic mismanagement of George W. Bush’s war in Iraq. The Democrats had indeed sought to make Republican corruption a central issue in that midterm cycle—but until the Foley scandal broke, their pitch came across as too procedural and removed from the daily lives of most Americans to land effectively; it hinged mostly on Abramoff’s convoluted deals to represent Native American casino interests, and pay-to-play accords in the gilded precincts of the Beltway. But here was the Republican congressional leadership plainly covering up the actions of a sexual predator targeting idealistic young men under his direction. (The reasons for then–Speaker of the House Dennis Hastert’s dilatory handling of the Foley affair would later become painfully clear when he was also exposed as a serial sexual predator, who had exploited the high-school students in his charge when he was a high-school teacher and wrestling coach.)

It’s striking how many parallels this midterm cycle shares with 2006, from a Republican Party at a loss to distance itself from its president’s unpopular war in the Middle East, his mishandling of the economy, and his breathtaking record of corruption. But Ramaswamy’s blunder, like the earlier Foley debacle for the GOP, may well reinforce the party’s justly earned reputation as an enabler of sexual predation and an enthusiastic defender of the manifold abuses of women normalized by the online manosphere. Especially after the uprising over the horrific gang-rape at Cornell, which has led to the predictable doxing and defamation efforts from the online right, the bid to discredit a rape victim who was 12 years old when her predator was arrested looks pathological in the minds of fair-minded observers. It’s now up to Democratic leaders to make sure no one in the country is allowed to forget Vivek Ramaswamy’s moral depravity—or his ambition to be the Trump figure for the next generation of right-wing misogynists—between now and November 3.

#3413. Nuclear Weapons Waste Made Workers Sick. Despite a Law Meant to Help, the DOE Keeps Fighting Their Claims.

Lonnie Rouse shuffled into a wood-paneled conference room at the Washington state Capitol in March 2018. It had been nearly a decade since medical experts diagnosed him with a neurological disease they attributed to his exposure to chemicals at work. In the time since, he had acquired a stutter, begun limping and lost several teeth biting into a banana.

For nearly 20 years, Rouse had packaged plutonium and monitored radioactive waste at the Hanford Site, a now-decommissioned Department of Energy nuclear weapons production plant in south-central Washington and one of the most contaminated nuclear sites in the country. Tanks full of radioactive sludge exposed him to at least 48 potentially hazardous chemicals. The worksite was so toxic, he said, that the floor once melted the rubber off his boots.

Rouse was among the thousands of workers responsible for the ongoing environmental cleanup at Hanford, which has been underway since the DOE shut down the last reactor in the late 1980s. Congress has acknowledged, based on voluminous studies, that radiation at DOE sites poses health risks to workers.

Over time, some people at Hanford became so sick they could no longer work. Like Rouse, they filed for workers’ compensation. The DOE consistently challenged their claims and tried to shift the blame onto the workers themselves.

By the time Rouse took his place behind then-Gov. Jay Inslee, he was desperate. His 2009 workers’ compensation claim had been denied. He had burned through his retirement savings and declared bankruptcy.

As he watched, the governor signed a bill that was supposed to change his life.

For years, Hanford employees filing workers’ compensation claims had to prove the chemicals they were exposed to on the job made them sick. The new law Inslee signed flipped that burden of proof. Under the law, agencies were instructed to presume that working at Hanford had caused certain cancers, neurological diseases and respiratory illnesses. Now the Department of Energy would have to prove to the state that something other than the job had made the workers sick if it wanted to deny their claims.

Rouse thought the presumption law would speed up his claim for benefits. State lawmakers and the former governor thought it would force the DOE to take responsibility for the harm Hanford workers were experiencing.

But despite the law, the DOE continues to fight new and existing claims, Cascade PBS and ProPublica found. We asked the state for claims records going back to the presumption law’s passage in 2018, but the state only provided records going back three and a half years, to 2023. In that period, workers filed 102 new claims for benefits, state data shows. The DOE did not recommend approving a single one.

The state, which has final say, overruled the DOE and approved 75% of those claims. The DOE appealed all but seven of those approvals.

#4104. Experts: How are climate scientists using artificial intelligence?

📅 22 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Artificial intelligence (AI) is developing at a rapid pace and is increasingly being used by scientists to better understand climate change.

AI is perhaps best-known for its use in “large language models”, such as ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini.

From weather forecasting to analysing satellite images, climate scientists are using various types of AI to develop faster and more efficient ways to study the warming planet.

However, the technology comes with no shortage of risks and challenges, including overreliance on unverified results, intensive energy use and exacerbating existing inequalities in climate science.

Carbon Brief spoke to 10 experts working across a range of climate-related fields – from developing climate forecasts to analysing changes in the energy grid – about how AI is being used.

Their answers are set out below, grouped by theme.

Dr Doug McNeall, science lead in data-driven climate modelling at the UK Met Office:

One of the most significant recent developments has been the success of AI weather-prediction models, which learn from large volumes of weather data to predict the next few hours ahead and then repeatedly feed those predictions back into themselves to generate forecasts up to two weeks into the future.

These models have demonstrated that AI can provide accurate predictions while requiring far less computing power than existing numerical models [which rely on mathematical equations to simulate the climate].

One of the main challenges is that AI weather-prediction models have been developed for relatively short timescales, where large amounts of observational data [for example, temperature, rainfall or sea ice extent data] is available and model performance can be evaluated quickly and rigorously.

Climate prediction is fundamentally different. To provide useful climate information, we need to make projections for future climates that lie outside the observational record.

One promising route forward is, therefore, to use AI to emulate or accelerate these existing models, rather than relying solely on purely data-driven approaches. This blended approach could make climate risk information far more accessible and affordable to produce, while retaining the physical understanding [based on mathematical and scientific principles] that underpins confidence in climate projections.

Prof William Collins, professor of AI for climate science at the University of East Anglia:

We’re seeing a really exciting renaissance in both weather and climate science…Machine learning [a subset of AI that uses algorithms to let computers learn from data and make predictions without being explicitly programmed] has had an enormous impact on numerical weather prediction.

So, for example, the European Center for Mid-Range Weather Forecasts is now deploying an AI forecast system as part of their official communication of weather predictions to the European community, alongside the traditional forecast system. AI systems have become good enough that they’re absolutely competitive with the world’s leading numerical weather prediction…So that’s been quite remarkable.

Much of machine learning and AI involves learning from data and climate represents a future for which we do not have data. We’re literally trying to predict the future. We don’t have observations of it. So, where does the data come from to inform the models? That is, at the moment, the central challenge that the climate community is attempting to overcome.

Dr Amirpasha Mozaffari, researcher in Earth artificial intelligence at the Barcelona Supercomputing Centre:

AI forecast models do well on typical weather, but underperform physics-based models on record-breaking and unprecedented extremes – precisely the events that matter most for adaptation [to climate change].

Solving that – whether through hybrid models [which combine AI with traditional models], physical constraints [in which rules are implemented based on the laws of physics to help the models make realistic forecasts] or new ways of evaluating models on what they have not seen – is the central open problem for the field.

Dr Stefano Materia, researcher in climate variability and change at the Barcelona Supercomputing Centre:

Besides the climate modelling and prediction framework, the proliferation of Earth-observation satellites has created a massive influx of geospatial data (for example, images showing deforestation or sea ice melt). AI is used to translate raw pixel data into actionable climate intelligence.

Methane tracking and ecosystem monitoring are two examples of this kind of application. Methane is a potent greenhouse gas, but leaks are largely invisible and hard to track. AI algorithms now automatically scan hyperspectral imagery [which uses data from across the electromagnetic spectrum] from satellites to pinpoint specific industrial methane leaks globally, alerting operators in near real-time.

At the same time, machine-learning models process thousands of satellite images daily to detect illegal logging, track land-use changes and monitor glacier retreat long before humans could manually review the footage.

Dr Clare Eayrs, polar research scientist at the Environmental Fluid Dynamics Lab at New York University:

In sea ice research, machine learning is now used across the sea ice modelling workflow…The most mature application is extracting more information from observations, for example, mapping fine-scale sea ice deformation from ship radar and classifying snow layers in micropenetrometer profiles [which measure the hardness or softness of different layers of snow].

Forecasting is the most visible application in this field. Ice Net [a sea ice forecasting system] was an early demonstration that a deep-learning system [which uses models inspired by the layers of a human brain to process information] could outperform a leading physical model in seasonal Arctic forecasts. Machine-learning systems are now used to predict [climate] variables such as sea ice concentration, thickness and drift.

Prof Cristiana Stan, vice-chair of the World Climate Research Programme (WCRP):

AI now appears across all major parts of the WCRP. [For example,]…the ”weather prediction model intercomparison project” [an initiative to create a centralised database of physics- based, machine-learning and hybrid model forecasts] includes physics-based numerical weather prediction models, hybrid [models] and AI weather prediction models. Because it is focused on operational models, the project offers a direct comparison between state-of-the art systems. [It] is intended to accelerate the deployment of the next generation of operational models and contributes to development of metrics needed to assess relative strengths and weaknesses of models.

Downscaling is another area that is harnessing AI power to refine the coarse-resolution model output into high-resolution.

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) is performing its seventh assessment report (AR7)…This time, for the very first time, there’s a model intercomparison project just for models using AI [for weather and climate forecasting]. So that is a real milestone…It’s a very exciting proof of principle or proof of concept and, I think, the harbinger of many more developments of this kind to come.

[For more on how model intercomparison will inform IPCC AR7, see Carbon Brief’s recent guest post.]

A few years ago, it took two or three years for a new architecture to make its way from the machine-learning community into weather and climate work. Today it is a matter of months: diffusion models, foundation models and generative ensembles have all arrived in our field almost as soon as they appeared elsewhere. That speed opens up things that were previously out of reach.

And the cost of running a global forecast has dropped by orders of magnitude…which, in principle, changes who can produce weather and climate information at all.

Prof Priya Donti, assistant professor at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and co-founder of Climate Change AI:

As we introduce more solar, wind and other renewable energy into the power grid, we need to rethink how we actually manage the various sources on the power grid…

AI is used in a few different ways here. One of them is just trying to get better predictions of solar and wind power production in order to reduce that uncertainty and allow us to plan with a little more foresight.

Another big one is helping to actually optimise and control the various components we have on power grids – the power generators, the batteries, flexible loads like industrial loads or the batteries in your electric vehicles or heating and cooling systems.

Dr Carlos Rodriguez-Pardo, postdoctoral researcher at Politecnico di Milano, affiliated scientist at the European Institute on Economics and the Environment and at the Euro-Mediterranean Center on Climate Change:

AI is already useful at every stage of the climate policy cycle.

The first stage is measurement: understanding what is happening, what has been tried and who is exposed. Machine learning helps us process satellite imagery to estimate emissions, improve air quality monitoring, harmonise fragmented policy databases and map a scientific literature that has grown far beyond what any human team could read.

The second stage is modelling futures. The models we use to explore climate and economic scenarios are computationally very expensive, so AI-based emulators [simple models that can be used to represent more complex mathematical models] that reproduce their behaviour at a small fraction of the cost are transforming what we can do. Instead of a handful of scenarios, we can explore thousands and quantify uncertainties that were previously out of reach.

Reinforcement learning [a type of AI in which a computer programme learns to make decisions through a process of trial and error] is also allowing us to study how multiple actors, such as countries or electricity producers, compete and cooperate under different policy designs. This can reveal strategies that classical analysis misses.

The third stage is evaluation, where causal machine learning is extending the toolkit we use to answer whether a policy actually worked. That is a different – and much harder – question than predicting what will happen next.

The WCRP has established a task team to address this very question. The focus areas considered by the task team include modelling and projections, data and observations [and AI] impacts and services, engagement with the private sector supporting development and integration of AI and machine learning into climate research and applications and the scientific rigour and trustworthiness of AI and machine learning methods.

I think the IPCC’s eighth assessment [likely due in the late 2020s] may itself largely be written using large language models. Basically, the job of the people writing the IPCC report is to synthesise…One could certainly argue whether or not that needs to be done by people. AI should be telling us the known unknowns, not the known knowns… The thing that’s value-added [by AI] as part of that assessment process is to tell us what isn’t certain, what isn’t known well. Because then that tells you where the science should be heading.

[In September 2026, the IPCC established an “informal group” on AI to “provide timely guidance on the responsible use of AI and emerging digital technologies in IPCC processes”.]

AI will also be an integral part of the eighth Coupled Model Intercomparison Project, with machine-learning codes running alongside traditional climate models.

The inclusion of AI in climate and land-vegetation modelling represents a paradigm shift. Rather than replacing physical models entirely, hybrid modelling merges the mathematical rigour and physical consistency of traditional process-based Earth system models [advanced computer models that combine physical, chemical and biological processes to simulate the climate] with the pattern-recognition power and speed of machine learning.

This new approach will be able to overcome computational bottlenecks, replace unresolved subgrid processes [which are too small to be captured by the grid cells of a computer model], improve the terrestrial biosphere – finally narrowing down the uncertainties on the terrestrial carbon sink and accurately pinpointing emergent constraints and non-linear tipping points.

Much of what we know about Antarctic sea ice comes from the satellite era, which begins in 1979. That is a short record for a system with strong decadal variability. A great deal of information about earlier conditions is contained in ship logs, whaling records, expedition photographs and early aerial and pre-1979 satellite imagery. Machine learning is well suited to extracting it. Lengthening the observational record would improve almost everything else we do, including training and testing AI systems.

AI is frequently thought of as large generative models, like [those] developed by large tech companies. But that is not mostly what is useful in AI for tackling climate change.

People talk a lot about energy usage of AI and negative impacts on society, including on the climate, as a result of that energy usage and associated negative impacts like water usage. These are very real worries. However, it is worth noting that the massive energy and other requirements of AI systems are referring to those large-scale generative and agentic systems. They’re not mostly about the kinds of AI systems that are actually used to fight climate change.

I think the general public thinks of AI as this one specific thing…often large language models, chatbots, etc…People who are making allocation decisions for who funds scientists might have a mismatched understanding of what needs to happen, which means the right science doesn’t necessarily get funded.

Frontier models [which are at the cutting edge of AI research] are built almost exclusively in the global north, trained on data that is densest where observations are richest and validated against benchmarks that reflect the same regions.

Cheap inference [in which a trained AI model applies its learned patterns to new, unseen data] does not fix that on its own: being able to run someone else’s model is not the same as having a say in what it is trained on, how it is evaluated, or whether it serves your decisions.

Without deliberate effort to share data infrastructure, evaluation metrics that centre on people’s wellbeing and genuine co-production of knowledge, AI risks automating and amplifying the existing divide in the global climate information system rather than closing it.

If done well, [developing AI-based Earth system models] could dramatically lower barriers to entry. A small research group, or an agency in a data-poor region, could adapt a pre-trained model on modest hardware rather than building bespoke systems from scratch. That matters because [the] data is thinnest and the resolution gap is largest exactly where vulnerability to climate [change] is highest.

Dr David Rolnick, associate professor in the school of computer science at McGill University and cofounder of Climate Change AI:

Climate action should fundamentally be about democratisation of action and equitable response to climate change, both in terms of mitigation and in terms of adaptation. The consolidation of large-scale AI can often work counter to those goals by consolidating power in certain geographies and stakeholders, and by defining action based upon the goals of those stakeholders.

When you build technology in specific institutions, they do not necessarily understand the needs of the majority of the world’s population or local stakeholders from other geographies and other entities.

The biggest challenge is trust…Good performance is necessary, but it is not enough. We also need independent evaluation, physical credibility, process-based validation, robustness and some degree of interpretability.

The risk I worry about most is that the apparent success of these systems could be read as a reason to invest less on observations. AI is not a substitute for observation, physical understanding or careful evaluation. Especially for Antarctic sea ice, where observations of sea ice and snow thickness remain sparse and understanding of snow-ice processes remains limited, we need more, targeted observations, not fewer. It also makes the case for documenting them – and their uncertainties – far better than we do now.

The challenge is always making sure that you’re getting the right answer for the right reason and the risk is getting a plausible answer that is “truthy”, but not the truth…Ask a computer for an answer and it will give you one. And it’s very good. It will always obey. It will always give you an answer. The question is whether or not that answer is valid or not.

The first and foremost risk is AI diverging attention from other forms of climate action. AI is not a silver bullet. It is not an answer in itself. And where it is useful, it is only one part of the solution.

Prof Detlef van Vuuren, a senior researcher at the PBL Netherlands Environmental Assessment Agency [in a 2026 Carbon Brief interview]:

There are now many more proposals being submitted than in the past, as people can use AI tools. So our science discipline has to start learning how to deal with this, both in terms of what we feel is allowed in creating [scientific] articles – what is allowed in reviewing [and] what is allowed in proposal writing. This is something that we have to learn.

The partnership between academia, the data centres and private industry is going to be integral to our success going forward. And I think the challenge is going to be navigating that relationship in a way that respects open science, uses open source code, etc…

You do not want to be in a situation where we have something like a large language model where it’s not open source…[We need] fairness, accountability, transparency and explainability.

High-resolution outputs can look exact when they are not; uncertainties compound in invisible ways and predictions that feed into policy need honest, well-calibrated uncertainty estimates, communicated in decision-relevant terms.

Like any other technology, AI should augment human judgement in climate decisions, not replace it. As researchers, it is our responsibility to ensure the field serves as a force for social progress.

In early August, election workers north of Spokane, Washington, went across the street to the post office to check for late mail-in ballots. It was three days after the primary and they were expecting no more than a few dozen.

Instead, their mailbox was brimming with envelopes — more than 500 of them, each a vote that wouldn’t count. The sight was “jarring,” said Auditor Lori Larsen, a Republican who oversees voting in Stevens County.

Across the region, other election officials were getting similar jolts. In the 11 predominately rural counties in eastern Washington, nearly 2,000 ballots came in late, compared to about 600 in 2022, the previous midterm primary.

In recent weeks, concerns about mail-in voting disruptions have focused almost entirely on President Donald Trump’s now-shelved plan to sharply restrict who can vote by mail.

But a ProPublica data analysis shows that delays resulting from the U.S. Postal Service’s cost-cutting measures have endangered the validity of ballots cast in rural areas, not only in Washington, but across the country.

Over the past three years, as mail volume continued to plummet and annual deficits ballooned, the Postal Service started consolidating the processing of first-class mail at larger regional plants instead of at smaller hubs closer to local post offices. Then, in a little-noted switch that mostly took effect after the 2024 election, the agency cut the frequency of mail pickups in half at many post offices 50 miles or more from these processing plants, from twice daily to once.

The changes often added a day or more to delivery time and, most importantly for ballots, pushed back postmarking, which doesn’t happen until mail reaches the regional centers.

In this year’s primary, the effects of the cuts were clearest in five states that make detailed data on mail-in ballots available: Washington, California, Ohio, North Carolina and Oregon. All have or recently had grace periods allowing ballots to count if they come in after Election Day as long as they’re postmarked by the deadline.

In these states, the number of ballots rejected for arriving late soared in counties affected by the service changes compared to 2022, ProPublica’s analysis showed.

Rejection rates in counties where services were cut doubled or tripled from what they were four years ago, dwarfing any increases in counties where postal pickups remained as they were, our analysis showed. In urban Spokane, for example, the number of ballots rejected for being late actually dropped this year.

Across the five states, the delays have had the most substantial effect in areas that voted for Trump in 2024, ProPublica’s analysis shows. In Washington, Secretary of State Steve Hobbs, a Democrat, said he sees the service changes directed by Republican appointees leading the Postal Service as a way to undermine mail-in voting.

“I don’t think they realized how much it was going to affect their voter base,” he said, adding that Washington has “a lot of rural Republican voters.”

Postal Service leaders say such delays are less likely in the Nov. 3 general election because of “extraordinary measures” the agency puts into place for these votes, when there’s typically far more turnout. They have circulated a memo to the nation’s more than 30,000 post offices saying that for the 10 days before the election, managers should separate completed ballots from other first-class mail and deliver those intended for local election offices the day they come in.

At an Oct. 15 news conference, Postal Service leaders emphasized the agency’s longstanding recommendation that voters mail ballots back at least a week before their states’ deadlines, while defending the agency’s ability to fast-track ballots through the system.

“We work to identify and address issues quickly,” said Amber McReynolds, chair of the Postal Service board of governors, “so ballots can continue to move swiftly from election offices to voters and from voters back to election offices.”

Election consultants, researchers and former Postal Service officials told ProPublica that the guidance for the upcoming vote gave more detailed directions on how to expedite ballots. They said they hoped the agency’s special measures would limit delays, but some said mistakes were likely.

“I don’t think we can say with any kind of conviction that there will be no ballots rejected,” said Tammy Patrick, chief programs officer of the National Association of Election Officials. “It isn’t a question of ‘if,’ but a question of ‘how many.'”

A pilot program instituting the delivery changes in 18 regions before the 2024 election ran into problems. An audit by the Postal Service’s inspector general found that agency staff in these areas sometimes didn’t handle ballots correctly, mainly because instructions weren’t clearly communicated. (A Postal Service spokesperson said “learnings from the pilot” have been applied as the program has expanded.)

After this year’s primary, reports by media outlets noted that mail-in ballot rejection rates were higher than in the past, but ProPublica’s analysis shows these increases were directly connected to Postal Service changes. Research published in September by the Brookings Institution also found this connection, projecting that if the pattern held in the general election, tens of thousands of mail-in ballots could be rejected.

At least seven closely contested congressional districts — including Washington’s 3rd, California’s 22nd and North Carolina’s 11th — are in areas that were affected by service changes in the primary, ProPublica found.

Elena Patel, who conducted the Brookings analysis, said service changes may still imperil mail-in votes in the general election.

“I think there remain risks to ballots,” she said, adding that the Postal Service’s plan for the general election “depends on flawless execution.”

Mail-in voting has become more popular over the last decade, with many states broadening access to it during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Nearly 1 in 3 Americans voted this way in 2022 and 2024. Trump himself frequently votes by mail, and in recent weeks has started to encourage his supporters to do so as well, though he has claimed without evidence that the process is rife with fraud.

The Postal Service has mostly ensured the timely delivery of hundreds of millions of ballots, even as mail volume overall has dropped 50% from its high in 2006.

When Louis DeJoy was appointed postmaster general in spring 2020, he inherited an $81 billion operating deficit and rapidly took steps such as eliminating overtime to reduce costs, under pressure from Trump and others in his administration. (DeJoy did not respond to questions from ProPublica.)

The NAACP sued that August, alleging the measures could affect mail-in votes in that year’s general election. Courts ordered DeJoy to reverse some of the cuts and the Postal Service agreed to implement special procedures to ensure ballots weren’t delayed. The Postal Service has continued following those procedures since.

#3626. The Fight for Educational Equality Is Also a Fight for a Just Supreme Court

📅 22 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

The court has severely weakened our ability to make an education system that fulfills the promise of Brown.

Last month, a piece by New York Times journalist Nikole Hannah-Jones sparked conversations across America about race, education, and parenting. In the piece, Hannah-Jones described in painstaking—and often painful— detail the consequences of the deliberate decision she and her husband made to send their daughter to a mostly Black, lower-income, underperforming New York City public school rather than a school with better educational prospects and, perhaps, fewer Black students. Hannah-Jones’s story is personal, but it also represents one that Black parents in the United States have faced for generations: How do we make the best choices for our children in an education system that has never kept its promise to them?

For Black parents, the question of where to send our children to school has never been simply a matter of parental preference. It has been shaped by government decisions regarding where we are allowed to live, how resources are allocated, how school districts are drawn, and ultimately what the Supreme Court of the United States considers to be a fair and just education for our kids.

While much of the current debate has boiled down to scrutiny over which choices are right for Black parents, it is imperative that we ask the deeper question running through Hannah-Jones’s piece: Why has any Black parent been forced to make such consequential trade-offs in the first place?

Long before the Legal Defense Fund, the organization I lead, won the Brown v. Board of Education case in 1954, ending state-sponsored racial segregation in public schools, Black Americans understood education as essential to freedom. Immediately after Emancipation, Black communities got to work building schools, training teachers, and raising money to invest in educational pursuits because they knew this would help smooth their rocky path to full citizenship.

In doing so, Black communities were helping to establish the very infrastructure of universal public education. Across the South, according to the National Park Service, newly enfranchised Black voters and elected officials fought diligently “to create the frameworks for public education in the 1860s and 1870s.” In 1868, Black state legislators in South Carolina helped pass laws that created one of the first statewide systems of public schools in the nation.

Brown was a constitutional milestone in the ongoing struggle to ensure that Black children could receive a quality education in the same public school system their ancestors and communities worked to build. Yet, over 70 years later, Black families must still navigate the education system of a country that fails to make it real, too often forcing them into choosing between their child’s psychological safety and the quality of their educational opportunities.

That’s in part because, in the decades after Brown, the Supreme Court has repeatedly weakened the structures that might have lessened the need for Black families to consider these trade-offs. As a result, our public schools continue to struggle with hyper-segregation.

Take 1973’s San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez. In that case, the court upheld a Texas school funding system that overly relied on local property taxes, despite the fact that such a system produced significant differences in resources available to different districts due to state-sponsored racial residential segregation and its residual effects. This ruling, in effect, failed all students seeking government protections from an unequally funded school system, and was reinforced by the fact that education is not a fundamental right under the United States Constitution.

Nearly 30 years later, in Alexander v. Sandoval, the court held that individuals and other impacted parties could not use civil rights statutes, like Title VI, to challenge policies and practices, including those in the education system, that disproportionately harm certain children on account of race.

And in 2006’s notorious Parents Involved in Community Schools v. Seattle School District No. 1, the Supreme Court cannibalized its own legacy by striking down valiant efforts by Seattle school districts to maintain racially diverse schools through parent-led initiatives.

Taken together, these decisions cut off avenues to enact true change and fulfill Brown’s promise, instead placing the burden on families to navigate a defective system. That is the true tension at the heart of the school-choice debate that Hannah-Jones’s confessional conjures. And that is why LDF is fighting to advance policies that aim to expand educational opportunities across the country, including in New York, where we represent a multiracial coalition of students seeking an equal chance to compete for admission to New York City’s specialized high schools.

The broader debate over the future of the Supreme Court cannot be separated from the debate over the future of public education. The court, ironically, once described public education as “the very foundation of good citizenship.” But it has issued critical decisions that undermine that project and ignore that high-quality, equally accessible public education is essential for our multiracial, multiethnic democracy to thrive.

The lesson from Hannah-Jones’s experiences is not that individual parents need to make “better” decisions; it is that our institutions need to give every family a meaningful choice. At its core, Brown promised America’s children equal educational opportunity and equal citizenship. Our branches of government, including the judiciary, must ensure that our society is legally and lawfully equipped to fulfill that promise.

For decades, the Supreme Court has been unwilling to do so, at the expense of tens of millions of children subjected to a woefully substandard education in taxpayer-funded schools. A court that is unwilling to fully uphold the breadth of its canonical decision on equality cannot be entrusted to define the boundaries of our Constitution and the rules of our democracy without appropriate checks on its authority. That’s why the fight for educational equality also requires a fight for a just Supreme Court. Otherwise, most Black families will continue to have school choice in name only, the scourge of school segregation will persist, and America’s foundation of good citizenship will remain unlaid.

#4236. ‘Gratifying and humbling’: First Nation leads ghost net cleanup from Interior B.C. rivers

📅 22 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

This article has been co-published with IndigiNews, and was produced as part of our 2026 Indigenous journalism fellowship.

After starting the day with a prayer and a safety demonstration, a group of volunteers gears up in water-resistant clothing and lifejackets, ready to hit the water.

The crew is preparing to scour a stretch of the Fraser and Thompson rivers, removing any fishing gear or waste left behind. Fishing nets left underneath the water are commonly known as ghost nets, which trap and kill fish.

As the saying goes on a volunteer call out poster, “ghost nets create ghost fish.”

TLKemchEEN (Lytton First Nation) has led the river clean-up program in the area for the past 20 years, all in an effort to preserve salmon populations and aquatic life following harvest season.

Over two days in September, a group of 40 volunteers spent a total of 20 combined hours on the water.

From Lillooet to Lytton during the first day, and from Lytton to Yale during the second day, volunteers passed through white-water rapids and rugged terrain such as the famously turbulent passage of Hell’s Gate.

When the raft leader yells “hold on,” that’s exactly what participants do, reaching down for a rope attached to the raft, laughing and hollering, ripping through the Fraser Canyon rapids.

The cold river water splashes everyone — waking up the crew as countless bald and golden eagles watch from above.

“I didn’t realize how impactful everything is along the shores. I guess I didn’t realize that some leave their garbage and nets out there,” volunteer Rebecca Riley, who is from the Stʼatʼimc Nation, said.

“Once I saw that with my own eyes, I wanted to jump in and be a part of it.”

It’s a sentiment shared by many volunteers on the boat, who eagerly approach the shoreline when a ghost net comes into sight.

Whether the net will take one person to remove, or up to five to pull out of the water, is unknown until people get their hands wet.

During the non-rapid portion of the cleanup, countless stories were told, Indigenous people exchanged words in their languages to compare similarities and differences and folks even had hot chocolate along the shore, courtesy of Kumsheen Rafting, which was contracted to support the program this year.

During the first year of the program, 126 ghost nets were collected.

“This year we picked up six nets and some garbage, some years prior we’ve picked up zero. We have to remind our fishermen not to be lazy and take their gear home,” Kevin Duncan told the Narwhal.

Duncan is an elected councillor for TLKemchEEN in B.C.’s Southern Interior, part of the Nlaka’pamux Nation Tribal Council. He has been involved with the program since its inception. He attended both cleanup days this year with his son Aaron.

For TLKemchEEN, stewarding their waters as they always have is crucial — especially as salmon numbers decline. Life along the Fraser and Thompson rivers is deeply connected to salmon. And TLKemchEEn is home to where the two rivers meet, in the country’s largest salmon-bearing watershed.

“For future generations, sometime down the road, I’m afraid all they’re going to be looking at is pictures of salmon in a book,” said Duncan.

The nets recovered by the program are given to those in need, including single mothers, low-income families and Elders from the nation.

“As a Nlaka’pamux or Stʼatʼimc member … that fishery is a communal right, it’s not an individual right. It’s looked after by your nation,” Duncan said.

“Most times, you just get to see portions of the river from the highway, but once you’re down on the water, floating for eight hours from Lillooet to Lytton, it gives you a better perspective on where people fish.”

Removing fishing gear from the cold and fast-moving river can be dangerous, which is why Duncan starts each day of the program with a yémit, or prayer in Nlaka’pamxcín.

“Salmon are the lifeblood of our people, and when these fish pass through coastal communities, they pray for them in the lower Fraser. We’re doing the same thing as they pass through our territories in the mid Fraser. … These fish hear that prayer.”

Although she’s been on the Fraser and Thompson rivers many times throughout her fisheries career, she said that the ghost net removal program was “eye opening,” regarding the number of fishers out on the water.

She said that Lillooet Tribal Council is happy to help as the neighbouring nation to Nlaka’pamux territory, given the large area to cover and the shared value of protecting salmon.

“When we’re having the long days and we’re getting splashed and it’s cold, if we save a salmon or two that are still alive and let them free, or we clear out a net so we know that other animals can’t go in, it’s very gratifying and humbling,” she said.

“At the end of the day, when you’re riding the bus home and you’re cold and hungry, you know in the back of your mind it was so worth it.”

While the focus of the program is cleaning up the river, volunteers take the opportunity to support other initiatives while on the water, such as mapping traditional fishing sites to prove occupancy.

According to Conservation Biology, “Proof of occupancy is the foundational legal and moral requirement needed to establish Aboriginal Title, regain territorial sovereignty and protect ancestral lands from unauthorized exploitation.”

Lillooet Tribal Council’s extreme heat resiliency planner, Lyza Adolph, did a lot of heavy lifting over the two days removing ghost nets, but also had a mission of her own.

A member of the Stʼatʼimc Nation, this was her first time participating in the ghost net removal program.

“It was very cold, but with the group that we had, it was very enjoyable and uplifting,” she said.

With a focus on planning for extreme heat in her position at the tribal council, Adolph monitored fishing sites during the program to see if there were any shade trees nearby, scoping out areas to potentially plant some.

“The program tied in with my traditional shade tree planting. I know more about the upper river, but from Lillooet to Lytton and beyond, I didn’t know too much about it. It was definitely nice to see the various fish camps and see if there were shade trees,” she told The Narwhal.

Shade trees are beneficial for both humans and aquatic life.

As spawning salmon break down, their nutrients feed the trees. In turn, trees offer shade to cool the water, which is critical for survival on a warming planet, and tree roots and branches give salmonids space to hide from predators while also preventing erosion.

In the Fraser Canyon, which includes the communities of Yale, Lytton and Lillooet, fishing sites can be hard to access. Adolph’s shade tree monitoring data, collected by boat, will be used in future tree planting projects.

“I would definitely do it again,” Adolph said. “Although it was very cold, it was very refreshing and rewarding by the end of the day.”

In March of 2026, in a nondescript, 46th-floor office overlooking lower Manhattan, about two dozen members of Scientific American’s editorial staff had gathered in person and online to talk openly about their approach to political coverage.

David Ewalt, the editor in chief, wanted them all to understand something: The magazine was not “kowtowing” to the Trump administration. In fact, according to a recording of the meeting obtained by The Nation, he asked staff present to “raise your hand if you’ve ever heard me say not to cover a political story.”

For well over a year, rumors had been circulating among the staff about top-level editors’ rejecting pitches they saw as too political, controversial articles triggering angry phone calls from Scientific American’s corporate owner, and even, on at least one occasion, ownership forcing editors to blunt language critical of the Trump administration.

Then there was what happened to the opinion department. In the first months of the new administration, it had been publishing about 15 pieces a month, many of which criticized Trump’s attacks on scientific research. But after the department published a particularly provocative column in May 2025—“Science Tells Us the U.S. Is Heading toward a Dictatorship”—management, in what one staffer would later call an “unprecedented” move, had told the department to “pause” publishing new articles until the new editor in chief started.

The opinion department’s hiatus had worried some people more than others—there were legitimate journalistic reasons to want to emphasize breaking news, even if not everyone agreed with them. But by March 2026, nine months into Ewalt’s tenure, the opinion section seemed to be gone for good—and other departments were feeling political pressure, too. That, along with a round of layoffs, a restructuring of the newsroom, and veiled threats about the future of the magazine, had left the staff frustrated and looking for answers. Now that the new editor in chief was finally coming to the full editorial staff “to listen as well as to speak,” as one staffer put it, they began asking questions.

What about that rumor, someone asked him: Had editors really been told to not put the word “Trump” in headlines?

That was true, Ewalt said. Last year, a Breitbart article had criticized Springer Nature, Scientific American’s German owner, for attacking those “skeptical of the coronavirus vaccine” and spreading “gender insanity.” “No more taxpayer money for woke publishers!” declared Donald Trump Jr., retweeting the article. In June 2025, Axios reported that all federal subscriptions to Springer Nature publications had been canceled. Although Trump officials later walked back the bulk of the cuts, they had shocked the scientific community: Without access to Springer Nature’s thousands of journals, including the prestigious Nature publications, researchers at federal agencies—from the NIH to NASA—would struggle to stay abreast of scientific developments. The company itself, meanwhile, risked losing as much as $25 million in federal contracts.

So if Ewalt had asked people to keep Trump out of the headlines last summer, it was because corporate higher-ups were visiting the site all the time. “And if they saw the word ‘Trump,’” he said, according to the recording, “they were wetting their fucking lederhosen.” People had no idea how “lily-livered and weak-kneed and cowardly” Springer Nature was, he said. “They are so fucking afraid of the Trump administration.”

The opinion editors had long since been reassigned to other roles, but now, almost a year later, Ewalt was telling his staff that even news stories sometimes had too much of a slant to them. “I am not ever telling anybody here ‘Don’t go after the Trump administration,’” he told them. “I’m saying I don’t want to hear your opinion.” He just didn’t think it was their “strongest weapon.” If everyone stuck to objective, hard-hitting reporting, Ewalt believed that they would be able to stay out of trouble with Springer Nature.

Mentioning Trump in headlines was now allowed. “But let’s be realistic about the world we live in,” he told his staff. Last August, Ewalt warned, Springer Nature could have easily decided that the magazine’s “$4 million in losses” wasn’t worth risking millions more that the company earned from federally funded subscriptions to its scientific journals. He had been “trying to keep Springer Nature from freaking out and shutting us down,” he said.

As the meeting came to an end, Ewalt seemed optimistic. “This is useful,” he said. “We’ll do this more.”

Or would he retract articles if readers sent too many angry emails, or if Springer Nature’s billionaire CEO called? Those concerns, one staffer said, weren’t “quelled in that meeting at all.” Rather, it had transformed accumulated gossip and implication into something far more concrete—and “set everybody’s teeth on edge,” said another staffer, who, like a number of sources for this story, chose to speak anonymously because they feared retaliation from current or prospective employers. “He was trying to be honest with us,” the staffer said. In recent years, however, Springer Nature’s hands-off reputation has begun to falter. Interviews conducted this summer and fall with a over a dozen current and former Scientific American employees depict a magazine that, especially over the past two years, has struggled to protect its editorial independence in the face of sometimes overwhelming pressure from its corporate ownership—a picture corroborated by audio recordings and documents obtained by The Nation.

Back in 2017, Springer Nature had made headlines when it admitted that it had censored hundreds of academic articles in mainland China at the behest of the Chinese government. But for most staffers, the first sign of a more hands-on approach came in 2021, when an opinion article about the medical situation in Gaza was pulled from Scientific American’s site after right-wing backlash.

At the time, the article’s authors received no explanation. But current and former Scientific American staffers familiar with the situation told The Nation that the reason was simple: Stefan von Holtzbrinck himself—known for his enthusiasm for Israel—had called then–editor in chief Laura Helmuth and ordered that it be taken down.

It wouldn’t be the last conflict between Helmuth and Springer Nature. Helmuth, who joined the magazine in 2020, had pushed it to cover more politics, arguing that science cannot be disentangled from its social context. Scientific American had issued presidential endorsements for the first time under her leadership. In 2024, after Helmuth called Trump supporters “bigots” and “fascists” on social media on election night, the magazine again became the target of right-wing backlash. This time, facing pressure from Springer Nature, she resigned.

The magazine had been struggling financially for years, but the pressure to be profitable only increased as Springer Nature repeatedly tried and failed to go public; in 2024, it finally succeeded. After Helmuth’s departure, “it felt like a pendulum swing back to ‘let’s stick to the science,’” said Kelso Harper, who was then the magazine’s senior multimedia editor. “Let’s not get involved in too much messy political stuff.”

In the first months of Trump’s second administration, though, messy politics was almost unavoidable. Trump brought disaster after disaster for science writ large—thousands of grants suspended or canceled, tens of thousands of workers fired or pressured to resign, billions of dollars of funding slashed or frozen. Scientific American’s opinion department critiqued Republican attacks on everything from trans health care and measles vaccines to fluoride and the National Weather Service.

Then, in April 2025, as the administration’s war on DEI in science continued to accelerate, psychiatrist and anthropologist Eric Reinhart posted a letter to X that the Trump-appointed US attorney for Washington, DC, Ed Martin, had originally sent to the Illinois-based medical journal CHEST. The letter, dated April 14, asked: Was CHEST protecting the public from misinformation? Was it disclosing when viewpoints were influenced by funders? Did it publish articles from competing viewpoints? “It has been brought to my attention that more and more journals like CHEST Journal are conceding that they are partisans in various scientific debates,” the letter read. “The public has certain expectations, and you have certain responsibilities.”

CHEST is not well-known outside its field, but the letter made national headlines as an example of the administration’s unprecedented efforts to undermine what the right called “woke” science. Reinhart submitted an op-ed to Scientific American senior editor Dan Vergano arguing that the letter represented “an authoritarian threat to science.” As the story moved toward publication, news broke of similar letters to other journals, including the prestigious New England Journal of Medicine and JAMA. Reinhart’s op-ed quickly progressed through an initial edit, a “top” edit, and a copy edit; according to e-mails and documents obtained by The Nation, a legal reviewer made only one, minor change.

But when the draft returned from another round of edits, this time from acting editor in chief Jeanna Bryner, the word “fascist” had disappeared, along with a reference to the genocide in Gaza. A paragraph in which Reinhart argued that fascists benefit from “self-interest, automatic obedience, and ethical cowardice” had been cut entirely. In a comment in the document, he protested: “Shying away from publicness and directness on these issues will only make them worse.”

The draft next went to Springer Nature’s US head of legal for review. He left a comment declaring, with no explanation, that the cuts were “necessary.” “Got it,” Bryner responded.

A tranche of recently released documents show that Springer Nature had good reason to fear retaliation: On April 14, 2025, Ed Martin had sent Scientific American a threatening letter nearly identical to that received by CHEST. He sent similar letters to Nature Medicine—also owned by Springer Nature—along with at least 10 other publications. (Martin is currently facing disciplinary proceedings from the DC bar for sending a different threatening letter to Georgetown University.)

At the time, Scientific American president Kim Lau responded to Martin privately, asserting that its content was “fully protected by the First Amendment.” Behind the scenes, however, Springer Nature leadership was insisting on substantial changes to Reinhart’s op-ed, against Lau’s objections.

Those objections were why they were “ultimately allowed to print it” at all, Lau told Jeanna Bryner and the two opinion editors in a May 2025 meeting, according to audio obtained by The Nation. The only reason things didn’t “blow up in a bigger way,” she said, was that Reinhart eventually agreed to the changes. But when the piece was eventually published, on May 1, it received little attention—probably, she said in the meeting, because they had “watered it down so much.”

And when Dan Vergano published his column “Science Tells Us the U.S. Is Heading toward a Dictatorship” two weeks later, it was the final straw. Shortly after, Lau called Bryner, Vergano, and the chief opinion editor, Megha Satyanarayana, into the May 2025 meeting.

“There’s no way to sugarcoat this,” Lau told them. Springer Nature felt that Scientific American’s criticism of Trump was threatening their broader business—selling subscriptions to their scientific journals. The federal government was a major client.

“I have reiterated repeatedly that we have a history of editorial independence, and that these things are crossing that line,” said Lau, referring to Springer Nature’s repeated editorial interference. “It’s very clear that they understand that. But given where our business is, and the threats to the larger business, they don’t care.”

The department’s work, she said, was being placed on “pause” until the new editor in chief, David Ewalt, arrived.

Lau was explicit about the pressure they were all under. “We’re upsetting the company on a daily basis,” she said. Germany was six hours ahead of New York, and she and Bryner would wake up to early-morning calls from the German board. “If I thought me quitting would help the situation, I would have done it,” Lau said. “They are terrified about the things that are happening in the US.”

Although Lau gave the editors reason to hope that Ewalt might manage to resurrect the opinion department—“I think he sees value in it,” she said—he would turn out to be on the same page as the Springer Nature executives in at least one regard: It was time to reduce opinion output significantly. During his interviews, Ewalt had pitched them his new vision of the magazine: less editorializing, more facts.

In May 2025, though, the opinion editors—like the rest of the magazine—still didn’t know much about their new boss. Lau assured them they could all talk things over with Ewalt and see how he wanted to approach the issue. “We just need to be clear that what we’re fighting for is worth it. And I’ll be honest with you,” she said, “I don’t think this is the thing I want to die on the hill for.”

“We are in a fight right now for the future of SciAm in general,” she said. “There is nothing that’s a given in our future. We are losing a lot of money, and we do not necessarily have a clear path to closing that gap.”

Over the course of the next year and a half, at least 53 of the approximately 84 people employed at the magazine would either be laid off, resign, or retire.

Ewalt took over the magazine on June 2 with a mandate to increase traffic and subscriptions. But there were other changes, too. Helmuth’s departure had triggered a new attention to employees’ political expression, and Ewalt’s arrival reinstated objectivity as the journalistic North Star. Things that had produced no objections in the past became contentious: People were discouraged from following only left-leaning social-media accounts, for example, to avoid the appearance of bias. Although he initially promised that his restructuring efforts would not include layoffs, in October 2025 10 employees lost their jobs, replaced by a slate of hires to new positions, including three breaking news reporters. In December, Kim Lau threatened further changes if revenue didn’t start picking up—more layoffs, workers guessed, or maybe a sale.

Though October’s layoffs galvanized the then-incipient union campaign, staffers had additional concerns. Vergano, who had been reassigned to report on Washington, remembers hoping that the union could serve as a counterweight to the “clear, corrosive corporate interference and influence” he’d witnessed at the magazine. That emphasis on editorial independence, said WGAE organizer Andy Greenberg, was one of the things that set the campaign apart from others he’d worked on. “We have to look out for us,” one staffer realized, “because Springer Nature is not.”

But it was becoming difficult to enjoy the moment. In the weeks after the vote, rumors began to circulate that Springer Nature executives were “basically measuring everybody’s desk to sell it,” Vergano said. “It was not subtle.” The bad news hit on June 23: sale to LabX, salary cuts, mass layoffs.

“The amount of panic, the amount of tears, the amount of ‘I don’t know the next time that I will see you, goodbye forever maybe’ conversations that were happening in the newsroom—I mean, traumatic puts it lightly,” said books and culture editor Brianne Kane. Even those who had received job offers from the new owners were told to clear their desks and that their laptops would be shut off by midnight.

The next week, a few dozen workers, freelancers, and allies gathered at the 1 New York Plaza office. Despite the previous week’s events, the mood on the picket line was jaunty. A small but energetic brass band accompanied chants of “New York is a union town,” and the signage was heavy on puns (“I Do Not Cosine These Layoffs”).

Behind the scenes, Springer Nature was still doing its best to nullify the election, including by trying to withdraw the petition for an election by pointing out that it had laid off everyone at the magazine. “This was a case where a corporate parent restructured and sold its subsidiary in a transparent attempt to defeat an election,” the WGAE wrote in a legal filing. “The Board should not reward such conduct.” When the NLRB counted the votes, the tally was 31 to 1 to unionize.

In the immediate aftermath of the sale, staffers were left scrambling to learn more about Scientific American’s new owner. Previously, LabX’s best-known publication had been Discover, a onetime dentist’s office stalwart that was hollowed out by private equity buyers in the aughts; according to multiple staffers, the ownership transition seemed rushed, chaotic, and disorganized. When one worker reached out to a former employee of one of LabX’s other publications, the stories they heard—including ones related to editorial independence—were part of why they turned down their job offer, they said. (LabX did not respond to multiple requests for comment.)

The NLRB case with Springer Nature remains open, but the union also filed for a new election. LabX hasn’t hired any outside union-busters, but it has held three meetings asking for more time to prove that a union isn’t needed. “It’s comical,” said one staffer, given that the company’s first action as the new owner was to lay off “many very talented, very successful staff members.” The bargaining unit is much smaller this time around—from 35 to less than 20, with a few positions still in dispute—but workers remain as committed to organizing as ever. The result, counted this week, was 12 to one.

When Dan Vergano reached out to the Society of Professional Journalists’s ethics committee for advice in May 2025—shortly after the department’s work was put on pause—he was told to start by working through official channels: Going public could come at a high personal cost. Besides, as a journalist, “you don’t want to be part of the story,” Vergano said. “It’s literally stomach-churning.”

Still, he said, when a company is willing to interfere in critical journalism, how could reporters not end up pulling their punches? “We are trying to deliver you the raw stuff of democracy,” he said. “If a company allows their own greedy interests to shape that, then that’s a problem for democracy.”

“Reporters like to think that there should be a 60-foot brick wall between the business side of a news outlet and the editorial side,” Dan Axelrod, the chair of the Society for Professional Journalists’s ethics committee, told The Nation. “The reality is there isn’t, and there never has been.”

For most staffers, it’s still unclear exactly what motivated the sale—the money Scientific American was already losing, or the far vaster sums that Trump had threatened to take away, or both. Journalists at Nature News, also under the Springer Nature umbrella, were enduring “the same scrutiny” of their reporting on the Trump administration; Lau had told the opinion editors as much last year. But, Lau said at the time, Nature was “not a brand that’s losing money.” Scientific American, meanwhile, was a public-facing magazine owned by a company with little interest or expertise in public-facing journalism.

“The decision to divest reflected a strategic focus on our area of greatest expertise, academic research and scientific publishing,” Springer Nature’s North American president said in a statement to The Nation. They don’t need us. They don’t even, frankly, want us anymore.” (Both Ewalt and Lau declined to comment on this story, and both have left Scientific American.)

Though it’s not ideal for democracy or free speech, Axelrod said, the journalism code of conduct does not bar a publication’s owners from steering it away from public service in search of profit. But “it’s sad and surprising to think that they would potentially do that on the fly, in a way that breaks readers’ trust.”

It was tragic, he added, “that the journalists who are supposed to be reporting on undue influence by the government on the creation and dissemination of scientific findings appear to be subject to that same undue influence.”

For one staffer, who started working in journalism after the recession, the field had been in bad shape for as long as they could remember—but Scientific American, they said, was always held up as “one of the icons, if not the icon, of meaningful, impressive journalism about science.” It was their dream job. On its own, they said, Scientific American wasn’t going to solve all the challenges at the intersection of science and society—attacks on public health, a lack of climate action, widespread disinformation. Still, they worried about “losing the spirit of it,” and that it would become just another website publishing less-meaningful “cool stories about science.”

In a letter to readers in Scientific American’s September issue, the new editor in chief—Jeanna Bryner, promoted once again—announced that the magazine was moving out of its Battery Park office. “I can’t promise that the next chapter will be free of mistakes or tumult,” Bryner wrote. But the change in ownership was “an opportunity for SciAm to be even better”—and, she added, “have some fun.” (Bryner did not respond to multiple requests for comment.)

At least one freelancer has already written publicly about their negative experience with the new Scientific American, and two more editorial staffers were laid off at the end of September, including Megha Satyanarayana, Vergano’s former colleague in the opinion department—who declined to comment for this piece—and a data reporter. Vergano remains worried that LabX seems more interested in “gee-whiz stuff” than critical, substantive reporting. From what he’s seen of their publications, he said, “they produce things that are schlockfests. And we were doing good work at Scientific American.”

In the May 2025 meeting that shut down the opinion department, Vergano and Satyanarayana had grappled with the unintended consequences of that work, work that leadership had once championed. “We thought that by being more provocative and by becoming more of a voice ourselves that we would hopefully make a difference,” Satyanarayana said to Lau. The experiment, she said, was “costing us more than I ever thought it would.”

Satyanarayana was talking about their columns, but she could as easily have been referring to a broader project: Scientific American’s efforts, dating back to Laura Helmuth’s tenure, to advocate for science at a time when science was under attack more than ever before.

“This is something that many outlets are struggling with right now,” Lau said. “That doesn’t excuse it, or make it better. Probably it makes it worse.”

#3639. How to Stop Trump’s Coup

📅 22 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

South Korea’s lessons for the US midterms.

How do you stop a would-be dictator? The first requirement is knowing that it’s possible. In 2024, the people of South Korea took to the streets and stopped a would-be coup in its tracks. Their example offers lessons for how Americans can counter Donald Trump and his allies if they disrupt or defy the results of November’s midterms.

After escalating conflicts with South Korea’s legislature and bribery accusations against his wife, President Yoon Suk Yeol abruptly declared martial law on December 3, 2024, at 10:23 pm. Like Trump, Yoon had already appointed loyalists to key military posts, and his state prosecutor used libel laws to attack political opponents and journalists. Now Yoon banned all political activity, protests, and strikes, and claimed the right to detain anyone he chose. He ordered the military to enter the National Assembly by force to remove the members, “including the use of firearms if necessary.”

But 16,000 protesters rushed to the building to protect the legislature. As Korea’s opposition leader and others headed there, they livestreamed calls for more people to join them. Citizens went on to nonviolently block armored vehicles. When military helicopters landed inside the assembly grounds, staffers constructed barricades from furniture and used fire extinguishers to slow soldiers breaking through the windows.

By 1 am, enough parliamentarians had reached the chamber to form a quorum, and they unanimously exercised their constitutional right to revoke Yoon’s martial law. For the next 10 days, citizens surrounded the parliament despite freezing temperatures—chanting, singing K-pop songs, waving light sticks, and calling for Yoon’s impeachment. The protests grew to over a million people by December 14, when the assembly impeached him. Yoon’s party lost the presidential election the following summer, and he is now serving a life sentence for insurrection.

Here are five key lessons for the US from South Korea.

1. Elections matter all the more under a would-be dictator.

Because South Korea’s opposition party had won major gains in the April 2024 election, it was already blocking some of Yoon’s initiatives before he declared martial law. Party leaders had defied previous attempts to silence them, and their leadership on the night of the attempted coup was critical. Because overwhelming vote margins are our best guarantee of securing our elections, we can’t let fear of Trump’s interference deter us from doing all we can to get people to vote.

2. Act quickly to confront attempted seizures of power.

As soon as Yoon declared martial law, activists and opposition party leaders called on people to protest. Had they waited, martial law would have become the status quo, making protest much harder.

For the US midterms, most points of contestation will be local: Citizens and local governments need to protect polling places and vote-counting centers, including having police protect them from disruption. Groups like the League of United Latin American Citizens are suing in advance to block ICE from polling places, state attorneys general and secretaries of state preparing emergency legal briefs, and the Brennan Center has published checklists refuting potential administration arguments. But massive, immediate nonviolent protest will also be essential.

3. Encourage security forces to stay loyal to the Constitution.

South Koreans who confronted the martial law forces said many soldiers showed open or subtle resistance to the coup. For example, soldiers didn’t actively repel the crowds in front of the parliament or remove the aides who were resisting inside. A major and a sergeant who were ordered to issue ammunition refused, saying that replenishing it would take too long. Officers commanding two military buses ordered their troops to stay aboard rather than fully deploy. A sergeant refused flight approval for a special forces transport helicopter. When one unit was ordered to seize computer servers to prevent a parliamentary vote, they delayed by stopping to eat ramen noodles at a nearby convenience store.

Ordinary citizens also urged soldiers to refuse unlawful orders. Former special forces members invoked their shared backgrounds with the soldiers tasked with putting down the protests. Protesters asked soldiers to remember the 1980 Gwangju massacre and not support a dictatorship.

In our own context, it mattered enormously that six US senators and House members with military and national security backgrounds created last November’s video on the duty to refuse illegal orders. We need to keep highlighting this duty to service members. We hope we’ll never reach a moment like South Korea’s, but if we do, we shouldn’t assume that the entire US military will do Trump’s bidding.

4. Prevent the coup’s initiators from consolidating power.

The day after the attempted coup in South Korea, civil society organizations held press conferences demanding the president’s arrest. Daily protests outside the National Assembly culminated in the demonstration by over a million people on December 14, pressuring legislators to impeach Yoon. Protests then shifted to the presidential residence and the Constitutional Court, demanding Yoon’s arrest. Labor unions demonstrated at the offices of legislators who refused to support impeachment and created a website to swamp those legislators’ phones with text messages. A widely circulated PowerPoint debunked Yoon’s arguments and offered counter-slogans.

The lesson for the United States is that power gets consolidated in stages, and every stage that’s blocked makes the next stage easier to prevent. Legal challenges are essential, but so are the actions of ordinary citizens. For example, Indivisible and No Kings have been conducting Hands Off the Vote trainings and recruiting poll monitors, and the long-standing 866-OUR-VOTE national hotline is expanding its presence. Veteran nonviolent organizers the Freedom Trainers have mapped out how people can respond to multiple scenarios, from preelection attempts to suppress the vote to attempts to block vote certification.

5. Create festive, diverse forms of resistance.

As South Korea’s demonstrations kept growing, K-pop light sticks replaced the traditional candles as the protest symbol. The sticks gave the rallies the feel of a concert, as protesters turned K-pop hits into chants. Musicians and artists showed up, adding to the festive atmosphere. #3632. Could Mississippi Really Send a Democrat to the Senate?

📅 22 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Think about the races that are likely to determine control of the Senate in this year’s midterm elections. The chances are pretty high that the Mississippi Senate contest doesn’t feature on your list. After all, the last time the state sent a Democrat to the Senate was in 1982. But a growing number of observers on the ground in that deepest of Deep South states believe there’s more than a fighting chance that Democratic nominee Scott Colom, the district attorney for Mississippi’s 16th Circuit, could unseat sitting GOP Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith.

Hyde-Smith, whose lead in recent polls ranges from 3 to 6 percent, has what critics might call “baggage.” In November 2018, she raised a storm of controversy by stating that if she were invited to a public hanging, she would want a front-row seat. In a state with an ugly history of racist mob killings, this was widely taken as a shout-out to vigilante lynching. More recently, Hyde-Smith has hailed both the Supreme Court’s evisceration of the Voting Rights Act and the stampede toward mid-decade redistricting in the South, aimed largely at erasing Black-majority congressional districts. She has also been a particularly vociferous Trump supporter. She failed to stand up to Trump on tariffs, which have hit farmers in rural states such as Mississippi particularly hard, and she uttered not a peep of protest against last year’s “one big, beautiful bill,” even though the bill’s vast cuts to Medicaid and SNAP hit a poor state like Mississippi, which gets far more back in dollars from federal programs than it sends to DC in taxes, particularly hard. More than 100,000 of the state’s residents will lose Medicaid in the coming years because of the bill that Hyde-Smith voted for; tens of thousands of Mississippians will also lose their SNAP benefits, and hundreds of local grocery stores, reliant on income from customers using SNAP, will be put at risk of going under.

In fact, the only thing on which Hyde-Smith has distanced herself (albeit ever-so-mildly) from Trump is his recent decision to flood the beef market with imports of cheap beef—a move that, as a cattle farmer herself, Hyde-Smith knows is likely to hurt the American cattle industry.

Historically, Mississippi, whose population is roughly 37 percent African American, has been one of the most racially divided states in the country. For a hundred years after the Civil War, it was the epicenter of the Jim Crow South, and the Mississippi Democratic Party was a redoubt of segregationist white politics. In the civil rights era, as the national Democratic Party embraced the Voting Rights Act and other reforms that, cumulatively, dismantled the Jim Crow infrastructure, the parties flipped: Increasingly, whites in Mississippi aligned with a GOP that was veering ever more rightward, while the African American electorate moved into the Democratic Party’s camp. The divisions have remained so rigid in recent decades that, even as the African American electorate expanded, the white majority’s overwhelming adherence to the GOP ensured continued Republican dominance in statewide elections.

But the electoral math has been slowly changing in recent years. Since 2018, the size of GOP victories in statewide races has frequently shrunken to a few tens of thousands of votes. In 2023, incumbent Republican Governor Tate Reeves won reelection by only 27,000 votes—the state’s closest gubernatorial race since 1999. “We’ve been chipping away over the last more than half-decade,” Charles Taylor, executive director of the Mississippi NAACP, told The Nation. “Mississippians are fed up. That’s going to translate at the ballot box. There’s a lot of voter education going on. Voters in Mississippi and across the country are seeing the midterm elections as an existential crisis.”

In an election year in which many GOP voters are increasingly uncomfortable with the country’s direction under Trump, and with African American voters hyper-motivated to vote in the wake of the recent assaults on voting rights from the Supreme Court and a string of Southern state legislatures, Democrats have an opportunity to over-perform in the November Senate election. “We have a fighting chance here,” says Taylor. “And the country will be surprised by what happens.”

In a year with high African American voter participation—Democratic turnout in the 2026 primary was up 90 percent compared with the previous electoral cycle—only 20 percent of white voters would need to vote for Colom for the Democrat to flip the Senate seat. Given the conservative bent of white Mississippians, it’s still a long shot, but certainly not an impossibility. “There are a lot of social justice and civil rights [themes] bubbling up in Mississippi and throughout the South, and one of those has to do with what the US Supreme Court did,” Mississippi-based civil rights attorney Carroll Rhodes explained. “There’s a lot of hostility and anger and angst—and motivation within the African American community to vote and not to lose their right to vote. There’s probably going to be a bigger turnout than usual.”

“The demographics have been trending in this direction,” Derrick Johnson, president and CEO of the NAACP, told The Nation. “The current economic climate and restlessness with policies that have not worked, all of those things—people see it is possible to win a statewide race.” For Johnson, the confluence of national and state issues represents a potential tipping point for Mississippi. “Any candidate that speaks up to an administration in this moment who are taking resources out of Mississippians’ pockets will do well. The quality of life for Mississippians is not improving. We don’t have a single Fortune 500 company headquartered in this state. People are looking for a change.”

Colom, an African American attorney whose mother was a judge and father was a Republican who worked for Ronald Reagan, believes he can tap into that desire for a new politics. “We’re a state that can’t afford the corruption we’re seeing in DC right now,” he told The Nation. “Our farmers are already starting to feel the tariffs; fertilizer prices are spiking because of the war in Iran; and now the president is importing cheap beef. To me, what’s happened is a betrayal. They told the farmers, ‘We’re taking your votes for granted.’ It’s so stark, and the betrayal is so personal.”

Colom believes that in the lead-up to the election he can erase Hyde-Smith’s small but persistent lead in the polls. “A lot of people are going to be surprised,” he said. “But I’m not going to be surprised, and a lot of people in Mississippi won’t be surprised. You defeat the corruption by showing up and using your power in a moment when they’re trying to take it away.”
