#2179. Why HAS football gone soft on diving? It appears that 'simulation' is a plague we have chosen to live with

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Published: 20:31 BST, 19 October 2026 | Updated: 23:20 BST, 19 October 2026

‘It just wasn’t a penalty for me.’ Football fans have become accustomed to hearing managers say this in post-match interviews. They’re just not particularly used to hearing it from the man whose side profited from the decision.

Fair play, then, to Mark Wilson. Partick Thistle’s head coach was charitable enough to concede that the spot kick awarded to his side in Friday’s Scottish Championship match at Ayr United – a decision that ultimately decided the match – should not have been given.

‘I need to be honest because everybody’s bored of managers coming out and going, “Oh well, I didn’t see it”,’ he added. ‘I did see it. I said to the fourth official at the time, “I don’t think that’s a penalty.” And he says, “Well, we’re kind of blocked [in our view] here”.

‘We are, but I think you can kind of tell, can’t you? When you’ve been in the game a long time, you can tell what’s a foul and what’s not a foul.’

The incident in question occurred on the stroke of half-time, Thistle’s Seb Drozd tumbling inside the box – in instalments, it must be noted – under pressure from Ayr’s Liam Dick.

Standing just yards away and with a perfect view of it all, referee Matthew MacDermid had no hesitation in pointing to the spot, much to the incredulity of, well, just about everybody who saw it.

Partick Thistle front man Seb Drozd was captured smirking on the turf after his tumble

Commentating on the match for the BBC, James McFadden said: ‘I’m still struggling to see anything wrong there.’ Former Celtic striker Chris Sutton described it as ‘a contender for BBC 606’s Simulation Game’. The Ref’s View podcast, hosted by former officials, called it a ‘horrific decision’, adding: ‘Matthew MacDermid has been conned there.’

Ayr boss Gary Naysmith was understandably seething, not least because his side had been on the receiving end of a similar injustice against Dunfermline Athletic a week earlier.

‘I’ve now seen it back more than once and I think it’s another really poor decision that has gone against us,’ said the former Scotland international. ‘I think it’s a dive and I think the one last week was a dive.’

In a league where fine margins are routinely the difference between challenging for promotion and avoiding relegation, these two decisions – and the subsequent goals scored – could yet prove to be season-defining for Ayr, and potentially even affect the future of Naysmith, his staff and some of their players.

Referee Matthew MacDermid didn't cover himself in glory with the preposterous penalty call

And whilst referee MacDermid has rightly fielded considerable criticism for his part in it all – this is far from the whistler’s first controversy – the incident raises a further, more important question.

Where, for example, is the contempt for Drozd, who lay smirking on the Somerset Park turf after the penalty was awarded? His reckoning may – and should – yet come in the form of retrospective sanctions. If Thistle boss Wilson wanted to polish his own halo, he could even punish Drozd himself. A week’s wages would surely remind his squad of the standards required.

But Drozd’s tumble is merely a symptom of a bigger, enduring problem.

Once upon a time, to dive was to imperil your reputation. Fans, players, pundits – they didn’t so much take a dim view of the behaviour as regard it with unambiguous disgust. Those caught in the act were forever tainted; not quite pariahs but besmirched, for sure.

Thistle boss Mark Wilson was honest in the aftermath but should look to fine his errant player

Sometime between then and now, however, diving has become a plague that we have apparently chosen to live with, to tolerate instead of eradicate, and nobody knows exactly when or why.

Maybe it was around the time Jurgen Klinsmann laundered his reputation for such antics with a supposedly self-deprecating celebratory dive after scoring his first goal for Tottenham Hotspur in 1994, a stunt that immediately endeared the German striker to fickle fans. ‘He might be prone to cheating but at least he’s got a sense of humour,’ they cooed.

Maybe it was when the first referee dispensed with IFAB protocol and elected not to book a player for trying to con them. Maybe it was when ‘simulation’ – the acceptable face of diving, its sanitised, much cuddlier identity – transcended the rulebook and became part of the sport’s uber-trendy modern lexicon, gleefully tossed around by podcasters and pontificators galore.

Regardless, diving is cheating. It’s deliberate, disrespectful and dishonest. Unlike other forms of sporting chicanery, it should also be relatively straightforward to police.

Intent, for example, is often easier to discern. For what reason, other than an attempt to deceive, would players throw themselves to the ground, arms flailing and faces contorting as though they’d stared directly into the Ark of the Covenant? By dint of their inherently laughable theatricality, the most blatant dives are easy to distinguish from a stumble or a trip, particularly for properly trained officials.

Jurgen Klinsmann made diving fashionable by making it his signature celebration in his heyday

And yet it feels like it’s not regarded as the offence it once was. Where referees once flashed yellow cards, they now mostly motion to the players responsible to ‘get up’; a tsk-tsk in lieu of a caution.

By the midway point of last season, and despite a ‘crackdown’ by PGMOL, the body responsible for professional match officials that now goes by Pro Ref, there had been only 16 bookings for simulation in the English Premier League.

Call me cynical, but the implication that just 16 dives warranted punishment across 190 matches – 285 whole hours of football – feels far-fetched. Still, at least it was an improvement on the equivalent period the previous season, by which point only seven dives had been punished with a caution.

At a time when football appears hell-bent on tackling other forms of cheating – hello, Manchester City – it would do well to tackle all forms of it. They don’t all require litigation and thousand-dollar-an-hour lawyers. A consistent application of the game’s very own rules will suffice.

On the back of that, we asked our experts for their favourite goal they have ever witnessed live...

Zlatan's overhead kick from about 35 yards for Sweden against England. Simply the most Zlatan moment you could imagine. The opening of a new stadium in Stockholm came with a general feeling of underappreciation in the media for Zlatan's enduring qualities. Me included to be honest.

Zlatan Ibrahimovic's amazing strike towards the end of the match sealed Sweden's 4-2 win

I lost my voice, picked up a few bruises in the celebrations, and can still picture it as vividly now as I did at the time. Technique, context, euphoria, the goal had it all.

#2163. “Compete against the best” – Kluivert speaks about Milan history, Bournemouth’s growth and turning point

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Son of former AC Milan striker Patrick Kluivert, Justin Kluivert spoke ahead of Bournemouth’s meeting against the European giants.

It’s a dream match-up for Bournemouth, a chance to feature on the world stage and a real testament to their rise in recent years. 20 years ago, they finished five points above the relegation spots in League Two. 10 years ago, it was five points above the relegation spots in the Premier League.

A fantastic footballing story, this will be a pinnacle (so far) of their rise up the ranks and Milan cannot allow it to be a game that’s remembered fondly. As seen in Ruben Amorim’s press conference, the English press are paying a lot of attention and he, aside from anyone else, needs to prove something.

As much as it’s a big chance for the Cherries, it’s a bigger chance for the Rossoneri.

Speaking in his press conference last night, the Dutch attacker spoke about the history of the two clubs, his own relationship with the Diavolo and the game itself, as MilanNews relays.

What’s the atmosphere like for you players the day before an occasion like this?

“It’s going to be a fantastic match against an opponent who’s doing very well this season and has a great history. So yes, I’m sure the fans are looking forward to this match with great anticipation.”

Has there been a turning point in the locker room?

“Yes, 100%. I think we were a bit unlucky at the start of the season: we had played some good games, then the one against Chelsea was a poor performance for us. I’d call it a small wake-up call, and our reaction afterwards was excellent.

“We want to continue on this path and we feel good. We’re also recovering players, including me, we’re doing well, and this will certainly give the team a further boost.”

Did anyone on the staff or Marco Rose talk to the players about the history between Milan and Bournemouth? About the fact that your red and black stripes in the ’70s were inspired by Milan, as was your nickname?

“We haven’t talked about it, but it’s really nice to know! But yes, tomorrow will be a real battle of the Rossoneri shirts.”

“They have several players who have played a lot of Premier League games, so they know our league and what Bournemouth are capable of. It’s up to us to show them that again tomorrow on the pitch, bringing the intensity we usually show at home, if not even greater.

“As I said, it will be an extraordinary match, especially because I know they’re doing very well in Serie A. It will undoubtedly be a fantastic match.”

You already faced Milan when you were at Roma…

“Yes, I’ve played against them and I’ve always known that they’re a huge club with a great history, that has always had great players and continues to have them. So it will be a great challenge for us, but I think it will definitely be a great challenge for them too.”

Why have Bournemouth been so much more effective in the Europa League than in the Premier League so far?

“Well, we all know that the Premier League is the highest level in the world, 100%. The physical impact of the Premier League is the best around. However, as I was saying, we’ve played some excellent games: if you look at the start of the season, for example, at Manchester City, we held on until the end, only giving in at the end.

“And I think the first few games have all gone a bit like that. The only game we really failed to achieve was the one against Chelsea, but the reaction afterwards was excellent, and we’ll start from there.”

“Absolutely, 100%! I’m really happy to be back in shape. I’ve already got minutes in my legs in the last two games and now I feel great. These are the games everyone wants to play in and where you want to prove your worth.

“As I was saying, we’re facing Milan and then Manchester United: these are the games you love, the ones you’ve dreamed about since you were a kid and for which you don’t even need any special motivation or encouragement.

What are your memories of this competition, having experienced it over the years?

“I remember my first six months and immediately finding myself in the Europa League final with Ajax. We lost to United, but it’s an extraordinary feeling. And even now that I’m older and haven’t played in other finals, I know how special that moment is and how wonderful it is to reach milestones like that.

“It’s something I really want to achieve with Bournemouth this year too, and I firmly believe we can do it if we stick together as a group. I have no doubt about that.”

Is this belief perhaps also based on the level of the teams you’ve faced so far?

“It’s simply the blind faith I have in our team. I know what we can do, how far we can go: there are extraordinary teams competing in Europe, but I consider ourselves one of those great teams, and the quality we have in our squad is exceptional.

“Of course, we need everyone available because there are so many matches scheduled, between the Premier League, the cups, and the Europa League, so everyone’s contribution is needed, and it’s excellent news that almost everyone is back.

“So yes, we can truly compete with our heads held high in every tournament and competition.”

You have a very strong family connection to AC Milan, because your father played there. What does he tell you about AC Milan as a club and his experience there?

“Yes, the time he spent there has remained in his heart, obviously. As I said before, we’re talking about a club with a great history; even though he didn’t play there for many years, he always speaks with immense love about AC Milan, and I can completely understand that.”

When the Europa League draw was held and you saw the match schedule, do you remember your emotions and those of the locker room when you saw AC Milan as your opponents? Was there great excitement in seeing such a renowned name come to play at the “Vitality Stadium”?

“Yes, absolutely! There are some clubs where you immediately say, ‘Okay, these are the clubs that can aim to win the cup or at least be among the best in the tournament.’ And AC Milan is one of them. But you always want to compete against the best teams in the competition, so we were happy to face them and we’re ready.

“As I said before, we’re hungry for big matches, and it’s even more wonderful to play it at the Vitality Stadium, our home. It will be a special night for the fans.”

Will facing one of the most prestigious clubs in football history be a further symbol of Bournemouth’s growth and ambition, as well as the progress they’ve made in recent years?

“Yes, I’d say so. One of the reasons I came here is precisely the ambition the club has shown. Not just in words, but also in deeds, with the kind of players they’ve signed and continue to sign. They’ve kept their word, and the rest is up to us on the pitch.

“Tomorrow will be a night where people will enjoy themselves and say, ‘We’re playing against AC Milan!’ You know, they’re not used to nights like this here, so it will be truly special. And I hope that, by winning, we can concretely demonstrate the growth we’re on.”

Comparing the histories of AC Milan and Bournemouth, the comparison almost doesn’t hold up. But if we talk about the ambition of this club, the progress made, and the calibre of players Bournemouth has in its squad…

“And I think that’s precisely the strength of our team, and it has been for the past few years: we believe that, no matter who we’re playing against, we can play our game and win against anyone. That’s what makes us strong.

“Of course, you can’t win every single game, but our mentality is this: we know we can beat anyone, and that’s why we’re able to play at such a high level at times.”

You said you spoke a bit with your father about AC Milan. Can you tell us more about that conversation? What did he tell you about AC Milan that makes it such a special club?

“He told me that it’s all about passion there, especially in Italy. People live football viscerally; for them, football is everything. If you do well, they treat you like a king: you can go anywhere and they don’t charge you anything!

“But there’s also the other side of the coin: if things go badly, you’re the worst of all, and they make you pay dearly! Jokes aside, we’re talking about a fan base and a people with an immense passion, for whom football is everything, and that’s something beautiful.”

Are there any AC Milan players you remember watching as a child or who inspired you? Besides your father, of course…

“My father is obviously one of those, but AC Milan has seen so many extraordinary champions pass through: I’m thinking of Franco Baresi, what he did there was incredible! Or Alessandro Costacurta, who has a wonderful history with the Rossoneri…

“I could name an endless list of legends, like Balotelli and Seedorf, who have worn that shirt. Great names, great champions.”

You’ve already impressed one of your father’s former clubs since joining Bournemouth, scoring a hat-trick against Newcastle. Could something similar happen tomorrow night?

“Let’s try, why not! You know, that’s exactly why we’re out there. Like I said, I want to be decisive for the team, and that means scoring goals, sacrificing myself on the pitch, and playing with incredible intensity.

“That’s what I always bring to my game, and tomorrow will be no exception. Let’s try to score three- why not!”
