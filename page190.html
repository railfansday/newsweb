#4127. This mysterious mushroom makes people see hundreds of tiny humans

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

People living thousands of miles apart have reported an extraordinarily strange experience after eating certain wild mushrooms. They begin seeing tiny human figures that appear to move through the real world, walking around objects, climbing over surfaces, and interacting with their surroundings.

These visions are known as Lilliputian hallucinations, named after the miniature inhabitants of "Gulliver's Travels," who stand just 6 inches tall. For decades, researchers suspected that cultural beliefs and folklore might explain why people in different regions described such similar experiences.

Now, a new study provides evidence that the phenomenon has a biological explanation.

Researchers at the University of Utah (U) used DNA sequencing to identify Lanmaoa asiatica as the mushroom responsible for these unusual hallucinations in Southwest China and the northern Philippines. Surprisingly, their investigation found no evidence of any known psychoactive mushroom compounds, including psilocybin.

The findings suggest that the mushroom may produce an entirely unfamiliar hallucinogenic substance. Identifying that chemical could help scientists better understand neurological disorders, unusual visual experiences, and the processes through which the brain creates our perception of reality.

The research was conducted by Colin Domnauer, a doctoral student at the U, and Bryn Dentinger, a mycologist affiliated with the university and the Natural History Museum of Utah. Their findings were published in the journal Mycologia.

In an interview with @theU, Domnauer explained how researchers traced the mushroom's identity, what makes its effects so unusual, and why the discovery could have implications far beyond the study of fungi.

A Century-Old Mystery of Tiny Human Hallucinations

The scientific mystery dates back to 1934, when Western researchers learned that people in Papua New Guinea sometimes experienced vivid visions of miniature humans after eating wild mushrooms. The bizarre effect became known as "mushroom madness."

During the 1950s and 1960s, anthropologists and mushroom specialists traveled to Papua New Guinea to investigate. Their work pointed toward several possible species belonging to the Boletaceae (bolete) family.

However, chemical testing failed to reveal any substances that could explain the hallucinations. Samples were even examined by Albert Hofmann, the scientist who first isolated psilocybin and discovered LSD. He, too, was unable to identify an active ingredient.

With no chemical explanation available, some researchers began questioning whether the mushrooms were responsible at all. They suggested that the visions might instead arise from cultural expectations or traditional beliefs. By the end of the 1960s, scientific investigation of the phenomenon had largely stopped.

The mystery resurfaced during the 1990s, when people in Yunnan, Southwest China, reported similar hallucinations after eating bolete mushrooms. Although the precise species remained uncertain, researchers suspected a recently described mushroom called Lanmaoa asiatica.

Scientists Identify the Mushroom Behind the Strange Visions

Another important clue appeared in 2024, when Domnauer learned of comparable experiences among an Indigenous community in the remote Northern Cordillera region of the Philippines.

Community members described a wild edible mushroom called "Sedesdem" that could sometimes cause people to see miniature human figures. Similar mushrooms were known locally as "Nonda" in Papua New Guinea and "Jian shou qing" in Yunnan.

These mushrooms have an important place in local food traditions, but consuming them without thoroughly cooking them can trigger the unusual hallucinations.

The Philippine accounts represented a third independent report of wild mushrooms causing remarkably similar visions of tiny people.

To investigate, Domnauer traveled to Yunnan and the Philippines, interviewed local residents, and collected specimens of L. asiatica along with other mushrooms belonging to the Lanmaoa genus.

The fieldwork included the first scientific survey of fungi in the northern Philippines. Researchers then analyzed DNA from the collected mushrooms to establish their identities and evolutionary relationships.

The results revealed that the mushrooms called Jian shou qing in China and Sedesdem in the Philippines were both L. asiatica.

This was an unexpected finding because scientists had previously believed the species occurred only in China.

Dentinger also visited Papua New Guinea in hopes of collecting specimens from the location where the mystery originally began. Unfortunately, unusually dry weather made it impossible to obtain samples for genetic comparison.

Although the Papua New Guinea mushroom remains unverified, the evidence from China and the Philippines provides a compelling connection. Three geographically separated cultures have described the same highly specific hallucinations, and DNA testing has linked two of those cases to a single mushroom species.

That consistency strongly suggests a shared chemical and neurological explanation rather than unrelated cultural influences.

Hallucinations of Tiny Humans That Seem Remarkably Real

What makes these hallucinations especially unusual is their striking similarity across different accounts.

People typically describe seeing dozens or even hundreds of miniature figures, generally measuring between 3 and 30 centimeters tall. The figures appear with extraordinary clarity, often seeming as solid and convincing as actual people.

According to Domnauer, approximately 90% of those experiencing the visions describe figures resembling elves, clowns, or other fairy-like characters, often wearing brightly colored clothing.

Even more surprising is the way these imaginary figures seem to behave.

Rather than floating through walls or ignoring their surroundings, they appear to follow familiar physical rules. They walk around obstacles, move across ordinary objects, and sometimes tumble from the edges of tables.

Domnauer recalled one particularly unusual account involving someone eating soup. The person reported seeing tiny humans leap from a spoon into the bowl, where they began swimming. When the person took another spoonful, the miniature figures reportedly remained visible inside their mouth.

Such detailed interactions distinguish these visions from many other forms of drug-induced hallucination.

A Mysterious Psychedelic Compound Unlike Any Known Drug

Scientists also investigated whether L. asiatica contains any of the chemicals already known to cause psychedelic experiences.

Using both chemical testing and genetic analysis, the researchers searched for established psychoactive compounds found in other mushroom species.

The absence of familiar hallucinogens was particularly intriguing because the mushroom's effects differ so dramatically from those associated with known psychedelic drugs.

Researchers now suspect that L. asiatica produces an unidentified psychoactive substance responsible for the tiny human hallucinations.

The compound has not yet been isolated or chemically identified, so its exact nature and mechanism remain unknown.

Discovering it could open an entirely new avenue of research into how certain chemicals alter visual perception.

What the Mushroom Could Reveal About the Human Brain

Although L. asiatica provides an unusually consistent trigger for Lilliputian hallucinations, the phenomenon is not limited to mushroom consumption.

Stories involving miniature humanlike beings appear throughout folklore around the world. Similar hallucinations have also been documented in people experiencing alcohol withdrawal, dementia, macular degeneration, and other neurological conditions.

These connections suggest that the visions may arise from a fundamental feature of the way the human brain processes visual information.

However, scientists still do not fully understand the neurological mechanisms responsible for these experiences or how best to treat them when they occur in medical settings.

This is where the mysterious mushroom could become especially valuable.

Unlike the neurological and medical conditions in which Lilliputian hallucinations occur relatively rarely, L. asiatica can produce the phenomenon in a more consistent and recognizable way.

If researchers can isolate the chemical responsible, they may gain a useful tool for investigating the brain circuits and processes that create these unusually detailed visual experiences.

Such research could eventually improve scientific understanding of hallucinations associated with neurological diseases.

The research team's next major objective is to isolate and identify the suspected psychedelic compound.

DNA Sequencing Reveals Four Previously Unknown Mushroom Species

The investigation also produced important discoveries about fungal biodiversity.

Rather than studying L. asiatica alone, Domnauer and Dentinger conducted the first comprehensive genomic analysis of the Lanmaoa genus.

They examined 53 mushroom specimens collected from natural habitats and preserved in fungarium collections around the world.

Genetic sequencing revealed 17 species within the group, including four species previously unknown to science.

Remarkably, two of those newly identified species had already been preserved in existing collections in the United States. Their distinct identities had gone unrecognized until researchers examined their DNA.

The analysis also indicated that L. asiatica is the only species in the genus currently known to produce psychoactive effects.

Scientists are now working to reconstruct the group's evolutionary history. They hope to determine how the unusual chemical trait developed and whether closely related mushrooms might produce comparable substances.

The findings also demonstrate the scientific value of museum collections. Specimens preserved for years can contain biological discoveries that become apparent only when newer analytical techniques are applied.

For Domnauer, the hidden diversity found in these collections is a reminder that many species and potentially important biological characteristics remain undiscovered, even among organisms that scientists have already collected.

Exploring Fungi Could Lead to New Medicines and Discoveries

The broader implications extend well beyond unusual hallucinations.

Fungi remain one of the least thoroughly explored groups of organisms, and researchers believe their chemical diversity could provide valuable clues about biology, medicine, and the nervous system.

One of the most famous examples is penicillin, the antibiotic originally obtained from a fungus that transformed the treatment of bacterial infections and became one of the most consequential medical discoveries of the last century.

Scientists hope continued exploration of fungal biodiversity could reveal other medically useful compounds and previously unknown biological processes.

The mysterious effects of L. asiatica illustrate just how much remains to be learned about the fungal kingdom. A mushroom long consumed by communities in Asia may contain a substance capable of revealing new information about the human brain and how it creates conscious experience.

How leaders allocate their limited time and attention is an important question in economics. This paper constructs and analyzes a novel data set covering the daily calendars of three Federal Reserve Chairs, Ben Bernanke, Janet Yellen, and Jerome Powell, containing more than 20,000 calendar entries. We document several patterns. First, all Fed Chairs spend more than 30% of their documented meetings with Fed staff, highlighting the crucial role of economists in the Fed’s decision process. #4155. Popular cannabis sleep gummies may have a hidden dark side

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Cannabinol (CBN) has become a popular ingredient in sleep supplements, often promoted as a natural, gentle way to relax and get a better night's rest. However, new research from the University of Connecticut suggests that this widely available cannabis compound may have risks that consumers should know about.

Scientists found that repeated exposure to CBN caused mice to develop signs of physical dependence. The findings, published in the November issue of the journal Drug and Alcohol Dependence, raise questions about the safety of using CBN regularly, particularly over long periods.

"CBN is everywhere right now. Gummies, tinctures, capsules, and it's marketed as a safe, herbal sleep supplement," says Steven Kinsey, professor in UConn's Elisabeth DeLuca School of Nursing. "Our findings suggest consumers should be aware repeated use may carry risks."

Popular Sleep Aid Shows Effects Similar to THC

CBN is a naturally occurring compound in cannabis, along with THC (tetrahydrocannabinol) and CBD (cannabidiol). Although CBN is generally considered less psychoactive than THC, the researchers discovered that it affects some of the same biological systems and can produce several key signs associated with drug dependence.

In the experiments, mice received CBN twice a day for three consecutive days. As treatment continued, the animals became less responsive to the compound, indicating that they had developed tolerance. When CBN was withdrawn, the mice also displayed physical symptoms resembling withdrawal, including head twitching and paw fluttering. These movements are comparable to the shaking that people can experience during drug withdrawal.

Another experiment revealed a surprising connection between CBN and THC. Mice previously trained to recognize and respond to THC's psychoactive effects reacted similarly when given CBN. This suggests that the two compounds produce overlapping subjective effects, at least in mice.

Together, the results indicate that CBN, despite its reputation as a milder cannabis compound, interacts with some of the same receptor systems that contribute to THC dependence.

Scientists Raise Concerns About CBN Sleep Supplements

The findings are particularly relevant because CBN products have become increasingly common, even though scientists still have limited information about their long-term effects.

The lead author of the study, Olivia Vanegas '26 Ph.D., notes "The marketing of minor cannabinoids is pretty wild right now. There are so many therapeutic claims about these drugs, but we really don't know much about their potential risks, especially with repeated use."

CBN is now widely promoted as a sleep aid, yet important questions remain unanswered. Researchers do not fully understand how CBN affects sleep quality or whether taking it repeatedly at doses commonly found in supplements could lead to addiction in humans.

There is also a historical reason for caution. Over the past century, several medications initially promoted as helpful sleep treatments were eventually found to carry serious risks of dependence and habit formation.

Potential Benefits Come With Unanswered Safety Questions

The researchers also identified potentially useful properties of CBN. The compound demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects, including at lower doses that did not produce psychoactive effects.

However, the experiments revealed another possible concern. During a simulated bacterial challenge, CBN reduced the body's ability to defend itself. This weakening of immune protection could make it easier for an infection to develop, similar to how bacteria can enter through a cut on the foot and cause illness.

These findings suggest that CBN may have medical applications, but scientists need a clearer understanding of its benefits, potential side effects, and appropriate doses before determining how it can be used safely.

Importantly, the experiments were conducted in mice, so the findings do not establish that CBN causes dependence or withdrawal in people. Additional studies are needed to understand how the compound affects humans, especially when taken frequently, at higher doses, or over extended periods.

"CBN may still have therapeutic value," he says. "But until we know more about dosing and long-term effects, the message for consumers is simple: buyer beware."

Apple's first foldable has new screen dimensions, leaving apps with a lot of unused space or awkward cropping. It'll get better over time.

For some lucky Apple superfans, today is the day they get to unbox their iPhone Duo. (Others may have to wait until January.) But after formally transferring all their software, they’ll discover a troubling truth: Some of their favorite apps look bad, with awkwardly unused space.

Yet the message from CNET phone reviewers over the decades is reassuring. If precedent is anything to go by, updates will be coming to adjust apps to the iPhone Duo’s novel screen ratios, from the squat and wide front screen to the 4:3 inner display. Don’t worry — your apps will get better (and some are already tuned to the device’s dimensions).

That may be little comfort for Duo owners looking forlornly at Android foldables, which have had the better part of a decade on the market for app makers to adjust to the new formats. Despite having a similar passport-sized format to Apple’s foldable, most apps are sized correctly for the Samsung Galaxy Z Fold 8.

But that wasn’t the case with the first Samsung Galaxy Fold, which launched in early 2019, as CNET Editor at Large Andy Lanxon recalls.

“I had exactly the same experience when I used the first Samsung Galaxy Fold on the day it came out, with many apps appearing to be oddly squashed into that larger inner display,” Lanxon said. “Over time, both Android and foldable makers like Samsung and Google developed better software to accommodate foldables, and I’m confident that Apple won’t take long to patch up any shortcomings in this area.”

Indeed, it’s easy to forget that as the world’s first mainstream foldable, the original Galaxy Fold launched with plenty of display, hinge and other hardware problems that were ironed out over the years. Apple’s first foldable benefits from that refinement, launching with a minimal crease, a smooth and seemingly durable hinge, internal software suited to a folding screen (with FOMO-inspiring animations), IP68 dust- and water-resistance and other qualities that ease use.

The Duo also benefits from being a single design, as opposed to the various screen sizes and formats across all the Android foldables. The Google Pixel 11 Pro Fold and Galaxy Z Fold 8 Ultra, for instance, both have 6.5-inch outer screens and 8-inch inner screens, but the former is slightly wider yet shorter than the latter.

But it’s the Duo’s specific design that might also accelerate app adjustment, Lanxon notes: The outer display has the same aspect ratio as the larger inner display, something Apple highlighted during its Sept. 9 presentation launching the device. So developers only need to design one app size for the Duo that will work perfectly well on both displays.

Compare that to Samsung’s Galaxy Z Fold 8 Ultra, which pairs a tall and narrow 21:9 cover screen with an almost square 10:9 inner screen.

That’s somewhat reassuring, but what iPhone Duo owners likely want to know is: When will my apps get updated? To predict that, we look to the past — both the development of other foldables and the radical changes in Apple’s own iPhone line.

The iPhone X, revealed on stage in October 2017, shifted to an all-glass front panel and the notorious notch at the top, requiring apps to adjust.

Given how long smartphones have had all-glass front panels, it’s understandable that it takes time to adapt apps to foldable displays. But it’s easy to forget that app makers had to go through the same process before when phones finally ditched physical buttons below screens for the all-glass front to begin with.

“I remember when the iPhone X came out, and apps that hadn’t been optimized had black bars above and below,” said CNET Editor at Large Scott Stein, who reviewed the device back in 2017. “It meant all apps didn’t initially make the most of the new phone’s size.”

The Duo will have the same growing pains until more apps get optimized, Stein added — it takes time, and it’s part of what it means to be a hardware early adopter.

The original Samsung Galaxy Fold, launched in Feb. 2019.

It’s important to remember how complex it is to make software for a foldable, as explained by Jessica Dolcourt, CNET Group and CNET Labs vice president of content operations and commerce. When you open and close a foldable device, you’re asking your app to perform acrobatics, instantly leaping between internal and external displays while simultaneously expanding or contracting the view window and rearranging all the pixels.

Dolcourt spent over a decade reviewing phones for CNET, including the original Samsung Galaxy Fold in 2019, where she got her first taste of apps rendering badly.

“Native apps could be slow to load, but brought the vision of a foldable future to life. Third-party apps — which were most of them — struggled to catch up quickly and smoothly to the demands of a folding screen,” Dolcourt said. “At the outset, some were barely usable.”

Nowadays, most apps are designed for Android foldables, in part thanks to years of Google software updates specifically addressing flexible displays. The Duo will have to play catch-up.

But as Lanxon pointed out, Apple has deep relationships with developers of its apps, which sometimes launch on iOS first before heading to the Google Play Store. Apple will surely be working closely with its software partners to ensure they’re using the Duo’s proportions to the best advantage, Lanxon said.

When your apps, like Instagram, will be updated to fit the Duo’s screens is hard to gauge (especially given it took 15 years to get an iPad-native Instagram app), but developers have many more advantages than they did when adapting to the first Android foldables’ displays. Over time — and hopefully soon — the Duo will be even better to use as apps catch up with it.

Alexander ran some IDs and found no issues. The music wasn’t even that loud, he thought. He told them to have a good night, and then he left.

That encounter, in early October last year, was the first of many that Alexander and Martinez would have in tiny Ingram, Texas, a 1.5-square-mile town of 1,900 people beside the Guadalupe River. But it was the only one before Ingram signed an agreement with U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement that would roil the town and tear Martinez’s family apart.

“Her and I, at the beginning, I felt like we were running into each other all the time,” Alexander told us, in one of eight conversations over four months that offer a rare window into the thoughts of one of the local police officers on the front lines of the Trump administration’s deportation campaign.

In a town as small as Ingram, drivable in five minutes end to end, more run-ins were probably inevitable. Later that October, Alexander pulled over a work truck for rolling through a stop sign, and soon there Martinez was, demanding that he let her friends go rather than let immigration take them. He didn’t. He pulled over another driver in November who turned out to be her cousin, the father of the baby they celebrated at the baptism party. Alexander handed him over to immigration agents as well.

He pulled over Martinez herself a few weeks before she was due to give birth, she says, surprising her when he knew her name and the type of car she typically drove. He warned her that the car’s window tint was illegal and let her go. After that, he says, he caught a glimpse of her and her husband and their newborn daughter in the toy aisle at Walmart. Martinez told us she doesn’t think it was even her that day — her baby girl was then just days old, and she couldn’t imagine running errands with her so soon. But in Alexander’s memory, she was shopping for Christmas presents, just like him.

Alexander, who is in his mid-30s, with dirty blond hair already turning gray, says he didn’t come back to Ingram to help deport his neighbors. In his telling, he just ended up making it part of his job. The town was where he started his law-enforcement career years earlier, before he took a better-paying position with the county, as a sheriff’s deputy. But things recently started to fall apart, first in his marriage, next at the sheriff’s office: A 185-page internal investigation found that he had an affair before his divorce was final and used a county vehicle to carry it out, then lied about it all. He says the investigator called him an “embarrassment to the profession.” The sheriff’s office fired him. (Alexander said at the time, and again to us, that he did not have the affair.)

Ingram’s Police Department took him back just when its new chief — its eighth in five years — was under pressure from the City Council to do more: more traffic stops, more citations, more arrests, more numbers. Alexander says he told the chief that the sheriff’s office had a deal with ICE and that Ingram should look into it; maybe it could help the department grow. “Blame me,” he eventually told people. “It’s my fault in a way.”

Donald Trump had retaken the presidency with a promise to carry out “the largest deportation in the history of our country.” America was still figuring out what that meant. Finding and expelling up to 4% of the U.S. population — the 14 million residents who are estimated to be undocumented — wasn’t something a few thousand ICE agents could do on their own.

Underway in Chicago that fall, and soon in Minneapolis, was one version of how the administration could try to do it: masked agents from across the federal government surging into a liberal city to round up immigrants, beating back — even killing — the citizens who tried to stand in their path.

But a quieter kind of surge was spreading across the country at the same time. In September 2025, ICE announced new incentives for a partnership program known as 287(g). The program’s most aggressive form, the so-called task force model, which Ingram opted in to, enlists local police officers as deputy immigration agents, and the new incentives included a $100,000 vehicle stipend, a $7,500 equipment stipend for every participating officer, quarterly performance bonuses and funding to help cover officer salaries and overtime. “By joining forces with ICE,” the announcement read, “you’re not just gaining access to these unprecedented reimbursement opportunities — you’re becoming part of a national effort to ensure the safety of every American family.”

Alexander completed an online course with ICE a few weeks after the baptism party, qualifying him for time-and-a-half pay whenever he helped the agency pick up immigrants. Here in the Texas Hill Country, he soon became the local face of the national crackdown.

Ingram was just starting to recover from its worst natural disaster in a century, the flood that summer that killed at least 139 people up and down the Guadalupe River, including 25 girls at Camp Mystic. In the riverbed, towering bald cypress trees now stood at a lean, their branches swept back by a 30-foot surge of water and mud, their trunks still plastered with debris. As construction workers rebuilding the community traveled to their jobsites via Junction Highway, Ingram’s five-lane main thoroughfare, a black Charger began stopping them before they could get there.

Within three months of the baptism party, Alexander and Ingram’s other patrol officer had delivered at least 70 people to immigration agents, picking off what the police chief called a “dumbfounding” number of undocumented drivers. Within a year, they would hand over the equivalent of 10% of Ingram to ICE.

The weight of what Alexander was doing started to settle on him after Christmas. He says he was out patrolling as one of his new ICE handlers, a man in a ball cap and dark glasses, hovered nearby in an unmarked SUV. Around lunchtime, Alexander followed two Latino men in a 2008 GMC truck for several blocks before stopping them, they recall him saying, for failing to signal a turn.

A young man sat in the passenger seat. Alexander checked his papers along with the driver’s and determined that both were undocumented. The agent in the unmarked SUV rolled up behind them. Alexander realized who the man in the passenger seat was only after Martinez arrived in tears. It was her husband, Ulices Macias, the father of her newborn daughter.

“I don’t normally do this,” Martinez recalls Alexander telling her, “but I’m going to open up the car. I’m going to take the handcuffs off him.” He let the family have a moment together. In a video taken by Martinez’s younger sister, Alexander stood by, expressionless, as Martinez embraced Macias by the rear door of the Charger. He watched Macias cradle his 2-week-old baby girl, swaddled in a white blanket, and hug Martinez’s 4-year-old son, his stepson.

Then the ICE agent put the handcuffs back on Martinez’s husband, wrapped a chain around his waist and led him away. Alexander says Martinez threw herself to the ground and sobbed.

Images of the family he broke up shortly after Christmas would intrude on Alexander’s thoughts for months, he told us. But it didn’t keep him from doing his job. His next shift came a few days after he handed over Macias, and he was back in the Charger, back to stopping cars on Junction Highway.

Alexander says he didn’t always want to be a police officer. He describes himself as “kind of a chameleon.” He grew up in nearby Harper, tried a few different colleges without graduating and spent his 20s cycling though jobs in freight logistics, pest control and insurance.

Still seeking his calling, he arranged a ride-along with a police officer outside Austin. What sold him on policing was the suburban officer’s power to enforce the law as he saw fit. That day, they pulled over a woman who had a warrant out for her arrest. She was coming back from the store with children and groceries in the back seat.

“You can use your discretion,” Alexander recalls the officer telling him. Wouldn’t they be doing more harm if they took her? What would the kids do without their mother? And wouldn’t it all create more paperwork? The officer let her go. Alexander imagined himself making the same kinds of judgment calls. In 2021, he enrolled in the police academy.

Even before the floods, tensions were rising in Ingram. Hundreds of Mexican immigrants and their families had settled there in recent decades as an increasingly militarized border made it harder for seasonal ranch hands and other workers to cross back and forth. Latinos would come to make up 45% of its population, and some residents began affectionately calling Ingram “mini Guanajuato,” after the central Mexican state where many have roots. Yet when Alexander returned, Ingram’s City Council still consisted of six white men.

A council faction led by Claud Jordan, now the town’s mayor, and Rocky Hawkins, now the mayor pro tem, came to power in November 2020, in a revolt against a new sewer project. They abolished Ingram’s city administrator position and took direct control of the Police Department, cycling through chief after chief. Five former city employees say Hawkins regularly used racist slurs for Mexicans. Four say that he pressured the city to go after Latinos through code enforcement or targeted patrols, and two say that Jordan did, too. One of the employees recalls Hawkins complaining that Mexicans were “taking over Ingram.” (Hawkins and Jordan each declined to speak on the record or address detailed questions we sent them by email. In a brief text-message exchange, Hawkins said that none of it was right but declined to offer any specific concerns.)

These were the men the new chief, Dustin Breshears, had to answer to as ICE revived the task force program, which had been shut down for a dozen years until the day Trump returned to office in January 2025. ICE, under President Barack Obama, had phased out the program after some of the first sheriffs to participate in it, including Joe Arpaio of Maricopa County, Arizona, used it to disproportionately target Latinos in their communities. Investigations by the Department of Justice found that deputies in the task force program pulled over Latino drivers at up to nine times the rate of other drivers and that some retaliated violently against people who accused them of racial profiling. But now, under Trump, any law-enforcement agency, no matter how troubled or small, was encouraged to sign up.

Breshears began appearing at City Council meetings armed with statistics: number of arrests since the last meeting, number of traffic stops, number of cleared cases from the backlog of hundreds he inherited — and number of what he called “deportations.” (He later explained this was his imprecise shorthand for “immigration-related enforcement activities.”) At the council’s October 2025 meeting, held a couple of weeks after Alexander showed up at the Martinez family’s baptism party, Breshears reported a remarkable 162 traffic stops by Ingram’s two patrol officers — Alexander and Christian Perez, who was 25 — in just 20 days. (Perez declined to comment.) The officers made more stops in two months than the department had made in the entire previous year. The City Council gave Breshears an $18,000 raise.

Before Breshears arrived and Alexander returned in August 2025, the weekly blotter in The West Kerr Current, the local newspaper, documented the humdrum of community policing: loose dogs, neighborhood disputes, code violations. Then, suddenly, it was filled with drivers stopped for defective brake lights, damaged license plates, cracked windshields, illegal window tints or other unspecified “equipment violations.”

Breshears and Alexander strongly deny targeting Latinos. “It’s honestly not what’s happening,” Breshears says, noting that his half brother, wife and daughters have Mexican heritage. Ingram’s traffic stops were simply up across the board, he says, so more Latinos were getting stopped. He shared statistics showing that stops of Latinos were proportional to their share of the population.

Alexander, who on top of his roughly $70,000 base salary was now logging dozens of hours per month for ICE, told us the same. “I don’t care who’s in the car,” he says. “I really don’t.”

As the ICE program took hold in Ingram, warnings about Alexander and his black Charger — the department has several — spread through the Hill Country and far beyond. Groups on Facebook and WhatsApp seemed to track his every move. The Charger was outside Rita’s taco shop. The Charger was near Dollar General. The Charger was parked by the church. A member of one particularly active Facebook group texted him screenshots, and he scrolled through blurry photos of himself looming behind work trucks or leaning over car windows.

Many Latino motorists trying to reach worksites up the Guadalupe began driving an extra 90 minutes each day to avoid the section of Junction Highway that Alexander patrolled. Ranch hands began sleeping at their employers’ properties in nearby Hunt, coming to town only when they knew — or hoped — that Ingram’s officers would be off duty. Residents were rattled the day a Customs and Border Protection helicopter hovered above town and the elementary school went into lockdown as the police hunted for a Mexican man who had run from one of Alexander’s traffic stops.

One family notarized guardianship papers, in case the parents were detained and the children left behind. One woman swore Alexander showed up outside a memorial service and asked people for IDs. Another woman said he detained her and her husband when they returned home from church. Parties dwindled. Invite lists shrank. People began to move away, to Kerrville and San Antonio, and Alexander’s reputation reached as far as La Ceja, a village in the mountains of central Mexico, where an elderly resident said he and his neighbors were familiar with the Texas town’s police. “El Charger negro,” he said, with a knowing nod. He estimated that some 20 or 30 people in the village were recent deportees from Ingram.

Alexander’s fortunes seemed to rise as the Martinez family’s fell. With her husband gone, Joanna Martinez couldn’t afford payments on the trailer they shared. She and their infant and her 4-year-old son moved back into her childhood bedroom in her mother’s crowded home, where several relatives and their children also lived. She tried to stay connected with her husband, Macias, via WhatsApp calls while working as much as she could as a house cleaner to keep afloat. “I can’t lock myself up in my room and just cry,” Martinez says.

Winter for Martinez and her family brought a string of dark incidents. The January arrest of her cousin near the carwash as she drove to pick up her children from school. A canceled baby shower for her older sister, Jazmin, over a rumor that the Charger planned to show up. Another cousin, himself the father of a newborn, stopped and taken right in front of her mother’s house as Martinez watched.

A half dozen of the attendees from the October baptism party would soon be gone from Ingram, after being detained and deported or leaving to join relatives who had been. Some people had gotten wind that Alexander was making money off his enforcement. When a Spanish-language Facebook page shared a photo of him questioning the passenger of a beige Ford pickup, someone posted an animated image in the comments: a stack of green dollar bills growing taller and taller in an endless loop.

Alexander received his first ICE payments in early December. In January, as the country’s attention turned to the violent immigration crackdown in Minneapolis, he was often spending three or four hours a day and sometimes entire shifts working on behalf of the federal agency. By the time federal authorities announced a drawdown in Minneapolis on Feb. 12, Alexander had already made $3,264.46 from his task force hours, according to his pay stubs.

Ingram was by then one of more than 750 task force model partners around the country, up from not quite 500 when it signed up in October. At least 43 million U.S. residents, according to an analysis of ICE records by the legal data firm Titus Consulting, already lived in a county with an active task force agreement. Alexander was one of thousands of deputized officers in Texas alone.

In interviews, Alexander described the immigration system he was enforcing as “skewed” and “weird,” but he saw himself as largely separate from these concerns. “If you’re taking federal money to do a job,” he says, “I think you should do the job.”

Still, he seemed to struggle with the contradictions of his role. He told us he sometimes shared his lunch with the people he detained and that he let them hold on to their phones to call loved ones from the rear seat of the Charger. He handcuffed them with their hands in the front rather than the back so they could use their hands.

Martinez told us about a moment when she watched him detain one of her cousins. He allowed the man’s wife and children to hug him goodbye and waited until his 7-year-old daughter wasn’t looking to handcuff him. To Martinez, it seemed as if Alexander was trying to prove to her that he wasn’t a bad person. “But I’m not going to fall for it,” she says.

There was a protest in Ingram the following day against Trump’s mass deportations. Martinez decided to go. As she stood among a few dozen protesters waving a handmade sign with the name “J. Alexander” circled and crossed out, she saw him drive by again and again.

A few weeks later, Katie Winer, the leader of the group that staged the protest, was following the Charger after Alexander detained another immigrant in a traffic stop. He approached her car and threatened to arrest her if she followed him in the future, on charges of interfering with public duties. But he also seemed to want to talk, and she recorded him as they discussed what was going on in Ingram. “I think you guys are making a bigger deal of it than they are,” he said of the people he handed over to ICE. The only outliers were “loudmouths” like Martinez and “her little crew.”

Winer interjected that Martinez’s husband was the family’s breadwinner, and it was Alexander’s actions that tore them apart.

“My actions did not,” Alexander said. “His actions did. I made a traffic stop. He was here illegally. That’s not my fault.”

Still, Alexander’s uncertainty seemed to be growing. In March, another of Martinez’s cousins confronted him outside the taco shop, pressing him about Macias as she filmed with her phone.

“If I’m being 100% honest,” he said, “I don’t enjoy when families are separated, like with the Joanna situation. That kind of fucked me up.”

A month later, as Ingram’s handoffs to ICE surpassed 100 people, another video of Alexander circulated among a small group of community members. It showed an evening traffic stop, at least the fifth stop Alexander made that day. He had followed a silver Ford F-250 from the highway until it pulled into the gravel driveway of a mobile home. There, the man in the passenger seat, a landscaper named Victor Ramirez Hernandez, tried to hurry inside. Alexander grabbed him before he could get there.

The truck’s driver pulled out his phone and began filming. “Why are you arresting him?” he asked.

“I’m not,” Alexander answered, struggling to place handcuffs on Ramirez as he squirmed. “I’m detaining him.” Then he wrapped his hands around the laborer’s chest, lifted him off the ground and slammed him down on the driveway. “I played nice with you, and now I’m done,” he said. He straddled Ramirez and cuffed his hands behind his back.

“He’s bleeding because you hit him,” the driver said evenly. They argued back and forth.

“You’re an idiot,” Alexander said, his face reddening. “Shut up. The more you talk it’s just making you look stupider.” (Alexander declined to comment on this incident. Breshears, who reviewed body-camera footage, says Alexander’s use of force was justified to control a potentially dangerous situation.)

Acrimony pulsed through Ingram that spring. In the Latino community, family members and neighbors accused one another of snitching, which Alexander says did happen. Young women accused one another of having affairs with him, which he denies, insisting that people just misinterpreted his attempts to be kind. “I’m friendly with the people that I meet,” he says, “especially when they’re friendly to me.”

One subject of these rumors was Martinez. She and her older sister scoffed at them. “He’s taken our whole family at this point,” Jazmin says. “And she’s going to sleep with him? It’s really annoying.” But some in the community, even cousins, stopped speaking with her. “It’s like ‘Hunger Games,’” Martinez says.

As Latinos fled and avoided Ingram, the daily takeout orders at one Mexican restaurant on the edge of town dropped to 50 from 200. A corner store catering to immigrants lost around half its revenue and most of its Latino regulars. Landlords in a mobile-home park just outside town struggled to find tenants.

But it wasn’t just Ingram’s Latino community that was reeling. For Ingram’s non-Hispanic business owners, including many Republicans in a county that voted 77% for Trump in the 2024 election, the immigration crackdown was becoming an outrage. It was taking people they worked with and knew well. It was interfering with flood reconstruction. It was hurting the local economy.

The owner of a heavily damaged strip mall on the western edge of town, Steve Edelstein, lamented the loss of two workers who were helping him rebuild. “Technically local law enforcement is doing their job,” he told a reporter for The West Kerr Current, emphasizing that he supported Trump. “But it isn’t helping the community.” A manager overseeing the rebuilding of Ingram’s Little League field, which was washed away in the flood, made an angry call to the city after two of his workers were picked up, Breshears says. A contractor helping rebuild Hunt’s destroyed post office had his crew reduced to eight workers from 30 — causing what should have been a four-day job to stretch to two weeks.

Among the people who called Jordan, the mayor, to complain was his onetime high school classmate Neal Secor, who describes himself as “one of the most conservative guys you’ll ever meet.” His family business, a cluster of rental cabins called Waltonia on the River, was nearly destroyed by the flood. He was struggling to rebuild. He says that in the spring, in two separate stops, the Ingram police detained four of his workers and handed them over to ICE — the second set as they returned from a job repairing the docks at a nearby youth camp, a favor Secor was trying to do for a neighbor.

Secor says Jordan’s defense was simple: You voted for this. They’d all voted for this.

“Let me tell you something,” Secor says he told Jordan. “I didn’t vote for this. I voted to get the bad guys out.” The two employees detained coming back from the youth camp were like family, his friends and co-workers for over a decade. “There’s still a lot of bad guys here,” he continued. “These guys aren’t them.”

Secor recalls Jordan saying that Ingram’s officers were just following the law. But, Secor wondered, why was it that he could drive 85 every day on the 75-mile-an-hour interstate and police officers just let him pass? He told Jordan he’d put 175,000 miles on his truck and never been pulled over. “It’s a matter of what laws you want to pick and choose,” he recalls telling the mayor. “We all break laws every day if you really want to get down to it.”

On an unpaved side street in the Ingram hills in April, Alexander again faced a decision over whether to separate a young family. This time he had more of a choice; ICE had yet to arrive. A woman named Norma Luján took his hand and said she was praying for him.

Luján, 61, runs a successful housecleaning company based in Ingram. She had heard of Alexander long before she confronted him. She had seen him in the Charger, too: on the side of Junction Highway as she made her way to jobs at vacation homes or youth camps up in Hunt. On the side of the highway on the way back. In her rearview mirror for four excruciating minutes one afternoon as her “girls,” as she called her crew, held their breath and sank into their seats, protected by the tint of her car’s windows and the fact that a license-plate reader would show it was registered to her, a Texas-born U.S. citizen.

In January, at the carwash, Alexander had helped a sheriff’s deputy take one of Luján’s best employees into custody. The woman, Cinthya Carranza, was a single mother and asylum seeker from Guanajuato — and Joanna Martinez’s cousin. Carranza was on her way to get her children from school after she and Luján spent the day cleaning houses. Later that day, Luján saw a Facebook post showing a familiar face in a mug shot: Carranza’s. Luján broke into tears. To her, the arrest was a signal event. The Charger had mostly stopped men up to that point. This was the moment she understood that no one was safe.

Luján started losing workers in droves after Carranza was taken — some to traffic stops, most for fear of having to drive through Ingram. Her crew of 30 people dropped to fewer than 10. She worried for her business, but she worried more, she said, for her workers, the lives they had made in Ingram and the relationships she had built with them. They invited her to parties and baptisms, surprised her with presents on her birthday. She often led them in prayer. “I love them,” she says. She promised she would try to protect them.

When Luján’s employee Maria called that April afternoon to say the officer in the Charger was outside her house, detaining her husband as he returned from work, Luján jumped into action. Maria (who asked to be identified by her middle name because she is undocumented) was eight months pregnant and already terrified that she would have to give birth alone. Alexander had taken her brother a week earlier. Now Maria was hiding behind the curtains of her home, sneaking peeks outside, hoping Alexander would let her husband go.

“Cálmate” — calm down — Luján told herself as she approached the Charger. “Cálmate.” She took a deep breath. She says Alexander was sitting in the driver’s seat with the door propped open, staring at a tablet computer.

“Look, his wife has had a complicated pregnancy,” she told him. (This was true.) “She’s in the hospital.” (For that lie, Luján says, she later prayed for forgiveness.)

Then Luján reached out and grabbed Alexander’s hand. “I pray for your being, for your soul,” she recalls telling him as she cried. “I want you to think about what you’re doing. Feel it. Feel it, please.” She squeezed his hand. “I need you to look inside your heart,” she continued, “and let him go.”

Alexander didn’t react at first, in Luján’s telling. “God wanted me to meet you,” she said. He met her gaze.

“I’m that guy that everyone talks about on Facebook,” she recalls him finally saying. “If you think I don’t lose any sleep, you’re wrong.”

Luján pushed through the lump in her throat to speak. “I’m going to pray for you every night,” she promised him. She says Alexander squeezed her hand back, then pulled away. He walked off to make a call. She couldn’t make out much of what he said. It was quiet, save for the sound of Luján’s sobs and the pebbles from the unpaved road crunching beneath the officer’s feet.

Then he returned to the Charger. “Get him out of the car,” Luján recalls him saying. “Hurry up.”

“God bless you,” Luján said. “God will repay you for this.”

Alexander says he doesn’t remember the details of his interaction with Luján, but he confirms that he let Maria’s husband go, calling it a matter of “human decency.” He knew how hard it was to raise children. “A woman needs rest,” he says. “She needs help.” Maria’s husband “needs to be there helping out.”

At first, nothing seemed to change. Over the next two months, as the Hill Country prepared to welcome summer tourists and campers back after the devastation of the floods, Alexander worked around 100 task force hours, earning close to $5,000 from ICE.

But the next time he ran into Joanna Martinez, he seemed to be having misgivings. It was early June, and he was eight months into the ICE program. He had pulled over another of her family friends on Junction Highway, a person she had known since childhood. Martinez got a phone call and rushed to the scene, as she had so many times before. “I don’t feel good about this,” she recalls him saying when she arrived. “I signed up to be a cop, not an ICE agent.” (Alexander says he remembers that they talked and that he could have said something like that.)

Later that month, he pulled over a young man from Honduras who was on his way to a soccer game with his cousin, a 12-year-old with developmental disabilities. The boy’s father says he and his wife hid across the street watching it all happen, too terrified to step out and claim their son. The parents sent a friend from church, but she was not a legal guardian. Alexander says he had no choice but to hand the boy over to ICE, adding though that if he was one of the parents, he would see it differently. “If I’m in that position, there’s no way I’m letting my kid go,” he told us. “I’m probably fighting someone.”

Within a couple of weeks, Alexander’s task force hours dropped by about 40%, according to his pay stubs. It remains unclear if he tried to step back from immigration work or whether the number of undocumented drivers in Ingram had simply dwindled because of all he had already done.

According to Chief Breshears, Ingram had logged 194 “immigration-related incidents” as of this August. Whether every handover led to a deportation, Breshears couldn’t say. Alexander says he doesn’t know how many of the 194 are people he stopped. But he says he has now let at least 10 people go — many, though not all of them, mothers of young children.

Alexander sees this, and his relationship to the Martinez family, as evidence of his humanity, despite what people say about him. “For context, I’ve deported like six of their family members, and they’re still friendly to me,” he says. “So I’m not the monster they make me out to be.”

On July 16, the skies over Ingram opened again, dropping nearly 19 inches of rain in a single day, and the Guadalupe again overtopped its banks, again destroying businesses and homes. In the predawn darkness in the early hours of the emergency, Alexander began racing toward Ingram in his black Charger to help. He had barely left his house when surging floodwaters consumed the vehicle, destroying the Charger’s engine block. A driver in a pickup truck took him back home, and that’s where he stayed until the storm receded.

Alexander remains only one cog in a deportation machine for which the federal government has now earmarked $240 billion. Today more than 1,900 agencies in at least 30 states have signed up for ICE’s task force model, with more than 29,000 deputized officers. With their help, ICE recently hit a record of immigration arrests, totaling more than 50,000 in August.

On Sept. 11, Alexander met one of us at a taco spot on Junction Highway. Ingram had changed in the year since his return. Whole neighborhoods had emptied out, and residents noticed fewer drivers on the streets. In part because of the money from ICE, the Police Department had hired two additional officers. As a Mexican ballad played over the speakers, Alexander warned against making too much of the leniency he showed to families like Maria’s. “It’s like one free pass, I guess,” he said: If he encountered her husband again during a traffic stop, he would probably hand him over to ICE. He just thought letting the father-to-be go was the right call at the time. “I mean, don’t you?” he asked.

He said he hadn’t made all that much money working with ICE. But city records show that his earnings from the agency already totaled $18,793.25 in nine months, more than a quarter of his base salary. “I think it’s skewed a little bit,” he said, looking at the pay stubs we had gathered.

Alexander said it was hard for him to be out in public anymore. He can’t escape being recognized in Ingram and Kerrville. Sometimes he catches Latinos staring at him, then walking away, visibly uncomfortable. So he tries to do his shopping and errands in another town 30 minutes away.

He blamed the Facebook groups. They had created a climate of “unreasonable fear” in Ingram. “What do they think I’m going to do right now?” he asked, seated at his table in the taco shop. A steady stream of customers, many of them white, approached a counter staffed by two young Mexican sisters. He said their father used to hide out in Hunt to avoid being pulled over in town. Some of their co-workers had quit months before, too uncomfortable to serve Alexander when he came in for breakfast. But when the taco shop owner sees him, he said, she hugs him.

“I know the status of everybody in this room,” he said. He suggested everyone who worked there was undocumented. Whether they disappeared or were allowed to stay was up to him. And today, at least, he was just there for breakfast.

#4181. Astronomers may have discovered the first moon beyond our Solar System

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Astronomers may have found the first moon beyond our Solar System, but the mysterious object is unlike any moon we know. Observations from the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope (ESO's VLT) have uncovered evidence of an enormous moon-like body in the CD-35 2722 star system. Rather than orbiting a planet, this unusual object circles a brown dwarf, a celestial body more massive than a planet but not massive enough to become a star.

If confirmed, the discovery could mark the first detection of a natural satellite outside our Solar System, potentially changing how astronomers define moons and planets.

Kevin Hoy, an ESO student based in Chile and lead author of the study published in Nature, spent months examining the CD-35 2722 system. He describes its unusual arrangement as "super weird" compared with our own Solar System.

At the center of this relatively young system is CD-35 2722, a star with approximately half the Sun's mass. Orbiting the star is a brown dwarf, an object that occupies the murky boundary between planets and stars. Brown dwarfs are generally too small to sustain the hydrogen fusion that powers ordinary stars, yet they are more massive than planets.

The newly identified object orbits this brown dwarf, creating a remarkable arrangement in which a massive satellite circles an object that itself revolves around a star.

"This system is somewhat hard to define using Solar-System-based words like 'planet' and 'moon'," states Hoy, who is also affiliated with the Universidad Diego Portales and the Millennium Nucleus of Young Exoplanets and their Moons (YEMS) in Chile.

The researchers refer to the newly detected object as an exosatellite. It has at least as much mass as Jupiter, while the brown dwarf it orbits is more than 30 times as massive as Jupiter. This makes the possible moon far more massive than any natural satellite in our Solar System.

"The exosatellite is clearly massive enough to be a planet, but it does not orbit a star, though it orbits an object that orbits a star," says Hoy. "Being the third wheel in this system makes us want to call it a moon, even if it is nothing like the small, rocky moons we have in our system."

A Giant Exomoon That Challenges Cosmic Definitions

The unusual discovery highlights a fundamental problem with applying familiar astronomical labels to objects found in distant star systems.

An exosatellite is a natural satellite located beyond our Solar System, while the term exomoon generally refers to a natural satellite orbiting a planet or another celestial body outside our Solar System. However, astronomers have not established a universally accepted definition of an exomoon.

That distinction becomes especially important in the CD-35 2722 system, where the possible moon is itself massive enough to be considered a planet under some classification schemes.

Alice Zurlo, director of YEMS and a collaborator on the study, explains: "The satellite we report is a giant gaseous body orbiting a highly massive companion, itself several times the mass of Jupiter."

The arrangement is very different from the familiar structure of our Solar System, where planets orbit the Sun and moons orbit planets.

"We have a clear delineation between the planets and the Sun in the Solar System, so defining things like moons is simple. In the CD-35 2722 system, where we are blurring the lines between stars, planets, and moons, the whole thing becomes more complicated to describe," adds Zurlo, who is also an astrophysicist at Universidad Diego Portales.

Scientists Search for the First Confirmed Exomoon

Astronomers have spent years looking for moons around objects beyond our Solar System, but no candidate has yet been detected with enough certainty to establish a confirmed discovery.

That makes the latest findings particularly significant. Although scientists have identified more than 6,000 exoplanets, only a handful of possible exomoons have emerged from observations, and the evidence supporting them remains limited.

Earlier in 2026, a research team led by Quentin Kral reported possible signs of a satellite in the HD 206893 star system. Their observations, obtained with ESO's Very Large Telescope Interferometer, suggested that a moon-like companion might exist, but they did not establish a definitive detection.

The CD-35 2722 system now offers another promising opportunity, supported by measurements of how the suspected moon affects the motion of its larger companion.

How Astronomers Detected the Possible Alien Moon

To investigate CD-35 2722, Hoy, Zurlo, and their colleagues used the CRIRES+ instrument installed on ESO's Very Large Telescope.

The team applied a technique known as the radial velocity method, which was also used to discover the first exoplanet orbiting a Sun-like star.

This approach works by measuring extremely small changes in an object's motion. When two celestial bodies orbit one another, their mutual gravitational attraction causes both to move around a shared center of mass. Even a much smaller companion can produce a detectable wobble in the larger object's motion.

By examining subtle changes in the brown dwarf's motion, the researchers identified what they consider strong evidence that another massive body is orbiting it.

"As exotic as it is, this system is truly unique and represents a breakthrough: the first plausible detection of an exosatellite," says Zurlo.

Confirming moons beyond our Solar System would do more than add another category of objects to astronomical catalogs. These discoveries could help researchers understand how planetary systems form, how their components interact, and why some systems develop structures dramatically different from our own.

Future observations could also reveal whether massive satellites like the one proposed in CD-35 2722 are rare exceptions or part of a much broader population of unusual celestial objects.

ESO's upcoming Extremely Large Telescope (ELT) is expected to play a major role in that search. Equipped with an enormous 39-meter mirror and advanced scientific instruments, the telescope will give astronomers the ability to detect smaller exomoons that are currently beyond the reach of existing observatories.

As scientists begin identifying more satellites in distant planetary systems, they may discover that familiar distinctions between stars, planets, and moons are not as universal as once assumed.

A satellite is any object that travels around another object. Satellites can be natural (like our own moon) or artificial (like a spacecraft).

An exosatellite is a satellite located outside our Solar System. The term exomoon generally describes a natural satellite orbiting a planet or another object beyond our Solar System, although astronomers have not agreed on an official definition.

The possible companion in CD-35 2722 illustrates why that definition matters. With a mass comparable to or greater than Jupiter's, it resembles a planet in size and composition, yet its orbital relationship with the brown dwarf makes it a candidate for an entirely different classification.

Schizophrenia affects how people perceive their surroundings, organize their thoughts, and communicate with others. Although these symptoms are often studied separately, new research suggests they may be connected to a common disruption in brain activity.

In a study published in Science Advances, scientists identified changes in a brain rhythm known as beta-band activity that were associated with several symptoms of schizophrenia. Patients with the most pronounced changes also had greater difficulty processing sensory information, more disorganized speech, and more severe clinical symptoms.

Researchers examined brain activity in 25 people with schizophrenia and 25 healthy participants using high-resolution magnetoencephalography, or MEG. The recordings were collected at the McConnell Brain Imaging Centre at the Montreal Neurological Institute-Hospital (The Neuro). Participants completed a task involving the timing of sights and sounds, and researchers separately analyzed their speech for signs of disorganization.

Compared with the control group, participants with schizophrenia showed weaker and slower beta-band activity. These brain rhythms are involved in processes that help the brain interpret sensory information and coordinate responses to the environment.

“What struck us was that the same weakened brain signal kept showing up wherever we looked, whether patients were processing a sound, a sight or completing a movement,” said Hsi (Tiana) Wei, lead author of the study and a postdoctoral scholar in McGill University’s Department of Psychiatry and the Douglas Research Centre. “Seeing the same pattern across these different domains suggested there may be a common underlying process linking the symptoms.”

The results support the possibility that some schizophrenia symptoms arise from disturbances in how the brain constructs and updates its understanding of the world. Rather than representing entirely independent problems, difficulties with perception, behavior, and speech may share an underlying neurological process.

“This gives us early but compelling evidence that measuring the brain’s operations, especially how it builds and revises its own model of the world, may help us appreciate that there is a common thread behind the confusing array of symptoms we see in disorders like schizophrenia,” said Dr. Lena Palaniyappan, senior author of the study, Professor of Psychiatry at McGill University, and director of the Centre of Excellence in Youth Mental Health at the Douglas Hospital.

Palaniyappan believes that further research could eventually help clinicians identify young people who need additional mental health support before psychosis develops. “The very first sign of schizophrenia begins in our adolescence, but this often gets missed for a long time. If this finding is confirmed in larger samples, we may have a potentially useful method to identify who, among youth seeking help for mental health, needs extra support to prevent psychosis,” he said.

The study involved just 50 participants, and its findings establish associations rather than demonstrating that altered beta-band activity causes schizophrenia symptoms. The researchers also have not established whether measuring this signal can reliably predict psychosis.

“This is a first, modest-size study, and the associations with speech and clinical severity will need to be tested in larger independent samples before we can be confident in them,” Wei said.

If future studies confirm the findings, beta-band activity could potentially serve as a measurable indicator of brain dysfunction associated with schizophrenia. Wei also suggested that the signal may be worth investigating as a future treatment target, although its clinical usefulness remains unproven.

#4143. Scientists Discover That Ovaries May Be Doing Something Unexpected Decades After Menopause

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Ovaries often dismissed as inactive after menopause may still shape how women age, with human tissue revealing inflammatory changes and molecular similarities to ovarian cancer.

Ovaries continue to change for decades after menopause, long after their role in reproduction has ended. In a study of ovarian tissue from 28 women ages 50 to 75, scientists found age-related shifts in proteins associated with inflammation, immune activity, and the structure of the tissue surrounding cells.

The research, conducted by scientists at Northwestern University and the Buck Institute for Research on Aging, was published in Aging Cell.

“In the ovary, we reach a point of no return with respect to fertility in which it’s no longer producing eggs, and I would venture to say most people assume those ovaries are just sitting there doing nothing, similar to the appendix,” said co-corresponding author Francesca Duncan, associate professor of reproductive science in medicine at Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine. “But that’s what makes this study very different.”

To investigate what changes after menopause, the researchers looked beyond ovarian hormones to examine the tissue’s protein composition. Co-corresponding author Birgit Schilling, professor and director of the Mass Spectrometry Center at the Buck Institute, led the team responsible for that analysis. Spearheaded by Buck Institute staff scientist and Schilling lab member Mark Watson, the work used advanced proteomic technologies that can identify and measure thousands of proteins at once. The researchers analyzed 5,812 groups of proteins in normal ovarian tissue.

Human ovarian tissue is difficult to obtain, making the collection of 28 ovaries unusually valuable, Duncan said. Concentrating on a 25-year age range after menopause allowed the team to isolate differences within the postmenopausal years, rather than comparing young and old ovaries across an entire lifespan. The technology also made it possible to examine thousands of proteins in every sample.

“When we analyzed the data in multiple different statistical ways, what became clear across all these methodologies is that the ovary was not stagnant,” Schilling said. “In fact, over the decades, there were quite dramatic differences in terms of some proteins’ levels would go up with age and some proteins’ levels would go down, which tells us this organ is quite dynamic.”

Ovaries from younger postmenopausal women contained more proteins involved in maintaining cells and tissue structure. Older ovaries had more proteins associated with inflammation, immune activity, and the breakdown and rebuilding of the tissue around cells. The differences were greatest between women in their 50s and those ages 70 and older.

Some of the molecular patterns also resembled those seen in cellular senescence. In this state, aging or damaged cells stop dividing but remain biologically active and can send signals that affect nearby tissue.

“Our data points to progressive aging features in which the ovary appears to lose its original identity and ends up doing something different,” Duncan said. “We do not yet know if there’s ever a point in a woman’s life when this organ is no longer beneficial and it’s actually harmful. These results really point to the need to ask this question.”

Duncan’s group had previously found similar evidence of continued remodeling in postmenopausal ovaries of mice. She began both that research and the human study with expectations that the findings ultimately challenged.

“I was predicting that if we took a 50-year-old ovary, a 60-year-old ovary, a 70-year-old, an 80-year-old ovary, they would all look exactly the same, have no eggs, and basically be non-functional,” Duncan said. “The fact that across species we’re seeing this gives us confidence that this isn’t just some random event, but something truly meaningful is happening in the biology.”

Women now routinely live for decades after menopause, Duncan noted, making the ovary’s role during this long period increasingly relevant to understanding women’s health beyond fertility.

“The ovary is not just a reproductive organ,” Schilling said. “It really influences how somebody ages because it speaks to all these other organs, such as the brain, and also to the bone, and women after menopause experience often a lot of bone problems. So, we think there is a connection between all these organs.”

The changes in tissue structure also connect the new findings to an earlier question about ovarian cancer. In 2016, Duncan’s lab was the first to find that aging ovaries become increasingly inflamed, fibrotic, and stiff, resembling scarring in other tissues. Cancer cells can thrive in stiff, collagen-rich environments, prompting the researchers to investigate whether these changes could make older ovaries more hospitable to cancer cells. The new protein analysis provides a molecular view of that remodeling process after menopause.

All the tissue in the current study appeared normal and cancer-free. Nevertheless, some proteins previously associated with ovarian cancer were more abundant in aging ovaries, with the strongest signal among women in their 60s. This does not mean the ovaries were cancerous or precancerous. It suggests that healthy ovarian aging and ovarian cancer share some biological processes.

“It’s not that all postmenopausal women get ovarian cancer, but we know that that fibrotic inflammatory environment is certainly an environment that cancer cells prefer to home in on,” Duncan said. “This is all consistent with the idea that the aging ovary is a potential third hit for ovarian cancer, with the first hit being genetic and the second hit being environmental. So can we target the aging ovary therapeutically to lower the risk of ovarian cancer, or can we develop novel biomarkers of ovarian cancer?”

Whether aging in the ovary can drive changes elsewhere in the body remains an open question.

“Is the ovary the canary in the coal mine in women?” said Duncan, who is also an associate professor in residence at the Buck Institute. “Could aging in the ovary set off a cascade of changes that continues throughout the body as women get older?”

The findings do not answer whether healthy ovaries should ever be removed after menopause, the researchers emphasized. Much more research is needed to determine exactly what the organ does during these years and whether the changes observed contribute to chronic disease or are simply features of normal aging.

Shanes, Mary Ellen G. Pavone, Francesca E. Duncan and Birgit Schilling, 7 September 2026, Aging Cell. DOI: 10.1111/acel.70714

#4156. Snakebites Are Far Deadlier Than We Thought, New Study Reveals

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Even fatal snakebites can go uncounted, and a model estimating up to 513,000 deaths annually could help direct lifesaving antivenom to communities that health records miss.

Not every snakebite injects venom. When venom enters the body, however, it can cause snake envenoming. This is a potentially fatal condition, and it mostly affects poor rural communities across tropical and subtropical regions who don’t have easy access to medical care.

As researchers with decades of combined experience in snakebite and tropical disease epidemiology, we came to realize that snake envenoming faces a data crisis. Over half of the countries where venomous snakes pose a threat don’t keep count of how many people are bitten or envenomed.

In rural sub-Saharan Africa and south Asia, many victims live hours away from the nearest clinic, lack transport, or seek care from traditional healers. As a result, their cases are never captured in national health statistics.

To bridge this massive gap, we conducted a study to map the global burden of snake envenoming and related deaths.

We looked at data from 79 countries by reviewing studies published up to 31 March 2025. This allowed us to update the findings from our milestone 2008 global burden study. To fill the remaining data gaps, we built a geostatistical model that combines real-world data with local environmental and socioeconomic factors. This allowed us to estimate envenoming and fatality rates.

We conclude from our findings that snake envenoming is a public health crisis on a far greater scale than official statistics have shown. Our results show that sub-Saharan Africa bears the heaviest toll.

The World Health Organization has set an ambitious target to halve snakebite mortality and disability by 2030. Mapping the problem at the country level is the first crucial step.

To overcome the lack of reliable data, we turned to a model-based geostatistics framework.

To understand how this modelling works, think of it as predicting invisible patterns by combining known data points with environmental signals. Instead of assuming data for every region, this modelling uses statistical probability to calculate a range of likely outcomes based on environmental signals.

We fed our model with data including climate, snake species richness, and socioeconomic indicators. The model then calculated the probability of snakebites occurring in specific locations, even where official health records were completely blank.

By comparing different data sources (including hospital-reported data and regional statistics) against ground level community-survey data, our framework allowed us to account for unreported snake envenomings. It produced both a baseline floor and a potential upper ceiling for snake envenomings and fatalities.

Based on this, we estimate that at least 2.1 million people experience snake envenoming each year globally. The true number could be high as 7 million. Using our model, which adjusts for substantial under-reporting, annual deaths may range from 274,000 to 513,000.

The geographic distribution of this burden is uneven. Our research shows that sub-Saharan Africa alone accounts for 45% of all global envenomings and fatalities. That burden has likely been underestimated for decades. People in sub-Saharan Africa experience snake envenoming and death at nearly three times the rate seen in south Asia.

Countries like Nigeria, the Democratic Republic of Congo and Ethiopia face a high burden, driven by dense rural populations and high exposure in agricultural fields.

The reasons sub-Saharan Africa suffers such disproportionate harm are structural rather than accidental.

Firstly, the continent hosts a rich diversity of medically important venomous species. These include saw-scaled vipers (Echis spp.), puff adders (Bitis arietans), African cobras (Naja spp.), and mambas (Dendroaspis spp.). They are responsible for substantial morbidity and mortality across the continent.

Secondly, millions of rural agricultural workers labor in open fields without protective boots and walk on unlit footpaths at night. When a bite occurs, fragile health systems, long travel distances to regional facilities, and the high-cost or total absence of effective antivenom mean a treatable medical emergency can quickly become fatal. Similar conditions exist in parts of Asia.

We estimate that over 800,000 survivors are left with long-term physical disabilities each year, including amputations and other permanent impairments. These disabilities can reduce household income, increase healthcare costs, and push already vulnerable farming families deeper into poverty.

Meeting the World Health Organization’s goal requires moving away from guesswork and using spatial risk data to guide real-world policy.

By identifying high-risk hotspots, governments and global health partners can strategically stockpile antivenom where it is needed most, train primary healthcare workers in high-burden rural zones, and improve rapid transport infrastructure.

Snake envenoming has remained hidden for too long. Better data cannot solve the problem, but can help ensure that antivenom, healthcare services, and prevention efforts reach the communities that need them most.

#4159. Scientists Studied 865 Humpback Whales and Found They Grow Faster Than Expected

📅 24 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Despite decades of environmental change and population recovery, eastern Australian humpback whales appear to have maintained their historical adult size.

Eastern Australian humpback whales grow at an extraordinary pace, reaching approximately 10.9 meters (35.8 feet) in length by age five. By six or seven years old, they have already attained about 90 percent of their eventual adult body length, according to new research led by Griffith University.

The findings, published in Marine Mammal Science, suggest these whales grow faster than humpback populations studied elsewhere. “This is faster growth than other humpback whale populations that have been measured,” said lead researcher Stephanie Stack, a Griffith University PhD candidate.

To investigate how humpbacks develop throughout their lives, researchers measured 865 individually identified whales between 2021 and 2024. Using drones to photograph the animals from above, they calculated body lengths without physically disturbing the whales. Long-term photo-identification records helped the team track growth across newborn calves, juveniles, and adults more than 40 years old.

The measurements showed that humpbacks experience their fastest growth during early life, with the rate gradually declining as they approach adult size. Including calves and young whales allowed scientists to examine these crucial developmental stages directly rather than relying on estimates derived from older animals.

“These are also the first contemporary growth curves developed for eastern Australian humpback whales using measurements from living animals, updating knowledge that has relied heavily on measurements collected during the commercial whaling era in the mid-20th century,” Stack said.

Eastern Australia’s humpback population has recovered dramatically since commercial whaling ended, becoming one of the world’s most successful examples of whale conservation. Despite this population rebound and decades of environmental change, the new measurements closely match historical estimates of adult humpback size in Australian waters.

“These new growth curves establish a baseline for investigating how whale biology may respond to changing environmental conditions.”

“Understanding how quickly whales grow provides another piece of the puzzle in understanding the biology of this recovering population,” Stack said. “Growth influences many aspects of a whale’s life, including its health, energy requirements and potentially the timing of reproduction.”

The new growth curves provide researchers with a reference point for tracking possible changes in humpback development as ocean conditions shift. Future measurements could help determine whether environmental changes are affecting growth patterns in this recovering population.

“As environmental conditions continue to change, having robust biological benchmarks like these will be essential for understanding how whale populations are responding,” Stack said.

#4167. Physicists Use Light To Unlock a Crystal’s Hidden Electrical Powers

📅 24 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

A crystal’s laser-induced electrical order lasts just a trillionth of a second, giving researchers a way to explore ultrafast approaches to processing and storing information.

Inside a crystal, tiny electrical regions can point in different directions or line up in an organized pattern. Cooling helps bring them into alignment, but physicists at Auburn University have now shown that brief flashes of laser light can also produce order at warmer temperatures, where those regions would otherwise remain disorganized.

The team worked with single crystals of a modified form of strontium titanate. The material allows electrons to move through it while also containing tiny regions with distinct positive and negative sides, an unusual combination of electrical properties. These regions are known as polar nanoregions.

For decades, physicists have debated whether atoms shift together to create the material’s polarized state or whether its polar regions already exist and become organized as it cools. Telling those explanations apart has been difficult because the regions are so small and their signals so faint.

Wencan Jin, the Thomas and Jean Walter Endowed Associate Professor in Auburn’s Department of Physics, led the research with graduate student Mohamed Kandil. The project began at the 2023 American Physical Society March Meeting, where Jin connected with researchers in Japan who could grow the crystals. Collaborators at Japan’s National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology grew the samples used in the experiments.

The Auburn researchers built a highly sensitive laser-based measurement system, then spent nearly a year improving the equipment before detecting the regions’ faint optical signal. By measuring how the crystal changed incoming light, they could track the regions’ alignment through repeated rounds of warming and cooling. The measurements showed disordered directions at higher temperatures giving way to preferred orientations as the crystal cooled. This provided strong evidence that the polar regions were already present before they lined up.

The team still had to capture the fleeting moment when laser illumination brought the regions into alignment. “The breakthrough came in 2025, when Mohamed finally observed the disordered polar regions becoming aligned under laser illumination,” Jin said. “Seeing that signal emerge was a thrilling moment for the team.”

The light-induced alignment lasted only a picosecond, or one trillionth of a second (10⁻¹² second), but an optical measurement could still detect the resulting pattern. Being able to create and read that brief arrangement with light gives researchers a way to investigate extremely fast approaches to processing and storing information. It also provides a tool for studying electrical behavior that is otherwise difficult to observe.

“This experiment has truly been Mohamed’s odyssey,” Jin said. “It took him three years of dedication to complete the work and achieve his first publication.”
