#4119. The World’s 10 Most ‘Cursed’ Places: A Guide to Dangerous Destinations

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Home → Features → Natural Sciences → Geography

Some of these places look like dream destinations until you learn the disasters hiding underneath

Some places frighten you when you arrive. Others spook you just reading about them.

In Atlas of Cursed Places, Olivier Le Carrer delivers for both types of people — those with a morbid fascination for macabre locations and those who want to stay as far as possible from them.

The destinations he mentions are not all haunted in the candlelit sense. The “curses” here come in many forms: natural hazards, industrial nightmares, religious terror, mass death, environmental ruin, and places where legend has grown over real suffering like moss on a wall.

“The honest traveller is surprised to observe that the red sand is ringed by snow-capped mountains and impenetrable forests populated by tetchy bears,” writes Le Carrer in a chapter on the so-called Nevada Triangle. Le Carrer adds that any offer of an excursion by plane should be politely refused.

The reason is simple: thanks to the turbulence generated by the rugged landscape, more than 2,000 aircraft have gone missing there over the past five decades — more than that of the fabled Bermuda Triangle.

The author’s white-knuckle ride across America and around the globe features 40 stops ranging from Golgotha, the site outside Jerusalem of Christ’s crucifixion, to the Japanese suicide venue, Aokigahara Forest, and Australia’s Cape York, where crocodiles noted for explosive attacks approach speeds of 30 km/h.

The result is a travel guide in reverse: a gazetteer of places where the curious should keep one hand on the emergency exit. Here are just ten ‘cursed’ places I picked from the book.

Were it not for the people going about their business, Jharia might look like a place recently shelled. Smoke rises from cracks in the ground while the earth sags. The landscape has the exhausted look of a battlefield.

Except here, as Olivier Le Carrer notes, the fire “does not come from the sky.” It comes from below.

For more than a century, old coal seams beneath this district of Jharkhand in India have been burning. Such fires are not rare in abandoned mines, but Jharia is home to roughly half a million people. They live above or beside what Le Carrer calls a “steadily expanding underground inferno,” made up of dozens of separate blazes.

At first, residents even found uses for it. Steam from fissures could cook a pot. But the seeping coal fumes weren’t so useful when some houses split and roads started to buckle, all in an atmosphere of toxic fumes.

At the edge of Russia, near the Norwegian border, the Kola Peninsula first offers the usual northern temptations. There’s the never-ending tundra, the lovely fjords, and the frigid terror of polar night.

Zapadnaya Litsa has no shortage of abandoned barracks, broken factories, smashed windows, and military debris. Le Carrer gives it a harsher title: “the antechamber of hell.” The true horror lies lower down, in the fjords, where the retired monsters of the Northern Fleet wait.

Dozens of decommissioned, Cold War-era vessels — including massive Typhoon and fast Alfa class submarines — were abandoned here, left to rust and decay while awaiting complex and hazardous dismantling processes. Le Carrer mentions the old nuclear submarines sit “like harmless museum exhibits,” although they are anything but. The base once served a superpower. Now it keeps watch over radioactive fuel in quantities Le Carrer describes as “thirty times greater than that of the Chernobyl reactor.”

The Maldives sells the dream of a flawless lagoon. Thilafushi receives the bill.

Le Carrer labels it “The Toxic Lagoon,” a grim counterpoint to the country’s postcard image of overwater villas and transparent seas. In 1992, authorities began turning this narrow island near Malé into a landfill, a dump for all the garbage produced by the archipelago.

As the number of tourists to the Maldives skyrocketed, trash arrived by the ton. Migrant workers at the site burned what they could. Heavy metals, batteries, and chemicals seeped toward the water. When the island ran out of space, waste sometimes spread into the shallows, creating drifting rafts of garbage in a lagoon marketed to the world as paradise.

Le Carrer calls the result the “highly visible abscess of the infection.” Apparently, even Eden produces rubbish, and someone has to stand downwind of it

From Venice, Poveglia is almost insultingly close: a small green island in the lagoon, overlooked by a bell tower and abandoned buildings.

It might look charming but Le Carrer calls it the “Island of Death,” and the legends have done much of the work. In Veneto, Le Carrer writes, people say that “when a bad man dies, he wakes up on Poveglia.”

Stories claim plague victims and lepers were exiled there and thousands of bodies lie beneath the soil. A psychiatric hospital later added a second layer of horror. In the most lurid versions, a mad doctor tortured patients and threw himself from the campanile.

The archives confirm a quarantine station, later care homes, and then closure in 1968, but not every bloody detail the internet now repeats.

More recently, Poveglia (often called the world’s most haunted island) turned into a bizarre real estate battle. In 2014, the Italian state offered a 99-year lease on the island at an auction. A Venetian citizens’ group, Poveglia per Tutti, crowdfunded to buy it for public use, but a businessman named Luigi Brugnaro won the auction with a €513,000 bid. The deal eventually stalled, keeping Poveglia locked in a bureaucratic and spectral limbo.

The Sunda Strait is beautiful in the way a sleeping dragon is beautiful.

Between Java and Sumatra, the sea route carries ships through shallows, oil installations, new school pirates, and one of the most famous volcanic hot spots on Earth: Krakatoa. In 1883, after months of eruptions and tremors, the volcano exploded with such violence that its sound reportedly traveled thousands of miles. To this day, it’s considered the loudest thing in history. Tsunami waves smashed into nearby coasts. The official death toll reached about 40,000.

Krakatoa did not vanish after the disaster. Its offspring, Anak Krakatoa, rose from the sea in the 20th century and has continued to remind the strait that geology works on its own schedule.

Indonesia sits along one of the world’s most active tectonic regions, where subduction feeds volcanoes and tsunamis. To build, sail, and live there is to negotiate with forces that predate every port, bridge, and shipping lane.

Le Carrer calls Cape Horn the Sailor’s Nightmare.” In less than two centuries, some 800 ships wrecked near this dark rock at the end of South America, taking perhaps 20,000 lives with them. The region is blanketed by gray sea, cold rain, poor visibility, brutal wind, and “always the feeling that one’s troubles are only just beginning.”

Nobody came here for romance. Sailors came because commerce told them to. Before the Panama Canal, rounding the Horn was part of the long Atlantic-to-Pacific ordeal. Ships clawed their way through the Roaring Forties and Furious Fifties (strong, continuous westerly winds circling the Southern Hemisphere) that whip around the planet and the sea. With almost no land to stop them, the winds grew monstrous.

In 1914, the canal cut thousands of miles from the voyage and spared many ships the old punishment. Cape Horn, Le Carrer writes, “got what it deserved.”

Still, the place keeps its talent. It merely torments fewer people now.

The Houtman Abrolhos islands sit off Western Australia, low and reef-ringed, a trap disguised as an archipelago.

In 1629, the Dutch East India Company ship Batavia struck the reefs with more than 300 people on board. The wreck should have been the disaster. It became only the opening scene.

While a rescue party left for Java, assistant merchant Jeronimus Cornelisz took control of the survivors. What followed can only be described as organized murder. Cornelisz, a failed apothecary turned company official, began arranging resources by his own twisted arithmetic. Those he considered useless were sent to islands without fresh water, drowned quietly, or killed more openly once his intentions became impossible to miss. His men murdered “by whatever violent means” they had available. Women were spared only to be abused.

Rescue arrived from Java after more than two months. Cornelisz and six accomplices were hanged. Nearly 200 others were already dead.

The reef wrecked the ship. The crew did the rest.

Can a wildlife sanctuary suffer from too much nature? Kasanka National Park is one of Africa’s smallest reserves. Driven to the brink by poachers, conservationists successfully revived the park, reintroducing elephants and hippos. But nobody expected the sky to turn black. Every October, five million straw-colored fruit bats descend on a tiny forest within the park. Le Carrer describes the phenomenon as the “African remake of one of Alfred Hitchcock’s most unsettling films”.

These aggressive mammals strip the trees, devouring 330,000 tons of fruit annually. The massive swarm also attracts predators. Pythons, crocodiles, and birds of prey crowd the area, feasting on fallen bats. Ecologists study this colossal migration to understand shifting global wildlife patterns, yet the bats’ final destination remains unknown. The site remains a terrifying spectacle for anyone brave enough to visit.

Aokigahara lies at the foot of Mount Fuji, where beauty and dread share the same trees.

The forest is often called a suicide forest. It covers barely fourteen square miles, yet it claims around one hundred lives each year.

Le Carrer describes a dense, dim “sea of trees,” where the ground is uneven, mossy, and broken by crevasses. Paths are scarce and people can easily get lost. More painfully, many have gone there intending not to return. Authorities and volunteers have tried to intervene, but people looking to end their lives still venture here, as if lured by a siren call.

Pop culture has been blamed, notably Seichō Matsumoto’s 1960 novel Black Sea of Trees, where two lovers end their lives at Aokigahara forest. But locals insist the forest’s dark appeal predates modern literature. They believe the “yuurei — the ghosts of the departed who wander the earth in search of paradise — are well acquainted with Aokigahara”. Historically, families practiced ubasute here, abandoning elderly relatives to die.

In modern Carthage, amid villas, cypresses, and bougainvillea, one of antiquity’s darkest arguments lies underfoot.

The Tophet of Carthage, also known as the Salammbo, has yielded twenty thousand funerary urns, many containing the remains of very young children. Ancient writers accused the Carthaginians of sacrificing children to the gods Baal and Tanit. Some modern scholars have argued instead that the site may have served as a cemetery for infants who died naturally.

Ancient Carthaginian families allegedly offered their firstborn males to secure the gods’ favor. Despite these gruesome rituals, Carthage ultimately fell. The Romans destroyed the city in 146 BCE, and according to myth sowed the ruins with salt such that nothing would grow there. Le Carrer asks rhetorically if the city fell because “the earth of the tophet was never able to digest all that blood.”

#4102. Scientists reveal the hidden danger of missing blood pressure medication

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Missing doses of blood pressure medication could significantly reduce its ability to protect against heart attacks, strokes, and other serious cardiovascular problems, according to findings presented at ESC Congress 2026.

An estimated 1.4 billion adults worldwide between the ages of 30 and 79 have high blood pressure (hypertension), a major contributor to illness and death. Although medications that lower blood pressure are proven to reduce the risk of cardiovascular complications, approximately half of patients do not take their prescriptions as directed.

"We have evidence that poor adherence to antihypertensive medication is associated with worse outcomes," explained presenter, Miss Qianqian Yang from the University of Oxford, UK. "However, previous data has come from observational studies and could be confounded by other factors. We undertook an analysis of randomized trial data to assess the true impact of adherence on the benefits of antihypertensive treatment."

Major Study Examines the Impact of Missed Blood Pressure Medication

To better understand how consistently taking medication affects its benefits, researchers analyzed individual patient data from 91,339 participants across nine clinical trials. Each trial compared a blood pressure treatment (intervention group) with an alternative approach (comparator group: either placebo or a less-intensive regimen).

The researchers divided participants into two categories based on how closely they followed their prescribed treatment. Those who took at least 80% of their assigned medication were considered to have higher adherence, while those who took less than 80% were placed in the lower adherence group.

Even with the regular monitoring and structured conditions of clinical trials, patients became less consistent about taking their medication as time passed.

"A decline in adherence over time was apparent, even in the structured setting of trials," noted Miss Yang. Overall, 88% of participants met the higher adherence threshold during the first year, but that figure had dropped to just 79% by the fifth year.

Skipping Blood Pressure Pills Reduces Treatment Benefits

The difference in medication adherence was reflected in how much patients' blood pressure improved.

Among participants with higher adherence, systolic blood pressure, the top number in a blood pressure reading, was 5.2 mmHg lower in the intervention group than in the comparator group. For those with lower adherence, the difference was considerably smaller at just 3.0 mmHg.

The researchers believe that helping people take their medication consistently should become a greater priority in the treatment of high blood pressure. #3699. Wallhack M-001 Review: When Fun Overrides Specs

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Batteries. They're not typically thought of as a significant source of innovation. Most companies have spent the last few years removing mice in favor of more lightweight designs.

That's what's made the return of the replaceable and rechargeable battery surprising—something I once thought to be a lost technology from a bygone era. A few companies have recently realized that the ability to pop out and replace your battery in seconds is a great way to extend mouse life without needing to increase battery size or efficiency.

From the handful of mice I’ve tested with hot-swappable batteries so far, Wallhack does it best. The mouse is designed around the function’s ease-of-use, and everything else comes together to make a mouse that feels not only competitive for gaming, but also well thought-out and satisfying to use in everyday life.

The M-001’s shape is a standard right-handed, symmetrical design. This means the mouse’s left and right sides are shaped identically, but only the left side has thumb buttons. There are two main buttons, two side buttons, a standard ratcheting scroll wheel, and a power switch on the underside.

With a length of 120 mm, the M-001 is slightly shorter than contemporaries like the Razer Viper V4 Pro (125 mm) and Logitech Pro Superstrike X2 (127 mm). The overall width and height are comparable, at 63.5-mm tall and a 39-mm wide. The largest difference is the profile of the mouse: The front end sits significantly lower than other mice, and the rear has a more significant hump, which creates a more angled shape overall. When using a palm grip, this puts your hand at a slightly more aggressive forwards angle, with your palm sitting higher and your fingers pointing more downwards. It’s a slight difference, but I found this angle to be better for quick movements compared to a more “flat” design. However, like most shape-related choices, this is ultimately down to individual preference. (To compare mouse sizes, I typically use a website like eloshapes)

The two main buttons are lightly sculpted, with subtle concavity for fingers to comfortably rest in. Similarly, there is a subtle taper at the bottom center of the mouse which makes lifting and moving easier. The side buttons use a simple, rounded shape placed just above the taper, making it easy to rock your thumb upwards to press them without getting in the way. They’re just elevated enough that the buttons don’t recess into the shell when pressed. The only shape-related issue I had was the scroll wheel, which is a good bit more elevated than other mice. This isn’t a huge issue, and I adapted to it after a few hours of use, but it’s something I could see being off-putting for users accustomed to a lower-profile scroll wheel.

The most important part of this mouse’s construction is the magnesium shell, which provides significantly more rigidity than the plastic shells used on most mice. Because of this, the mouse feels incredibly sturdy and solid. The metal shell also gives the mouse a cool, slick feeling underneath your fingertips, which I prefer to the matte plastic feeling that most mice have.

Despite using metal, it only weighs 53.6 grams, making the M-001 quite light by most standards, It still falls slightly behind the Viper V4 Pro’s 50.3 grams and lags way behind Corsair’s superlight, 36-gram Sabre V2 Pro Ultralight. But the M-001 is incredibly solid, with none of the flexibility or flimsiness that most plastic mice have. When compared to other magnesium mice like the AngryMiao Infinity .97 or Corsair’s magnesium-shelled Sabre, the M-001 has an entirely solid upper shell without any cutouts or holes, which feels much better for regular use with a multitude of grip styles (plus, it’s much less likely to have dust, dead skin, or anything else build up inside of it). While the full magnesium shell increases weight, it’s a worthwhile trade-off for a smooth and rigid shape.

It’s also worth noting that some of Wallhack’s promotional material depicts the mouse as being a solid black color, but this isn’t quite the case. The unit I received is much closer to a gunmetal color, with a significant amount of metallic flake across the finish. The color looks fantastic in person, but it's difficult to properly capture in images.

Flipping over this mouse reveals one of the more atypical design decisions being made: the lack of a bottom shell. Instead of an entire underside, there is a thin frame around the circumference of the mouse that holds six feet in place. Between them is a chasm that reveals the entire PCB and battery.

Of particular note is the PCB’s function inside the mouse: Instead of simply sitting inside of the shell, the PCB works as a primary structural component. Similar techniques have been used in designing supercars and sport bikes, with the Ferrari F50 using the engine block as a stressed member that provides rigidity to the entire car, a technology borrowed from Ferrari’s F1 team. This provides some obvious benefits to rigidity and weight-savings, but it’s also just undeniably cool to be able to flip over your mouse and see the entire circuit board.

Right behind the PCB is the star of the show, a hot-swappable battery. While I’ve tested quite a few mice with this technology, none of them have managed to make the battery-swapping so satisfying. Instead of being attached with magnets or a rotating locking mechanism, the batteries use a custom-made -spring-loaded ejector similar to an SD card or a Nintendo Switch cartridge. To insert the battery, simply press until you feel a click, and it will lock into place. To remove it, do the same: press until you feel a click, and it will pop out slightly. Of all the mice I’ve tested with removable batteries, this is by far my favorite mechanism to use: It’s intuitive, fun, and feels sturdy.

This battery system does come with a trade-off. Because hot-swappable batteries means an effectively-infinite battery life (as long as you remember to plug the old one into the dongle when you swap), Wallhack has decided to entirely remove the USB-C port on the mouse. This means it only functions as a 2.4G wireless mouse and cannot be plugged or charged in directly. This isn’t a huge issue (it never inconvenienced me, since there was always a fully charged battery ready to swap in), and it theoretically means the connection quality will be better, since there’s fewer components between the antenna and the receiver. But it is worth noting that, if a battery dies or your dongle craps out, there’s no other way to use this mouse.

In every high-polling mouse I’ve reviewed, the dongle has been a bit of a pain point. A high polling rate necessitates proximity, which in turn requires in a small thing sitting on your desk. In most cases, these things have only one or two functions: They connect to the mouse, and they maybe have a small number of LED indicators (between 1 and 3) that can display battery life, connection quality, or some other setting. Almost every time, they’re small and light enough to slide around on your desk and will inevitably end up falling behind it once or twice due to the weight of the cable pulling it back.

The DPI dial features numbering from 400 to 25,600, and the polling dial goes from 125 Hz to 8,000 Hz.

I tended to prefer, for example, a higher DPI for Thing and a lower for Thor. Polling adjustments, too, were useful in situations where older games (like Destiny 2) got grumpy when I was using a mouse above 1,000 Hz.

Like many mice today, the M-001 uses an in-browser customization client. Wallhack’s web client, Wallhack Terminal, takes a unique stylistic approach, relying on a black-and-white, text-based system that resembles Command Prompt more than a typical gaming software. This is both a breath of fresh air stylistically, and much more straightforward than some of the software I’ve used in the past.

The configurator has an extensive feature set, including adjustments for mouse rotation, acceleration, and lift-off distance, which is adjustable by millimeters instead of the vague Low/Medium/High that some softwares use. The sensor can also be pushed to scan faster than the mouse’s 8,000 Hz polling rate, which can potentially improve the accuracy of the sensor, but limits battery life.

Along with adjusting DPI and polling with the dials on the dongle, switching them to the red “DPI” And “Hz” text allows for more granular adjustments within the configurator.

For anyone who doesn’t have an exhaustive list of mouse sensors floating around their head, this is the same type of sensor used in the 2022 Razer Viper V2 Pro (branded as the Razer Focus Pro 30k). That's four years old at this point. It’s not quite a cutting-edge sensor anymore. Of all the mice I’ve tested, the M-001 is by far one of the most enjoyable. The shape is comfortable and natural for multiple grip styles, and the material ensures that the mouse feels rigid and stable despite its light weight. The dongle is a brilliant design that elevates itself from a sad desk tchotchke to a practical and functional device. And the battery mechanism is one of the most clever and fun designs I’ve seen in ages.

Everything about this mouse is deeply satisfying, and I found myself excited to use it every time I sat down. Of course, this mouse isn’t at the cutting edge of sensors or switches, and because of this, I can’t exactly recommend this mouse to someone looking for an ultra-cost-effective option with min-maxed specs. But in using the mouse on the daily, I didn't find that it mattered as much as you might think. 8,000 Hz polling and a 25,600 DPI sensor are enough for all but the pros. Instead, it uses tried-and-true internals that function perfectly well and, more importantly, have crisp and precise tactile responses.

#4089. Just two drinks a day may be far riskier than you think

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Even alcohol consumption that many Americans would describe as moderate may carry significant health risks, including a greater likelihood of premature death, disability, cancer, and heart disease. That is the conclusion of a new study published in the Journal of Studies on Alcohol and Drugs, a journal published at Rutgers University.

The research, known as the Alcohol Intake and Health Study, was originally commissioned by the U.S. federal government to help shape updated national dietary recommendations. Researchers estimated that people who consume an average of 14 alcoholic drinks per week face a 1 in 25 lifetime risk of dying from alcohol-related causes. By comparison, consuming up to seven drinks weekly was linked to only small increases in risk for most health conditions.

"Even low levels of alcohol use come with health risks," says lead study author Kevin Shield, Ph.D., an associate professor at the University of Toronto and a senior scientist who leads the World Health Organization (WHO)/Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) Collaborating Centre in Addiction and Mental Health. "And that risk continues to increase the more someone drinks."

How Scientists Measured the Health Risks of Alcohol

To better understand the long-term consequences of drinking, Shield and his colleagues in the United States and Canada investigated how alcohol consumption over a lifetime contributes to disease and premature death among Americans.

Medical experts first examined more than 7,200 scientific publications covering illnesses and injuries associated with alcohol. Using this evidence, they estimated the risks for individual health conditions and combined those findings with large national health data sets. Statistical models then allowed the team to calculate how different levels of alcohol consumption affect the likelihood of developing serious health problems or dying prematurely.

The findings provide specific estimates that are missing from the latest U.S. Dietary Guidelines. The updated recommendations simply encourage Americans to "limit alcoholic beverages" without identifying a particular consumption threshold. Earlier versions advised men to drink no more than two alcoholic beverages per day and women no more than one.

"While the new U.S. Dietary Guidelines contain a useful 'less-is-best' message, they provide no quantitative framework. Our study was designed to do just that across the drinking spectrum," says study co-author Timothy Naimi, M.D., M.P.H., director of the University of Victoria's Canadian Institute for Substance Use Research and an adjunct professor at Boston University.

"It turns out that two drinks per day, which might be considered 'moderate' from a social standpoint, is associated with a substantially elevated risk of a premature death caused by alcohol," explains Naimi.

Moderate Drinking Offers No Significant Overall Health Benefits

The researchers looked beyond the risk of premature death to examine alcohol's connection to a wide range of immediate and long-term health problems. These included cancers (e.g., esophageal, oral, and breast), cardiovascular disease, liver disease, and injuries.

One of the study's most important findings challenges the longstanding belief that consuming small amounts of alcohol can improve health, particularly heart health.

"We did not observe a significant protective effect of alcohol on health at any level of consumption," says Shield. "At low levels, alcohol may be associated with a reduced risk of ischemic heart disease and stroke. But when you look across the full range of health outcomes, including cancer and other chronic diseases, those potential benefits are outweighed by the risks even at 7 drinks per week."

In other words, while some studies have suggested that light drinking may benefit certain aspects of cardiovascular health, those possible advantages do not translate into an overall health benefit once alcohol's links to cancer and other diseases are taken into account.

Although the analysis drew on extensive scientific evidence, the researchers emphasize that its estimates describe risks across populations rather than predicting precisely what will happen to any particular person.

Shield says the statistical calculations relied on "the best possible data." However, he cautions that individual circumstances can substantially influence the results.

"However, we can't assume that means one person's individual health risk is the same as what is reported here -- that depends on other factors like lifestyle, genetics, drinking patterns, and other choices that differ person to person."

To estimate alcohol's overall impact, the researchers calculated risks for health conditions already known to be caused by alcohol and combined them into a broader assessment. However, scientists are continuing to investigate additional possible consequences of drinking, including emerging evidence connecting alcohol consumption to pancreatic cancer.

"Understanding those relationships, and how much alcohol contributes to those risks, is an area that still needs further work," says Shield.

What the Findings Mean for U.S. Drinking Guidelines

The results suggest that alcohol-related health risks become more pronounced when consumption exceeds one drink per day, regardless of whether the person is male or female. This provides a more specific reference point for people trying to understand how their drinking habits might affect their long-term health.

Rather than suggesting that any particular amount is completely safe, the findings show how risks increase with greater consumption and why drinking less generally reduces exposure to alcohol-related harm.

"Having a clearer threshold helps people better understand what level of drinking is associated with increased risk and make more informed decisions when drinking," says Shield.

The study has also raised questions about how scientific evidence was incorporated into federal dietary recommendations.

In an accompanying editorial, Robert M. Vincent, a former Associate Administrator for the U.S. Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, offers his perspective on the circumstances surrounding the research and its role in the development of national guidance.

"The Alcohol Intake and Health report was explicitly invited to inform alcohol guidance during development of the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025-2030," he writes. "Despite the study's adherence to its mandate, its findings were sidelined."

#4135. Three giant black holes discovered in a galaxy from the dawn of time

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Astronomers have discovered three actively feeding supermassive black holes in a single galaxy more than 12.5 billion light-years from Earth. The finding, made by an international research team led by the Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics (MPE), offers a rare glimpse of how enormous black holes grew and interacted during the universe's earliest stages.

The black holes are located in a galaxy known as J0148-4214. All three are actively accumulating matter, which spirals toward them through surrounding disks of gas and other material. Because the galaxy is so distant, its light has traveled through space for approximately 12.5 billion years before reaching Earth. This means astronomers are observing it as it existed just 1.2 billion years after the Big Bang.

Over such vast distances, the expansion of the universe stretches light to longer, redder wavelengths. Astronomers measure this effect through a property called redshift. For J0148-4214, the researchers measured a redshift of z=5.02, allowing them to determine its remarkable distance.

"This is the first evidence of three active black holes in a single galaxy in the distant Universe," says Hannah Übler, research group leader at MPE and lead author of the study.

Two of the black holes lie near the galaxy's center, separated by a projected distance of just 620 light-years. The third is much farther out, approximately 5,500 light-years from the center.

The discovery provides important evidence about how massive black holes came together in the young universe.

"It suggests that processes in the early Universe were efficient at bringing massive black holes together, setting the stage for the massive black hole mergers we expect to detect with future gravitational wave observatories," says Übler.

Astronomers believe that galaxy collisions and mergers were common during the universe's early history. As galaxies combined, their central black holes could also move closer together and eventually merge. These mergers would produce increasingly massive black holes at the centers of the newly formed galaxies, potentially helping explain how some grew to enormous sizes so soon after the Big Bang.

How Astronomers Detected the Hidden Black Holes

The researchers identified the three black holes by examining distinctive patterns in the light emitted by hydrogen gas. Near a black hole, powerful gravitational forces cause gas to move at extremely high speeds. These movements leave recognizable signatures in the spectrum of light, allowing astronomers to detect black hole activity even when the objects themselves cannot be seen directly.

In the center of J0148-4214, the team found a particularly complicated hydrogen emission pattern. The best explanation was the presence of two closely spaced black holes rather than a single source.

To distinguish between them, the scientists used a precise measurement technique called spectro-astrometry. By tracking subtle changes in the positions of spectral emissions across the galaxy, they were able to identify the locations of both central black holes, even though the telescope could not resolve them as two separate points of light.

The observations also revealed the third active black hole in the galaxy's outer region.

Three Black Holes With Remarkably Different Masses

The three black holes vary dramatically in size. According to the researchers' calculations, their masses are approximately 80 million, 600,000, and 2 million times that of the Sun.

Surprisingly, the largest black hole is not the one feeding most aggressively. Its smaller neighbor, with a mass of roughly 600,000 suns, is accumulating matter at a much higher rate. In fact, its estimated feeding rate exceeds the maximum predicted by basic models of black hole growth (the Eddington limit).

This unusually rapid growth may offer clues to how some black holes became extraordinarily massive during the relatively short time available in the early universe.

"The JWST data allowed us not only to identify the three black holes, but also to estimate their masses, accretion rates, and the stellar mass of the galaxy," says Dr. Giovanni Mazzolari, second author of the study and researcher at MPE. "We find a total stellar mass of about 1.3 billion suns, and the black holes represent a significant fraction of that."

An Ancient Black Hole Merger in the Making

The two black holes near the galaxy's center are expected to merge within the next few hundred million years. Their eventual collision could help produce an even more massive black hole, illustrating a process that may have been essential to the growth of the universe's earliest cosmic giants.

"These results are extremely exciting," adds Roberto Maiolino, professor at the University of Cambridge and co-author of the study. "They suggest that black hole merging may be an additional, fast route for their rapid growth in the early Universe."

The third black hole presents another intriguing puzzle. Located well outside the galaxy's center, it could be a remnant of an earlier merger that was pushed outward by a powerful gravitational recoil. Such a kick can occur when merging black holes emit gravitational waves unevenly, sending the resulting black hole in the opposite direction.

Another possibility is that the third black hole is gradually moving toward the galaxy's center, where it may eventually interact with the other two.

James Webb Reveals Black Holes That Might Otherwise Remain Hidden

The discovery highlights the remarkable capabilities of the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) for studying black holes in distant galaxies.

In particular, the findings demonstrate the importance of integral field spectroscopy, which allows astronomers to examine how light and its spectral signatures vary across different parts of a galaxy.

Using Webb's Near Infrared Spectrograph in its integral field spectroscopy mode (NIRSpec-IFS), the researchers were able to separate signals that would otherwise have appeared to come from a single black hole.

Without that detailed spatial information, astronomers likely would have identified only one of the galaxy's three active black holes.

The results suggest that other distant galaxies may contain multiple growing black holes that have gone undetected. Finding more of these hidden systems could help scientists reconstruct the history of black hole mergers and better understand how the universe produced its most massive black holes so early in cosmic history.

#3708. Scientists Trapped an Antimatter Particle for Months to Study Its Secrets

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Antimatter is not known for being easy to get ahold of. That’s part of why these particles remain so cryptic. But recently, a team of physicists at Northwestern University managed to trap a single antimatter particle for months. Cooling it to temperatures near absolute zero, they could control its quantum states with great precision without even touching it. The experiment, published in Physical Review Letters, represents a breakthrough in our understanding of one of the great mysteries of physics.

In theory, antimatter should be everywhere. Mathematics predicts that every particle of matter has an equivalent antiparticle, a fact that scientists have verified experimentally. The universe should therefore contain similar amounts of both, but almost everything we observe is made up of ordinary matter, and we still don’t know why.

It is possible to produce antimatter particles in laboratories, but studying them is still incredibly difficult. When an antiparticle comes into contact with ordinary matter, the two annihilate each other and release energy. To prevent research facilities from exploding, scientists use vacuum chambers and electromagnetic fields that suspend the antiparticles in isolation. But this only partially solves the problem. Studying these particles, especially experimenting with their behavior on a quantum scale, remains challenging.

For this experiment, researchers at Northwestern trapped a positron, the antimatter counterpart of electrons. The team, led by Gerald Gabrielse, developed a device known as a single-positron quantum cyclotron, which used electric and magnetic fields to hold the positron in a vacuum at a temperature close to absolute zero. This means the antimatter particle was at its lowest energy state, allowing the researchers to induce controlled changes in its quantum behavior. Unlike other methods in particle physics, this approach didn’t destroy the particle. The researchers were therefore able to keep the single antiparticle trapped for months, allowing them to repeat measurements.

So what can taming antimatter particles actually teach us? Scientists know that matter and antimatter have very similar properties. For example, an electron and a positron have exactly the same mass, although their electric charges are opposite. However, questions still remain about exactly how similar these two forms of matter are and whether there are small differences that have gone unnoticed in experiments conducted to date.

To this end, researchers want to compare the magnetic properties of both particles. Physicists have already made very precise measurements with electrons, but achieving the same level of precision with positrons has proven more difficult. According to the American Physical Society, the new device could enable measurements of antimatter particles about 30 times more precise than scientists were capable of before.

So far, the experiment has not revealed any unexpected differences between the two particles. What the researchers have achieved is an improvement in their ability to detect these differences in antimatter particles. If future measurements find that a positron responds to magnetic fields differently than an electron, scientists would have to revise some of the laws they use to explain the behavior of matter and antimatter.

#4148. New evidence suggests spinosaurus spent most of its life underwater

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

For millions of years, evolution helped animals make the transition from life in the ocean to life on land. But the journey did not always move in one direction. Many species eventually returned to the water, developing new adaptations that allowed them to thrive in aquatic environments.

Dozens of major groups of mammals and reptiles made this remarkable evolutionary reversal. Now, a new study from Yale University is shedding light on when and how these transitions occurred, as well as which ancient animals became fully aquatic and which continued to depend on land.

The findings were published in the journal Current Biology.

"These secondarily aquatic groups adapted in strikingly similar ways to their new aquatic home -- evolving flippers and a suite of other features that made them better swimmers," said lead author Caleb Gordon, who completed his doctorate at Yale's Graduate School of Arts and Sciences (GSAS) earlier this year and is now a postdoctoral researcher at the Florida Museum of Natural History. "As a result, they've become textbook examples of convergent evolution, which can tell us a lot about the processes driving and constraining adaptive change in response to similar environmental cues."

Using Ancient Fossils and AI to Reconstruct Evolution

To investigate these evolutionary changes, the researchers examined hundreds of fossil and modern specimens housed at the Yale Peabody Museum and dozens of other institutions worldwide. They collected extensive new data, including measurements (more than 11,000), photographs, and CT scans.

The team combined traditional paleontological techniques with machine learning models that account for evolutionary relationships. They also incorporated a statistical approach originally used by naval forces during World War II. Together, these methods offered a new way to investigate one of the most remarkable transitions in vertebrate evolution.

"Reconstructing the lives of extinct life forms in a scientifically rigorous way, as opposed to just telling stories, is a precise and delicate undertaking," said Bhart-Anjan Bhullar, senior author of the study. Bhullar is an associate professor of Earth and planetary sciences in Yale's Faculty of Arts and Sciences (FAS) and an associate curator of vertebrate paleontology and vertebrate zoology at the Peabody Museum.

"It requires a careful interweaving of data from modern organisms, of which our understanding is necessarily far deeper, and knowledge of these living organisms' genealogy relative to fossil forms," he said. "Caleb's work accomplishes all of this and more, and on a massive evolutionary scale."

For decades, scientists have disagreed about when particular groups of prehistoric animals became fully adapted to life underwater. The fossil record often provides incomplete or conflicting clues, making it difficult to determine whether an extinct species primarily lived on land, in water, or in both environments.

Some skeletal features may indicate an aquatic lifestyle, while others suggest that the same animal remained capable of moving and feeding on land. These contradictions have led researchers to different conclusions about how ancient species behaved.

"In these cases, paleontologists are often stuck, as different lines of evidence disagree about what the ancient animal was like," Gordon said.

To address this uncertainty, Gordon developed a method using relatively simple but effective machine learning models trained on living animals. By identifying relationships between skeletal proportions and known lifestyles, the models can estimate how extinct species used aquatic environments. They can also predict soft tissue adaptations, including swimming structures that rarely survive in fossils.

Spinosaurus May Have Spent Most of Its Life Underwater

One of the most striking findings concerns Spinosaurus, a giant prehistoric dinosaur whose relationship with water has generated considerable controversy.

Spinosaurus lived approximately 113 million to 94 million years ago in what is now northern Africa. Although scientists generally agree that it was associated with aquatic environments, exactly how it moved, hunted, and spent its time remains a subject of debate.

Some evidence points to an animal that frequently submerged itself and pursued prey underwater, much like seals or penguins. Other findings suggest a lifestyle closer to that of a modern heron, with Spinosaurus walking and searching for food along shorelines and in shallow water.

The Yale team's analysis provides new support for the more aquatic interpretation.

"Our results shed new light on how much time Spinosaurus spent submerged, which could support the underwater hunting view," Gordon said. "We confidently recovered highly aquatic habits for Spinosaurus, indicating that it spent the vast majority of its time submerged in the water."

Although the findings do not settle every question about how Spinosaurus hunted, they strengthen the case that this enormous predator spent much more of its life underwater than on land.

Ancient Marine Reptiles That Never Fully Left Land

The researchers reached a different conclusion about mesosaurs, small marine reptiles that lived between 290 million and 274 million years ago in regions now forming parts of South Africa and eastern South America.

Mesosaurs have long interested paleontologists because they gave birth to live young, a characteristic some researchers have interpreted as evidence of a fully aquatic existence. However, the new analysis suggests that their limbs were not adapted for a life spent entirely in water.

Instead, their skeletal proportions indicate that mesosaurs regularly moved between aquatic and terrestrial environments.

"They spent a lot of time on land, like a modern-day alligator or platypus does," Gordon said. "Mesosaurs didn't completely leave the land behind."

The findings also suggest a broader pattern among the earliest marine reptiles. According to the researchers, all Paleozoic marine reptiles (all marine reptiles around before the dinosaurs appeared) continued to return to land regularly. None appeared to have evolved a completely aquatic lifestyle, with even the most water adapted species remaining amphibious.

The researchers found that forelimb proportions were particularly useful for distinguishing these lifestyles. Using this information, their models could predict with more than 90% accuracy whether an animal possessed soft tissue flippers and whether it had highly or fully aquatic habits.

A World War II Technique Helps Solve an Evolutionary Mystery

An especially unusual part of the research involved adapting a statistical method developed during the 1940s.

"Caleb resurrected a statistical technique from a bygone era," said co-author Jacques Gauthier, a professor of Earth and planetary sciences at Yale and curator-in-charge for vertebrate paleontology at the Peabody Museum. "It was developed during WWII to estimate the probability that an approaching blip on the radar screen was an enemy plane. Caleb cleverly used it to get at whether or not an extinct animal had limbs modified for swimming."

By repurposing this approach, the researchers could assess the likelihood that an extinct species possessed specialized swimming adaptations, even when direct evidence of those features was missing.

The method could eventually help scientists investigate other major changes in evolutionary history. Potential applications include studying when human ancestors developed the ability to walk on two legs and how the dinosaur ancestors of birds evolved the ability to fly.

Because most prehistoric vertebrates are known primarily through their fossilized skeletons, reconstructing their behavior is one of paleontology's greatest challenges. Bones preserve valuable information, but they rarely provide a complete picture of how an animal lived.

Traditionally, scientists have interpreted particular skeletal features to make arguments about the lifestyles of extinct species. Those interpretations can differ significantly, especially when fossils are incomplete or several anatomical characteristics point toward different conclusions.

The new approach offers a more systematic way to evaluate that evidence, using patterns observed in living species to make testable predictions about animals that disappeared millions of years ago.

"Bones are all that is left to us of the vast majority of vertebrate animals that have lived on Earth," Bhullar said. "We want to know so very much about these creatures and the succession of lost worlds they inhabited, but we have so little on which to base our conclusions. From skeletal remains, people have tried to discern life habits, generally making an argument on the basis of one preferred set of features or another. Caleb's method removes the subjectivity from this process and allows the data to speak for themselves."

The study's additional co-authors are Lisa Freisem, a doctoral student at Yale's Graduate School of Arts and Sciences, and Christopher Griffin, an assistant professor of geosciences at Princeton University who previously worked as a postdoctoral fellow at Yale.
