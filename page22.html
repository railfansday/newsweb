#3238. On the biggest stage, Junior Caminero and Jackson Chourio are emerging as national superstars

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

MILWAUKEE -- World Series discussions often center around the superstars -- especially if there are those easy ones we can call "future Hall of Famers." Two years ago, when the Los Angeles Dodgers and New York Yankees faced off, there were at least five active players that fit the bill and probably a few more with a decent chance. This time around, the Milwaukee Brewers and Tampa Bay Rays don't have any surefire Hall of Famers. Christian Yelich is the closest thing, and he might be short (it's a different discussion for a different time).

I think there's a good argument to be made, however, that the best position player on each team is, in fact, a future Hall of Famer. It's far too early to make such a declaration, but they have the early foundation and the required talent. Both are emerging superstars, though one is a little more nationally established than the other (thank you, Home Run Derby). They'll get their spotlight together in this series.

We're speaking, of course, about Junior Caminero of the Rays and Jackson Chourio of the Brewers.

Now, they aren't unknowns. I'm not suggesting that. But I'll also excuse you if you don't regularly watch either of these teams. Regardless, we are all about to be blessed with a significant part of the next wave of incredible baseball talent.

Caminero is a 23-year-old Dominican third baseman. His first full-time season was 2025, during which he clubbed 45 homers with 110 RBI. As an encore this season, he hit 42 home runs with 101 RBI. Don't mistake the line as regression. Though the power numbers dipped ever so slightly there, he raised his average by 12 points, his on-base percentage by 49 points and his walk rate by five percentage points, all while his strikeout rate dropped. To see this type of growth from one year to the next at age 23 while nearly maintaining his power leads me to believe there's an untapped extra gear in him as a player. We might well see him hit close to .300 next season and duplicate those 40 bombs. He also improved on defense, helping him vault to 5.7 WAR from last season's 4.3.

"I think the upside is just the consistency," Rays outfielder Cedric Mullins said on Thursday. "He's put some fantastic seasons together in back-to-back years. Even when he started off a little slow, he bounced back with a crazy hot streak. It's going to be exciting to see what he can do moving forward."

"Junior is a special player, special teammate," Rays manager Kevin Cash said earlier in the playoffs. "He's been incredible and would expect that, just as he's done every single stop throughout his career, whether it's playing winter ball or coming up, he's going to be very excited, motivated, and we'll rise to the occasion."

Caminero might well finish in the top two of MVP voting, behind favorite Yordan Alvarez, and I've seen enough to say with decent certainty that he's a future MVP.

Rays catcher Liam Hicks, acquired at the trade deadline, raved about something as simple as getting to watch Caminero in batting practice every day, but focused specifically on his ability to adjust and adapt at the big-league level.

"That's the crazy thing, too: how much power he has, how many home runs he hits," Hicks said. "He doesn't strike out a lot; he makes a ton of contact. I think that part of his game is just crazy. He's got as much upside as anyone in the game."

Chourio, meanwhile, is a 22-year-old Venezuelan (and World Baseball Classic champion!) who signed an eight-year extension before he even debuted in the majors. He started playing professionally at age 17 in the Brewers system and was a top five prospect in all of baseball by 2023, when he was just 19. There's a five-tool superstar in here just waiting to have a monster breakout season. Here in 2026, he had his best year, but there's clearly more in the tank. Remember, he's only 22. Injuries held him to 127 games this year, but he still hit .291/.351/.487 (129 OPS+) with 27 doubles, 25 home runs, 75 RBI, 96 runs, 23 steals and 4.9 WAR. He's a Gold Glove finalist and he's gone 20-20 in all three of his MLB seasons so far. He can do it all.

"Chourio is, he's special, man," Brewers pitcher Chad Patrick said on Thursday during media day. "When you watch him in the box, his presence, his tone, he's capable of being anything he wants to be in the box.

"His day-in, day-out is just happy. He's a happy guy."

"The ceiling he has is so high that we don't know if he'll ever hit it," reliever DL Hall said. "You know, just because he just keeps getting better and better and it seems like there's no limit."

"Playing with him every day for me and the rest of the bullpen, every time he gets up, we're like, 'he's probably gonna hit a homer.' Every single time he comes up to the plate, you just expect something special. It speaks to the work he puts in and how focused he is, especially at such a young age."

It's impossible to miss the talent just oozing out of Chourio. There's an earned swagger that says we ain't seen nothin' yet. Another future MVP? He absolutely has the talent, even if he's further away than Caminero at this point -- and that's no insult. Chourio has also shown a flair for the dramatic.

"No moment's too big for Jack Jack," Brewers manager Pat Murphy said after NLCS Game 3, when Chourio hit a first-pitch home run off former Cy Young winner Blake Snell. "He's special."

Brewers pitcher Dustin May has seen his fair share of talented teammates, including the likes of Shohei Ohtani, Freddie Freeman, Mookie Betts, Cody Bellinger and Christian Yelich. And that's only naming his teammates who have won MVPs.

"I can't say no," May said when CBS Sports asked if Chourio would join that list. "He's definitely got it in there, for sure. If he can put it together one year like I know he can, he'll win one."

"I think [MVP upside] is there for sure," said Patrick. "He's learned the game over the last three years and he definitely has the upside to win it."

Caminero and Chourio, the young studs of their respective clubs, are different players. The former is a corner infielder and prototypical middle-order slugger, even if he's more well-rounded than some might believe that term implies. The latter is an outfielder with great speed, capable of a 30-30 season as soon as next year, but he's unlikely to get up into the mid-40s in home runs the way Caminero figures to do annually. Caminero won't steal a ton of bases, while Chourio could well go for 40+ someday.

But no matter the skill set, they have something undeniably in common: they're the best position players on their team at a tender, young age. The 2026 World Series is a showcase for these two uber-talented players. It probably won't be the last.

The 2026 World Series matchup between the Milwaukee Brewers and Tampa Bay Rays is rich with subplots. Neither team has won a World Series, so we're in for a first when it comes time to hoist the trophy. That hasn't happened since the Philadelphia Phillies and Kansas City Royals met in the 1980 World Series.

There's also the backdrop of the ongoing negotiations on a new collective bargaining agreement. MLB, its constituent team owners, and commissioner Rob Manfred -- who takes his marching orders from those owners -- are pushing for a cap on payrolls. Indeed, everything the management side has proposed in those negotiations has been tied to the players' acceptance of a cap, and everything has been rejected by the players' union as long as it is tied to said cap.

From a rhetorical standpoint, this World Series matchup is quite inconvenient for Manfred and the owners. These are two of the smallest markets in MLB with two bottom-tier payrolls. The Rays especially don't invest much in the on-field product. Consider:

Add those payroll rankings together, and it's the lowest on record for any World Series. It's the smallest combined market size for a World Series since the St. Louis Cardinals and Kansas City Royals met in 1985. These realities are even more striking once you consider that, in 2026, the Brewers swept the dynastic Los Angeles Dodgers in the NLCS, while the Rays vanquished the historically preeminent New York Yankees in the AL East race and then again in the ALDS.

How do you argue that super-spenders like the Dodgers and Yankees, or even disappointments like the Mets and Blue Jays, are breaking baseball when this is your World Series? MLB will, of course, argue it anyway, but the narrative packs less of a punch this October.

To be sure, there's a definite element of ownership neglect with those payroll figures. Both teams are revenue-sharing recipients on balance, and such clubs that run low payrolls make enough via revenue sharing to cover most or, in some cases, all of their cash payroll. Circumstance plays an obvious role, yes, but so does the will to compete and win on the part of owners.

Still, the question remains: How have the Brewers and Rays built championship-grade rosters without spending much at all?

In terms of a tactical approach, both have seen high contact rates on offense as a neglected trait across the league, and they've selected for that. Like most other clubs, they prioritize velocity and repertoire depth on the pitching front. For these purposes, though, we're talking about the paths to roster construction, and, unsurprisingly, the Brewers and Rays have a lot in common in their preferred means of player acquisition (and not just because Brewers president of baseball operations Matt Arnold spent nine years in the Rays front office under Andrew Friedman).

The temptation is to think that both of these small-budget teams are heavily homegrown. That's the best way to get cheap production across the roster, especially when many of those homegrown talents are in their salary-controlled years. As we'll see, though, that's not really the case. These two teams are among the very best at identifying and developing talent acquired via the draft and through amateur international signings. As noted, these two clubs don't invest as much as they should in player payroll, but they do have large and wide-ranging baseball operations and scouting staffs within their front offices. The Brewers and Rays know their own players, of course, but if there's a superpower, it's how well they appraise players, especially young ones, in other organizations. That skill informs much of their current success.

To begin digging into the matter of how the low-payroll, wee-market Rays and Brewers built their rosters, we'll look at how they compare to the other 10 teams that made the postseason in 2026 when it comes to the three most common paths to player acquisition: free agents, homegrown talents, and trade acquisitions. We'll not worry about waiver claims and Rule 5 draftees, as those typically provide less or even negligible value.

First, let's look at each playoff team's number of homegrown players -- i.e., those initially signed as amateurs by the club in question and who have never played for another club -- on the current 40-man roster (note that most teams do not presently have all 40 spots filled, so these roster totals will vary from club to club):

The Brewers and Rays, as you can see, both rank in the bottom half among 2026 playoff teams when it comes to homegrown players on the 40. In the Rays' case, the most notable fully in-house talents are Jonathan Aranda, Chandler Simpson, Taylor Walls, and Ian Seymour. Additionally, lefty starter and ace-when-healthy Shane McClanahan, who's been out since early September with forearm soreness and presumably won't be able to pitch in the World Series, came up through the organization.

As for the Brewers, they're not the most homegrown of playoff teams, but one can argue no one's collection of internal talents has moved the needle quite so much. Consider that the Brewers' position players and pitchers during the regular season combined for a WAR of 50.4. Making up slightly more than half of that tally were five homegrown players: Jacob Misiorowski, Jackson Chourio, Brice Turang, Garrett Mitchell, and Logan Henderson. This is to say nothing of Cooper Pratt and Aaron Ashby, whom the Brewers also drafted and developed.

Next up is free agents, and these two World Series combatants aren't high on that list either:

The Rays' highest-salaried free agent addition is 36-year-old starter Nick Martinez, who inked a one-year, $13 million pact for this season. His deal also includes a largely meaningless mutual option for 2027. Martinez, who put up a 3.9 WAR during the regular season and figures to make at least one World Series start against the Brewers, has more than justified the investment from the team's standpoint. Lefty Steven Matz is on a two-year, $15 million contract, which makes him the Rays' largest investment on the market. Needless to say, the Rays aren't built through free agency.

The same goes for Milwaukee. The Brewers' largest market investment is Brandon Woodruff, who accepted the qualifying offer this past offseason and is being paid $22 million this season. Only team captain and organizational stalwart Christian Yelich is being paid a higher salary by the Brewers this year ($26 million as part of a nine-year, $215 million extension signed in 2020). Woodruff, though, isn't what we think of when it comes to typical free agent additions. He's an organizational lifer who's been with the Brewers since signing with them in 2014.

Woodruff is one of the best starting pitchers in baseball when he's healthy, but injuries have been the throughline of his career. Maybe, though, he'll be a surprise contributor in the World Series, as he's been assiduously working his way back from dogged shoulder issues. In terms of more traditional free agents signed away from other clubs, catcher and bench thunder-wielder Gary Sánchez is the Brewers' biggest current investment. He's on a one-year, $1.75 million contract.

Finally, we arrive at trade acquisitions. The majority of each team's 40-man roster is populated at a majority level by trade pick-ups:

Such frequency speaks to the Rays' and Brewers' established knack for knowing which players to target in trade and, broadly speaking, which players to give up. The "never trade with the Rays" contemporary chestnut of recent baseball wisdom is overstated -- they can and do lose trades -- but there's no question that trades have done the heavy lifting in the present roster's high level of success.

The Rays made their boldest stroke in acquiring 22-year-old burgeoning superstar Junior Caminero via trade with the Guardians in November 2021, when he was just an 18-year-old in the Dominican Summer League. Today, even just two full seasons into Caminero's MLB career, it looks like a franchise-altering trade for the Rays. That's not all, though. Yandy Díaz was also acquired in a trade with Cleveland, this one roughly three years before the Caminero swap. Closer Bryan Baker came to the Rays in a 2025 deadline trade with the division-rival Orioles. Speaking of the deadline, lead executive Erick Neander earlier this season raised the Rays' ceiling quite substantially by going out and getting ALCS MVP Freddy Peralta from the Mets, catcher Liam Hicks from the Marlins, and key setup man Tyler Wells from, again, the Orioles.

The Rays accumulated a WAR of 41.2 this season, and their trade additions make up 23.9 WAR of that total. It's a team, both in raw numbers and overall contributions, built mostly through perhaps the most direct test of an organization's faculties -- some of mine for some of yours.

There's some connective tissue here, too. As for Rasmussen, he's been one of MLB's top starting pitchers over the last two years, and his overall body of work with the Rays is exceptional. As for the Brewers and top decision-maker Matt Arnold, trades have also left a deep footprint on their current roster. The most famous trade in recent Brewers history was one made by Arnold's predecessor, David Stearns (coincidentally, he now leads the Mets, who traded for Peralta in the 2025-26 offseason). That was the 2018 blockbuster that brought Yelich to Milwaukee. Yelich is post-peak, but the newly named captain is still a vital presence for Milwaukee. Elsewhere -- and more germane to the current Brewers model -- trades for William Contreras, Jake Bauers, Andrew Vaughn, Trevor Megill, Robert Gasser, and Chad Patrick played a vital role in the team's success this season. Arnold, like the Rays' Neander, was also active at the deadline, as he fortified the pitching staff with Dustin May, JoJo Romero, and Antonio Senzatela.

Trade additions claimed both LCS MVP awards (Peralta for the Rays, Bauers for the Brewers), and it likewise wouldn't be shocking if a player acquired by the Rays or Brewers via trade winds up earning top individual honors in the World Series. That's a predictable consequence when the two World Series clubs have combined for, oh, almost 50 trade acquisitions.

In broad terms, the Rays and Brewers have built their championship rosters in the ways that low-spenders must. Milwaukee has made quite a lot of trades, but extracted most of its value in 2026 from homegrown players. The Rays have made even more trades, and most of their 2026 contributions have indeed come from outsiders.

When it comes to World Series teams of a recent vintage, this is something of a departure -- one worth appreciating and, no doubt, emulating if you're a team that aspires to reach the heights that the Brewers and Rays presently occupy.

#3221. Colts' Anthony Richardson facing charge for marijuana possession, per report

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Indianapolis Colts quarterback Anthony Richardson Sr. was hit with a misdemeanor charge for marijuana possession earlier this month, according to court records obtained by ESPN.

"We are aware of the situation involving Anthony Richardson and are currently gathering additional information," the Colts said in a statement. "We will have no further comment at this time."

Police pulled Richardson over on Oct. 12 after officers had questions about the car's registration. Police smelled marijuana in the car, and the quarterback admitted to smoking in the vehicle to avoid smoking marijuana at his house around his children. A search of the vehicle revealed a small amount of marijuana and THC-infused gummies that were confiscated by authorities.

The former No. 4 overall pick was not arrested and was allowed to leave. But he was issued a citation for an expired registration and a summons for the charge of marijuana possession.

Richardson, who is in the final year of his rookie deal, has not appeared in a game this season and was given permission to seek a trade in February. However, no trade partner appeared, and he returned to the fold to serve as Daniel Jones' backup.

As a prospect, Richardson was recognized for his athletic ability. He actually became the most athletic quarterback to ever go through the NFL combine process up to that point, checking in with impressive measurements at 6-foot-4, 244 pounds, then running an official 4.43 40-yard dash, registering a 40.5-inch vertical leap, plus a 10-foot-9 broad jump. It led recently fired general manager Chris Ballard to use the Colts' top pick in the 2023 NFL Draft on the Florida product despite his lack of experience, as Richardson had made just 13 starts.

Richardson went 2-2 as a starter during his rookie season before a shoulder injury ended his year early. There was considerable excitement surrounding his entering 2024, as Richardson scored seven total touchdowns over his first three NFL games. However, an oblique injury sidelined him in Week 4, and then came the infamous incident in Week 8 against the Houston Texans, when Richardson took himself out of the game because he "needed a breather." Richardson was then benched for Joe Flacco, but returned to the starting lineup three weeks later.
