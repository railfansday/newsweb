#1320. Will Jürgen Klopp's charisma be enough for Germany?

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

After defeat to Greece, Jürgen Klopp faces the first real test of his tenure as coach of Germany's national men's football team. Can his charisma alone push this group forward?

In his first season in charge at Liverpool, Jürgen Klopp lost just one of his first five games.

Germany hopes for a similar run emerges after a shock 1-0 defeat to Greece, a team that hasn't qualified for a major international tournament in 12 years. The defeat left Klopp with just one point after his first two games in charge of the national team.

True to form, the head coach took responsibility for the loss, shielding this new crop of Germany players from criticism.

"We have been together for a week now, all mixed together, and that needs time," Klopp said after the match.

"Let the new players develop. The established players are top players at their clubs, and they have my support. Feel free to criticize me, but not the boys. We don't need to completely tear everything apart. I was surprised that some people booed. The future is as bright as we make it. We'll take it on with everything we have."

He also emphasized development over short-term panic, citing the need for more training sessions to cement the automatic responses needed to break down well-organized defenses.

Herein lies the challenge for one of football's greatest coaches. In likely his final job as a head coach, can the multiple league winner adapt his coaching methods to work in the national team? Klopp, a coach and a person famous for his ability to develop relationships and grow a team at a club, has said he believes he can adapt.

His legacy at Borussia Dortmund and Liverpool leaves no doubt that his communication style is what this team needs. His charismatic focus on developing a culture and growing collectively in connection with the fans is what is required.

After many years of frustration and pain watching Germany's men at major tournaments, the biggest mistake would be to tear down this new group before it even begins.

The team both needs and deserves the time to figure out how far it can go. Bambase Conte and Hennes Behrens made their debuts against Greece after a host of others had done so against the Netherlands.

These are the first steps towards finding out who will drive Germany towards an improved outing at the 2028 European Championship. It is the main reason why Klopp chose so many players for these first four games.

But what if, for all of Klopp's positivity and culture building, this new generation of German players is just not good enough to win a trophy? Clearly, it is too soon to know the answer, as Klopp himself has said.

"We want to help prepare these players to be competing at the very top over the next two or three years," Klopp had said at his unveiling.

"We took a different approach and focused not on what we don't have, but on what we do have."

What Germany has right now is both a new team looking to grow and a team that just suffered their first-ever defeat to Greece.

At Liverpool, it took Jürgen Klopp two years and approximately 500 training sessions to reach the point where the team looked like his. In the 2017-18 season, they reached the Champions League final. Alongside the time it took for the team to learn the new system, new signings, notably Mohamed Salah, Roberto Firmino and Sadio Mane, improved the quality.

Klopp doesn't have that option with the German national team. He has to do more with fewer training sessions — Klopp will have approximately 200 before Euro 2028. The closest he can get to new signings is by calling up uncapped Germany players — there were 11 first-time call-ups in this Germany squad.

The 59-year-old said his whole career brought him to this job. It might well prove to be the greatest challenge of his career, too.

#1319. Widow of Middlesbrough icon Willie Maddren calls for greater protection for footballers over link between headers and fatal MND - which caused Rob Burrow's premature death

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Published: 18:51 BST, 22 October 2026 | Updated: 12:08 BST, 23 October 2026

Long before Rob Burrow's brutal illness and premature death launched Sir Kevin Sinfield on his quest to raise millions for research into motor neurone disease (MND) there was Willie Maddren.

For Teessiders of a certain age, Maddren needs no introduction.

For the benefit of others, he was a local hero at the heart of a glorious era for Middlesbrough, a fearless centre forward converted into a fearless centre half in Jack Charlton's brilliant team promoted as runaway second-tier champions in 1974.

They rattled the elite on their return to the top flight with an unlikely tilt at the title before a late fade to finish seventh.

England recognition came for Maddren at Under-23 level and senior call-ups in the 1974-75 season, but there would be no full cap before knee problems set in and cut short his playing career.

At 26, with 351 Boro appearances behind him, he retired. Later, he returned as physio and manager and, at the age of 44, was diagnosed with MND. He died in 2000 at 49.

After his death at the age of 49, his widow Hilary has become outspoken raising awareness of the connection between the disease and his playing career

Maddren played 351 games for Middlesbrough and was forced to retire aged 26

More than a quarter of a century on, Sinfield is on a mission to beat the disease that killed his team-mate Burrow, raising more than £11million to fund much-needed research.

There is still no sign of a cure. Precious little is known about the causes but as the list of sports stars with MND extends, Maddren's widow Hilary has become increasingly convinced that their athletic pursuits are central to the problem, whether that be the shuddering contact in rugby or heading the ball in football.

'There's got to be a connection,' Hilary tells Daily Mail Sport from her home in North Yorkshire. 'I follow it all closely and it takes me back with a slight sadness to see it happening to such a huge extent.

'It's quite frightening there's so many of them, that's the problem. I realise that you can't just go around bandying these things about, but it feels strange to me nothing is said.

'We know in football, heading the ball is causing neurodegenerative diseases. Years ago, we found out there was a bigger chance of getting lung cancer if you smoked. Not everybody with lung cancer smoked but now we know it is a contributing factor.

'When you see the percentage of rugby players and footballers, this has got to be the same thing with MND. There's nothing definitive, I know nothing is proven but it's got to be.

'I'm not trying to blame anybody. Willie would not want to blame anybody. I'm not on a witch hunt. I don't want to stop anyone playing the game, but I have nine grandchildren and nearly all of them are football mad. It's about education and being aware of heading the ball.'

The Maddrens are one of five families – all connected to Boro – leading a new campaign for clear legal limits to reduce heading in training which they believe will protect future generations from the post-career health problems they have witnessed at close quarters.

It is led by the Head Safe charity founded by Judith Gates, whose husband was a friend and teammate of Maddren's. Bill Gates, who retired before he was 30 suffering serious migraines, died three years ago at the age of 79 after living for years with dementia.

Others at the forefront of the campaign are families of England World Cup hero Nobby Stiles, Gordon McQueen and Hugh McIlmoyle.

Stiles, who died in 2020 aged 78, played for Boro after leaving Manchester United. Scotland legend McQueen, who died in 2023 aged 70, coached at the club for seven years and later worked for them as a scout.

McIlmoyle, 86 years old and diagnosed with Alzheimer's eight years ago, spent two seasons at Ayresome Park during his prolific career as a centre-forward in the 60s and 70s.

Gary Pallister, a former Boro, Manchester United and England defender, is also backing the cause.

When Pallister spoke at the launch of a new petition which the group hopes will force the matter into the House of Commons, he was embarrassed because he could not summon the name of Maddren, the man who had plucked him from the obscurity of non-league Billingham Town.

Former Manchester United star Gary Pallister is among the ex-players to throw their support behind the cause

Hilary teased him afterwards. 'Oh my God, you've got it already,' she said, although acutely aware that behind this light-hearted exchange between friends lurked a serious point.

'Gary and his contemporaries are into their early 60s. They've played their football and headed all those balls. It must be a little bit worrying when they start forgetting things.

'What's ahead? Are they going to suffer dementia? It feels like a ticking time bomb.'

Studies have proved the link between heading the ball and chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE), a degenerative brain disease which can lead in later life to different forms of dementia.

MND is slightly different but nevertheless a neurodegenerative condition which damages the nerves sending messages to the muscles. Over time, they stop working and the muscles weaken. It is terminal and often health deteriorates quickly.

Maddren's first sign of the disease was a twitch in a thumb later spreading to an arm, then to the other arm, by which point it had become clear it was not a trapped nerve as he hoped.

In search of reason, he wondered if it was to do with stress from the end of his playing career, and the long hours spent on his business running sports shops and managing Boro in crisis when the club he loved was going into liquidation.

But there seemed no point wasting time searching for answers he could never expect to find, he was determined to spend what limited time he had trying to enjoy life and make a difference.

Middlesbrough held a benefit match and proceeds enabled Maddren to adapt his home and transport to his wheelchair, and he threw himself into raising funds and awareness.

He took part in a clinical trial for riluzole, a drug that is now prescribed in treatment.

Proceeds from his book 'Extra Time' went to the MND Association and funds continued to be raised long after his death, estimated close to £500,000. His family including four children from two marriages, friends, former colleagues and Boro fans joined forces.

His eldest daughter, artist Lucy Pittaway, maintains an active partnership with the charity.

'Willie was convinced if you threw enough money at it, you would find a cause and a cure,' says Hilary, who has continued to campaign tirelessly and worked in volunteer roles for MND charities and others.

'We still don't have a huge amount of hope anybody can be given when they receive the diagnosis. It's not a lot different.

'The incredible amount raised in Rob Burrow's name will make huge inroads.'

Leeds Rhinos legend Burrow died in 2024 at the age of 37. Other international stars from the two codes of rugby include Scotland's Doddie Weir who died in 2022 at 52 and Joost van der Westhuizen of South Africa who died in 2017 at 45.

Lewis Moody, 48, revealed last year he was living with MND. Earlier this month, former Wallabies hooker Nathan Charles, 37, revealed he too had been diagnosed.

The MND Association notes the patterns but notes 'correlation does not prove causation' and that the health benefits of playing sport far outweigh any risks involved.

From football, Marcus Stewart and Stephen Darby are living with MND. Former Rangers favourite Fernando Ricksen died in 2019 at the age of 43. Lenny Johnrose, who played for Bury and Burnley among others, died in 2022 at the age of 52.

'Willie loved his life,' says Hilary. 'He thought he'd had an amazing life and was so lucky to have done the things he wanted to do. He played football for a living. He managed his club, albeit that wasn't brilliant, but he was involved in football, he loved it.

'And if you would have said to him, well this might be the cause of what's going to kill you, would he have done any different? Possibly not. And that's how I feel now. Don't blame anybody, let's draw a line, but we need to learn from it.

'Anybody who has seen the slow-motion footage of a clash of heads or a rugby tackle when you see the head thrown back, it's not a good sign, it's not the way our bodies are meant to work and it's got to be doing something. It's just got to be.'

Even at the earliest junctures of the season, the UEFA Champions League is a showcase for the world's best players on its very best days. Tuesday was no exception, this time providing a platform for a batch of players that usually play supporting roles to their headline-stealing teammates.

Paris Saint-Germain once again earned the distinction of being the Champions League's headliners, outdoing Barcelona in a 4-2 win that was the latest in a string of instant classics between two of Europe's most entertaining teams. The back-to-back champions surged to victory while relying on a wide range of talent, though they were hardly the only ones to showcase their depth. Liverpool's 4-3 win over Villarreal came courtesy of 18-year-old Rio Ngumoha, while Manchester City called upon Phil Foden in a 5-1 win over AEK Athens.

Depth will naturally be a theme of the Champions League campaign as it progresses, long seasons in store for title contenders and the teams destined for early exits. Tuesday's games offered an early glimpse at how several teams might fare down the stretch if things go according to plan – and who might throw a wrench in their plans, should the knockout phase draw put any of these teams on a collision course. Bodo/Glimt, after all, would like a word when it comes to the cute underdog narrative.

Here's what we learned from Tuesday's Champions League action.

Credit to the new Champions League format: it always forces questions early in the season with high-profile matchups and these days, one question in particular: Paris Saint-Germain can't possibly keep this up, right? It was a reasonable query – Barcelona seemed as well-positioned as any to challenge the back-to-back European champions, who saw both Ousmane Dembele and Khvicha Kvaratskhelia exit the game with injuries. PSG took the lead twice, only for Barcelona to eventually find an equalizer. Things were hardly straightforward for the Parisians but by the final whistle, they had an answer to the week's most pressing question: they can, in fact, keep this up.

As Dembele and Kvaratskhelia exited the pitch, a series of teammates filled in the gaps and in a variety of ways. There was Desire Doue, who scored the game's first goal and bolstered his argument as PSG's most entertaining forward with an assist on PSG's second. Then there was the midfield trio of Vitinha, Fabian Ruiz and Joao Neves, who did the one thing that seemed most challenging – shutting down a Barcelona midfield designed to let Lamine Yamal shine. They could not keep the teen sensation at bay entirely but that was hardly a reason to stress, since each member of the trio tallied a goal contribution. Vitinha and Ruiz bagged the game's final goals, ones that finally put things out of reach for Barcelona, Vitinha's strike among the most impressive on a day of bangers. His exuberant celebration was well-earned because PSG continue to make the game's most predictable storyline endlessly entertaining.

The accumulation of persistent plastics in the ocean is causing wide-ranging environmental problems1,2,3. Some commercially available compostable plastics4,5,6,7, developed to address these issues, degrade efficiently under industrial composting conditions; however, their degradation in marine environments, particularly in the deep sea, remains limited8,9,10. Here we demonstrate that thermally embedding enzymes into compostable polyesters (poly(butylene succinate) (PBS), poly(butylene succinate-co-adipate) (PBSA), poly(ε-caprolactone) (PCL) and poly(butylene adipate-co-terephthalate) (PBAT)) significantly accelerates their degradation in both coastal and deep-sea environments from water depths of 2–6 m to 5,503 m. Enzyme-embedded polyesters achieved near-complete degradation at a deep-sea depth of 757 m within 5 months. Furthermore, moderate biodegradation of enzyme-embedded polyesters was observed under extreme conditions on deep-sea floors of 5,503 m. In coastal seawater, films embedded with trace amounts (0.1 wt%) of enzyme underwent rapid degradation. The mechanical properties of enzyme-embedded polyesters were similar to those of enzyme-free polyesters. Chemical analysis confirmed the formation of low-molecular-mass degradation products and biochemical oxygen demand measurements supported the mineralization of the enzyme-embedded polyesters. The enzyme-embedding strategy enables enhanced marine degradation of compostable polyesters and represents a potential solution to marine plastic pollution.

Plastics are indispensable in many areas of modern life, but their accumulation in marine environments has emerged as a global environmental issue1,2,3. Biodegradable plastics have been considered a promising mitigation strategy4,5,6. Most commercially available biodegradable plastics are compostable4,5,7, meaning that their degradation is optimized for industrial composting conditions characterized by elevated temperatures, high microbial activity and humidity. However, some compostable polyesters have shown limited degradation and may therefore persist in these environments8,9,10. In marine environments, particularly in the deep sea, low temperatures and lack of viable microorganisms capable of degrading these plastics can substantially limit polyester degradation8,9,10,11,12,13, raising concerns about their environmental persistence.

Recent studies have demonstrated that embedding specialized enzymes directly into polymers can enhance their biodegradation14,15,16,17,18,19,20,21,22. However, these demonstrations have been confined to laboratory or closed-system incubations, and whether enzyme-embedded polyesters can achieve efficient degradation in situ under natural marine environments, particularly in the deep sea, remains unknown. At the same time, practical deployment requires enhanced biodegradability without compromising the mechanical properties needed for use.

In this study, thermally embedding Candida antarctica lipase B (CaLB) or Humicola insolens cutinase (HiC) in various compostable polyester films and microbeads, including PBS, PBSA, PCL and PBAT, has been shown to significantly accelerate their degradation in coastal and deep-sea environments, ranging from water depths of 2–6 m to 5,503 m (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1a–c). CaLB and HiC were selected for their broad hydrolytic activity towards aliphatic and aromatic–aliphatic polyesters23,24,25,26,27. Their high thermal stability in freeze-dried form enabled retention of catalytic activity during thermal embedding16,18,28.

Here we demonstrate that certain compostable polyesters that are widely regarded as persistent in the ocean can undergo substantial biodegradation with enzyme embedding in marine environments, including under extreme deep-sea conditions. Notably, efficient degradation was achieved using trace enzyme loadings while preserving initial mechanical properties resembling those of enzyme-free materials, supporting their practical applicability. This study demonstrates that the enzyme-embedding strategy enables marine degradation of such compostable polyesters while maintaining material performance, thereby broadening the range of candidate biodegradable materials with potential to address persistent plastic accumulation in marine environments.

To evaluate the feasibility of the enzyme-embedding strategy in various marine environments, we developed a series of enzyme-embedded compostable polyesters (PBS, PBSA, PCL and PBAT) (Fig. 1a,b). Building on the previously reported thermal stability of freeze-dried CaLB and HiC16,18, we embedded two different enzymes into polymer matrices by twin-screw extrusion at temperatures ranging from 90 °C to 130 °C (processing temperatures in Supplementary Table 1a). The final melt-processing approach yielded both thin films (100–300 μm) and microbeads (20–300 μm in diameter) (Extended Data Fig. 1), representative of common plastic waste forms and microplastic pollutants.

A comprehensive environmental degradation study was subsequently conducted in environments ranging from coastal seawater (2–6 m) to deep-sea floor (757–5,503 m) (Fig. 1c–e). Deployment sites were strategically selected to encompass various ecological conditions (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Table 1b), including one coastal site (port of JAMSTEC Yokosuka Headquarters (PJM)), three bathyal sites (off Misaki Port (BMS; depth = 757 m), off Hatsushima Island (BHT; depth = 855 m) and Myojin Knoll (BMJ; depth = 1,292 m)) and one abyssal site (Kuroshio Extension Observatory (AKR); depth = 5,503 m). Deep-sea deployment and recovery of the samples were performed aboard the human-occupied vehicle (HOV) Shinkai 6500 using a robotic arm (Fig. 1d), and follow-up evaluation of samples was carried out in the laboratory (Fig. 1e).

To account for the lower temperature, high pressure and distinct microbial activity in deep-sea environments, enzyme loading was increased from 0.1 wt% for coastal seawater deployments (PJM) to 3 wt% for deep-sea deployments (BMS, BMJ, BHT and AKR).

Enzyme-embedded polymer films were deployed across several distinct deep-sea floors (BMS, BMJ, BHT and AKR) at water depths ranging from 757 to 5,503 m (Fig. 1c). These sites exhibited contrasting temperatures, water pressures and microbial community compositions10. The decreases in mass of the films after 1 month of immersion in coastal seawater (PJM) and 18 months on deep-sea floors (BMS, BMJ and AKR) are presented in Fig. 2a. Notably, even after long-term immersion on the deep-sea floor, enzyme-free compostable PBS, PBSA, PCL and PBAT films exhibited little to no loss of mass (0.4–12.9%). Consistent with these results, no signs of degradation were observed in images of the films (Extended Data Fig. 2) and in scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Extended Data Fig. 3) except PCL film. Additionally, enzyme-free compostable PBS, PBSA, PCL and PBAT films placed at a coastal seawater site (PJM) exhibited a substantially faster degradation rate compared with those placed on deep-sea floors (BMS, BMJ and AKR) (Extended Data Fig. 4a and Supplementary Tables 2–5). Based on our previous studies10, the slower biodegradation of these compostable polyesters in deep-sea environments compared to coastal seawater can be attributed to several factors, including lower temperatures, higher hydrostatic pressure and reduced microbial abundance and diversity. By contrast, polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs) such as poly(3-hydroxybutyrate) (P3HB) demonstrate efficient degradation in diverse deep-sea environments (Fig. 2a). Their microbial origin and intrinsic biodegradability distinguish this family of materials from other synthetic polyesters29. Although PHAs demonstrate excellent biodegradability, their production volume is still low and their cost remains too high to meet the diverse demands of various applications30.

Embedding 3 wt% CaLB markedly enhanced the degradation of aliphatic compostable polyesters across diverse marine environments (Fig. 2a). This was exemplified at BMS (757 m depth), where CaLB-embedded PBSA and PCL films exhibited substantial mass losses of 57.3% and 99.0%, respectively, after 18 months (Fig. 2a), whereas enzyme-free control polyesters showed negligible degradation (Extended Data Fig. 4a). The substantial mass loss, accompanied by relatively large variability for films deployed at BMJ, is likely to be associated with the dynamic and heterogeneous sea floor conditions near hydrothermal vents, which may introduce greater environmental variability31,32. Finally, CaLB-embedded PBSA and PCL films also demonstrated significant mass losses of 30.5% and 7.7%, compared with 1.6% and 3.4% for enzyme-free PBSA and PCL films, respectively, after 18 months of immersion at AKR, located at a depth of 5,503 m (Fig. 2a). SEM observations (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 3) revealed the formation of surface holes on CaLB-embedded films after immersion, consistent with localized enzymatic degradation promoted by the embedded enzyme.

Laboratory pressure-vessel experiments conducted at 0.1 MPa, 10 MPa and 25 MPa (equivalent to atmospheric pressure, hydrostatic pressure at 1,000 m and hydrostatic pressure at 2,500 m) using a 100 lipase units (LU; 1 LU is the amount of enzyme that liberates 1 μmol of titratable butyric acid per minute from glycerol tributyrate under standard conditions) per ml CaLB solution prepared from autoclaved BMS deep-sea water further corroborate that enzymatic hydrolysis of PBSA and PCL occurs even under high pressures (Fig. 2b). This provides additional evidence that the mass loss observed in enzyme-embedded compostable polyester is directly attributable to enzymatic degradation. Although a decrease in the degree of degradation was observed for PBSA above 10 MPa and for PCL at 25 MPa, CaLB has been confirmed to remain effective under 25 MPa high pressure.

CaLB-embedded compostable polyester films placed at deeper deep-sea sites exhibited generally slower degradation rates (PJM > BMS > AKR), as shown in Extended Data Fig. 4a,b. This slowdown in degradation is consistent with the decreased enzymatic degradation rates observed in laboratory pressure-vessel experiments, which can be attributed to pressure-induced reductions in polymer chain mobility and free volume33, as well as partial changes in enzymatic activity34,35.

Moreover, comparisons of PCL films embedded with 3 wt% CaLB and placed at BMS for 5 or 18 months (Extended Data Fig. 4c and Supplementary Tables 3 and 9), indicated that the longer deployment increased mass losses from 39.7% to 99.0%. Similar increases in mass loss of HiC-embedded PBAT films placed at BHT for 7 and 12 months have been confirmed (Extended Data Fig. 4d and Supplementary Tables 6 and 10). These results revealed that enzyme-embedded samples underwent sustained enzymatic degradation, indicating that the embedded enzyme remained active even during long-term immersion at the deep-sea floor. Furthermore, increased concentrations of embedded CaLB resulted in faster enzymatic degradation (Extended Data Fig. 4e).

Previous studies have shown that HiC can hydrolyse both aliphatic compostable polyesters, such as PBS, PBSA and PCL, as well as aromatic–aliphatic polyesters, including PBAT, in buffer solutions18. To evaluate enzyme performance under environmentally relevant conditions, polyester samples embedded with 3 wt% CaLB or HiC were deployed simultaneously on the same BHT deep-sea (Fig. 2c and Extended Data Fig. 5) and PJM coastal (Extended Data Fig. 6) sites.

After 7 months of deployment (BHT7), PBS, PBSA and PCL films embedded with 3 wt% HiC exhibited significantly greater mass loss than the corresponding CaLB-embedded (BHT8) and enzyme-free polyesters, validating the higher hydrolytic activity of HiC compared with CaLB on these polyesters in deep-sea conditions (Fig. 2c,d and Supplementary Tables 6 and 7). A mass loss of 9.8 wt% was observed for PBAT embedded with 3 wt% HiC. Coastal seawater 1 month deployment (PJM1; Extended Data Fig. 6) revealed a similar trend, demonstrating that HiC-embedded polyester exhibited significantly greater degradation. This difference may be attributed to the structural characteristics of HiC, in which, unlike CaLB and most lipases, the catalytic site is located in a shallow, surface-exposed region rather than being buried within a deep, funnel-shaped active site. This makes the active site readily accessible to substrates24,36. The degradation-promoting effects of both HiC and CaLB illustrate the general feasibility of embedding enzymes within compostable polyester for accelerating biodegradation. However, the performance differences observed between HiC and CaLB emphasize the critical importance of identifying or engineering more efficient enzymes for optimizing biodegradation and improving the efficiency of enzyme-embedded biodegradable plastic systems, such as the recently reported enhanced polylactide (PLA) depolymerase, for embedment20.

A more detailed investigation was conducted over a 5-month period at a depth of 757 m on the BMS deep-sea floor (BMS 5-2; Fig. 3). Enzyme-free samples of PBS, PBSA, PCL and PBAT exhibited almost no mass loss, which was typically limited to only 3.4% (Fig. 3a). SEM images of PBS revealed no evidence of surface erosion, although some microbial colonization and biofilm formation were observed (Fig. 3c). These data suggest that biofilms did not contribute to the degradation of compostable polyesters under the tested deep-sea conditions.

As a comparison, embedding 3 wt% HiC markedly enhanced the degradation rates of aliphatic compostable polyesters. PBS, PBSA and PCL films exhibited losses approaching 100% within 5 months (BMS5-2; Fig. 3a), translating into degradation rates that were significantly higher than for enzyme-free polyesters. In particular, PBS embedded with 3 wt% HiC degraded at 97.0 ± 3.6 μg cm−2 day−1, compared with 0.9 ± 0.1 μg cm−2 day−1 for the enzyme-free PBS (Supplementary Table 8). Although substantial mass loss was observed, the remaining HiC-embedded PBS film preserved a continuous film-like morphology, indicating that the mass reduction was not solely attributable to physical fragmentation (Fig. 3d). No change in the molecular mass (Extended Data Fig. 9a) of the recovered HiC-embedded polyester films was observed, demonstrating that degradation proceeds predominantly in regions surrounding the embedded enzymes and that low-molecular-mass intermediates are released into seawater, allowing the remaining polymer to retain its original molecular mass. The thickness of HiC-embedded films also decreased as the enzymatic degradation proceeded (Extended Data Fig. 5f).

Embedded HiC similarly promoted enzymatic degradation of PBAT, although to a lesser degree. Mass loss reached 12.1% over 5 months (BMS5-2; Fig. 3a) for the embedded samples, higher than the 1.1% mass loss of the enzyme-free PBAT. This difference stems from the aromatic component (terephthalic acid units) of PBAT, which confers greater rigidity and overall resistance to enzymatic attack37,38,39. Although degradation for PBAT is slower than for aliphatic analogues, embedding enzyme HiC still significantly enhances its biodegradation in the extreme environment of the deep sea. Sustained enzymatic degradation during 7 to 12 months on the deep-sea floor of BHT (Extended Data Fig. 4d and Supplementary Tables 6 and 10) indicated that HiC remained active with high catalytic capability under low temperatures and high pressures.

Achieving enhanced environmental degradability without compromising physical performance is critical for the practical application of biodegradable plastics. Notably, the embedment of 0.1 wt% enzyme exerted almost no effect on the tensile strength of the polymer films (Fig. 3b), maintaining physical performance resembling that of the enzyme-free polyesters. Increasing the enzyme loading to 3 wt% HiC resulted in reduced tensile strength and elongation at break; however, the values remained within a practically usable range. Moreover, embedding HiC enabled synthesized compostable polyesters to degrade as rapidly as—or even faster than—microbially derived P3HB (BMS5-2; Fig. 3a), creating new possibilities for the design of high-performance marine-biodegradable plastics.

Microplastic particles often pose a higher ecological threat than larger plastic pieces, as their smaller size allows them to be ingested by aquatic organisms and transported through marine food webs40,41. Enzyme-embedded compostable polyesters accelerate degradation, but they may also lead to the release of microplastics during decomposition42. We thus utilized enzyme-embedded microbeads as a qualitative model of microplastics to examine whether enzymatic hydrolysis persists if polymer is fragmented into microplastic-sized particles. We tested HiC-embedded compostable polyester microbeads (20–300 μm in diameter), which were prepared using thermal extrusion and melt mechanical homogenization43 (Extended Data Fig. 1a–d). Results from microbeads align with the findings from films after 5 months placement at BMS (Fig. 3). Enzyme-free PBS microbeads retained their smooth surfaces with no notable signs of biodegradation (BMS5-2; Fig. 3e). Other enzyme-free PBSA and PBAT microbeads showed nearly no morphological changes under SEM (Extended Data Fig. 7a) after 5 months.

By contrast, PBS microbeads embedded with 3 wt% HiC developed many surface holes (Fig. 3e), consistent with localized enzymatic degradation. This set of experiments showed that embedded HiC remains catalytically active in microbeads in deep-sea environments. A previous study reported that the rate of enzymatic hydrolysis increases with decreasing particle size15. Additionally, HiC-embedded PBSA microbeads were more degraded than HiC-embedded PBS microbeads, resulting in complete degradation or fragmentation into particles smaller than the 55-μm mesh aperture. HiC-embedded PBAT microbeads also exhibited obvious pores (Extended Data Fig. 7a). The morphology of enzyme-embedded microbeads indicated that the enzyme-embedding method is similarly effective across beads of different sizes.

The morphology of HiC-embedded polyester microbeads after deep-sea placement, characterized by localized surface holes, was similar to that of the same HiC-embedded microbeads immersed in buffer under laboratory conditions, but the morphology differed from that of enzyme-free microbeads treated with external HiC buffer solutions (Extended Data Fig. 8). This indicates that the degradation under deep-sea conditions was induced by the HiC embedded in the microbeads.

To determine whether embedded enzymes generated soluble degradation intermediates independently of microbial activity, we analysed HiC-embedded PBAT films incubated in buffer solution using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Extended Data Fig. 9b–e). Water-soluble low-molecular-mass hydrolysis products were detected in the reaction solution, and the measured film mass loss closely matched the theoretical value calculated from product formation, indicating near-complete conversion of the polymer into soluble degradation products. These results provide direct chemical evidence of enzymatic depolymerization under abiotic conditions.

Next, we used 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene amplicon sequencing to analyse the deep-sea plastisphere communities on polyester films recovered after 11 months on the BMS deep-sea floor (BMS11; Extended Data Fig. 10) to explore the proposed biodegradation processes occurring under deep-sea conditions.

As shown in Extended Data Fig. 10, Rhodobacterales (family Rhodobacteraceae) colonize the surface and constitute a large fraction of the surface-associated microbiota. Rhodobacteraceae are known to secrete extracellular polymeric substances that form a conditioning layer and facilitate the attachment and settlement of a diverse microbial community under the prevailing aerobic conditions44. In biofilms formed after 11 months of submersion on the deep-sea floor at BMS, the dominant microorganisms were primarily aerobic, in contrast to the previously observed dominance of anaerobic Deltaproteobacteria in biofilms on the deep-sea floor at BHT after 14 months10.

For PBS and PBSA films, Burkholderiales were initially present at low abundance on control enzyme-free and 3 wt% bovine serum albumin (BSA)-embedded PBS and PBSA films, with relative abundances of 3.0% and 3.2% (PBS), and 2.4% and 1.2% (PBSA), respectively. However, the relative abundance of Burkholderiales gradually increased with increasing HiC loading, reaching 5.2% (PBS with 0.1% HiC), 15.0% (PBS with 3% HiC) and 7.3% (PBSA with 0.1% HiC) after 11 months of BMS deep-sea floor placement, whereas similar trends were not observed for PCL or PBAT films. Members of Burkholderiales (class Betaproteobacteria) have been detected on PBSA in seawater and were reported to potentially contribute to succinate metabolism45. The observed enrichment of Burkholderiales on the surface of HiC-embedded PBS and PBSA film is therefore consistent with a potential role in the metabolism of polyester-derived hydrolysis products. Previous metagenomic studies from the deep-sea floor at BHT10 and PJM coastal seawater environments45 have identified genes encoding polyester-degrading enzymes including cutinases and polyesterases in biofilms on plastic surfaces, supporting the possibility that similar functional potential may also be present in surface-associated microorganisms in this study. However, our data do not resolve intracellular metabolic pathways or provide evidence of direct microbial involvement in polymer chain scission.

Microorganisms within the biofilm or in the surrounding seawater may further metabolize low-molecular-mass enzymatic degradation products, potentially leading to CO2 formation. This proposed process is supported by biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) experiments using coastal seawater collected from PJM (Fig. 4b and Extended Data Fig. 9f).

Figure 3f summarizes a proposed integrated biodegradation process based on the above observations. For enzyme-free polyester films, microbial colonization occurred after long-term deployment, but there was no clear evidence of polymer degradation. By contrast, enzyme-embedded films underwent enzymatic depolymerization, resulting in the generation and release of water-soluble, low-molecular-mass degradation products. The composition of the microbial community observed on the enzyme-embedded films, together with the BOD results, is consistent with the possibility that these degradation products could be further metabolized by marine microorganisms, potentially contributing to CO2 production.

Although deep-sea degradation is crucial for compostable polyesters that sink, the majority of plastics initially enter coastal waters, rivers and harbours, where temperatures are higher and microbial communities are more diverse10,40. To evaluate the effect of embedded enzymes on plastic decomposition under these conditions, we immersed films and microbeads in shallow port seawater at PJM for 1 month (PJM1; Fig. 4).

Enzyme-free PBS, PBSA and PBAT films exhibited minimal losses of less than 3.0% after 1 month at PJM (Fig. 4a,c), whereas PCL film showed a 23.4% decrease. A study conducted at the same coastal site (PJM) demonstrated that the same enzyme-free polyesters resulted in losses of 2.7% to 34.0% when the deployment period was extended to 2 months10. The enzyme-free polyester samples exhibited slight decreases in molecular mass in coastal seawater (Extended Data Fig. 9g), which may be attributable to enzymatic degradation associated with the higher abundance of microorganisms present in coastal seawater10. The positive-control P3HB film exhibited a degradation rate of 61.1 ± 6.6 μg cm−2 day−1 over 1 month (PJM1; Supplementary Table 2), 2.5 times higher than the rate observed under deep-sea conditions (BMS 5-2; 23.7 ± 6.7 μg cm−2 day−1; Supplementary Table 8). These data support the idea that warmer waters and more abundant microorganisms may accelerate polymer biodegradation.

Embedding 0.1 wt% HiC accelerated degradation of compostable polyester films relative to enzyme-free controls at PJM. PBS, PBSA and PCL films embedded with 0.1 wt% HiC lost 27.4%, 100% and 85.5% of their mass, respectively, in 1 month (Fig. 4a,d). Notably, PBAT embedded with 0.5 wt% HiC also degraded by 11.3% in 1 month, with a degradation rate exceeding that of the enzyme-free PBAT films (Supplementary Table 2). These results demonstrate that a minimal enzyme loading of 0.1% can significantly accelerate compostable polyester degradation in coastal seawaters, potentially reducing the cost implications associated with higher enzyme loadings. Unlike in the deep sea, the molecular-mass decreases observed for the enzyme-embedded films in coastal seawater (Extended Data Fig. 9g) may be attributable not only to degradation by the embedded enzymes, but also to enzymatic degradation associated with microorganisms present in coastal seawater.

We also monitored subsequent mineralization of the HiC-embedded compostable polyesters by microorganisms in coastal seawater. BOD measurements (Fig. 4b and Extended Data Fig. 9f) using seawater from coastal seawater at PJM indicated that PBS film embedded with 0.1 wt% HiC elicited 50.9% theoretical oxygen consumption within 1 month. In a previous study, PBS with degree of polymerization over 7.5 showed no marine biodegradability in coastal seawater BOD experiments46. These results indicate that the enzyme embedded within the polymer catalysed the breakdown of polymer chains into low-molecular-mass, water-soluble, bio-assimilable monomers or oligomers, which were subsequently metabolized aerobically into CO2 by microorganisms in the seawater. BOD/theoretical oxygen demand (THOD) values of the HiC-embedded polyester films were similar or greater than those of cellulose standards (Extended Data Fig. 9f), highlighting the high biodegradability of HiC-embedded polyester films47. By contrast, enzyme-free PBS film exhibited no appreciable oxygen consumption, underscoring the importance of the embedded enzymes in rapidly hydrolysing high-molecular-mass polymers into low-molecular-mass hydrolysates for subsequent microbial utilization.

Parallel experiments for compostable polyester microbeads in coastal seawater confirmed that microbeads embedded with 0.1 wt% HiC rapidly formed visible pores and cracks within 2 weeks (Fig. 4e and Extended Data Fig. 7b,c). The presence of biofilm was also confirmed on the microbead surface after immersion in seawater (Extended Data Fig. 7b). Meanwhile, enzyme-free polyester microbeads had a smooth surface and remained undegraded over the same period. The accelerated degradation of these compostable polyester microbeads suggests a potential solution to reduce the environmental residence time of microplastics.

The enzyme-embedding technique may be extended to other polymer–enzyme combinations, as it is compatible with a range of compostable plastics (for example, PBAT, PBSA and PCL) and enzymes (HiC and CaLB). Furthermore, enzyme loading can be adjusted for different environmental conditions, as plastic debris often floats at or near the water surface before eventually sinking to deeper layers. A small amount of enzyme (for instance, 0.1 wt%) is sufficient for rapid degradation in coastal seawaters, whereas higher concentrations (3 wt%) may be required for deep-sea environments. Incorporating a small amount of enzyme within a polyester to enable its total degradation in shallow seawater before it reaches deep-sea environments may be a feasible route to mitigating plastic pollution.

Beyond films and microbeads, ongoing work is exploring the extension of this strategy to other material formats, such as fishing lines, fishing nets and other environmental materials. Moreover, embedding enzymes in compostable plastics may also contribute to acceleration of compost rate, leading to more efficient bioenergy production.

Although substantial degradation enhancement was achieved at a very low enzyme loading (0.1 wt%), the cost of enzyme incorporation and the already high cost of compostable polyesters relative to conventional plastics remain important challenges for large-scale applications. However, HiC and CaLB are already produced at commercial scale by multiple suppliers, and several approaches could further reduce the required enzyme dosage and ultimately contribute to reduce costs, including enzyme enhancement20, nano-dispersed enzyme strategy19, poly(ethylene glycol) modification (PEGylation) or polyethylene glycol coating of enzymes to increase dispersity17,48, nitrogen atmosphere processing28 and enzyme immobilization15,49. Furthermore, improved marine biodegradability alone does not guarantee overall environmental sustainability, which also depends on degradation rate, environmental persistence, greenhouse gas emissions, cost, material performance and end-of-life management50.

Accumulation of plastics in marine environments poses ecological risks owing to their persistence, particularly in the deep sea. Here we have shown that embedding CaLB or HiC into biodegradable polyester films and microbeads significantly enhances degradation in several distinct coastal and deep-sea floor environments with water depths ranging from 2–6 m to 5,503 m. HiC-embedded polyesters, including PBS, PBSA and PCL, achieved nearly complete degradation at a depth of 757 m within 5 months, far exceeding the degradation of enzyme-free polyesters. Aromatic PBAT also exhibited accelerated enzymatic degradation. Of note, low enzyme loadings (0.1 wt%) had almost no effect on the mechanical properties of the polymers, preserving their functional performance. Enzymatic hydrolysis generated soluble low-molecular-mass products, and BOD measurements supported subsequent microbial mineralization in seawater.

These results demonstrate enzyme embedding as a material-level strategy to enhance the environmental degradability of the compostable polyesters examined in this study across diverse marine environments without compromising their mechanical properties. Enzyme-embedded compostable polyesters may expand the range of biodegradable materials capable of mitigating long-term accumulation of persistent plastics in marine environments.

PBS, PBSA, PCL, PBAT, P3HB and polyethylene terephthalate (PET) pellets were purchased from or provided by various commercial sources shown in Supplementary Table 1a. HiC (Novozyme 51032, 15,000 LU g−1) and CaLB (Lipozyme CALB L, 5,000 LU g−1) were purchased from Wako Fujifilm. Enzyme powders were prepared as described16,18.

Enzyme-free PBS, PBSA, PCL, PBAT and P3HB films were produced by first drying their pellets at 40–50 °C under reduced pressure for ≥12 h to remove residual moisture. Approximately 2–3 g of each dried polymer was then fed into a counter-rotating twin-screw micro-extruder (Thermo Scientific HAAKE MiniLab) operated at 120 rpm and held at 90 °C (PCL), 100 °C (PBSA), 130 °C (PBS and PBAT) and 180 °C (P3HB); the total residence time in the extruder was limited to 3 min to prevent thermal degradation and further enzyme denaturing. The resulting molten polymer was extruded through a 1 × 2-mm rectangular die and cooled to room temperature. To form films, about 300 mg of the extrudate was compression-moulded for 30 s at 2 MPa and at the respective processing temperature used for extrusion, followed by quenching in ice water for 10 s; the total thermal processing (extrusion and pressing combined) did not exceed 3.5 min. All films (approximately 100–300 µm thick) were stored in a desiccator at room temperature for at least 48 h and up to 1 month. Enzyme-embedded films were prepared by incorporating HiC or CaLB into the above polymer pellets at a loading of 0.1 wt% (and up to 3 wt% for deep-sea deploying samples). The dried polymer pellets were mixed thoroughly with the enzyme powder and melt extrusion following the same procedure and temperature settings as for enzyme-free polymers. After 3 min of continuous mixing in the extruder, the extrudate was cooled, and approximately 300 mg of the enzyme-embedded polymer blend was compression-moulded for 30 s at 2 MPa before undergoing the same ice-water quenching and storage protocol.

For preparation of enzyme-embedded PBS, PBSA and PBAT microbeads, 4 ml of silicone oil was placed in a stainless-steel tube and heated to the designated melt-processing temperature using an aluminium-block thermostatic bath (DTU-1C, TAITEC) (Extended Data Fig. 1). Pre-cut pieces of the enzyme-embedded polymer extrudate (0.2–0.3 g) were introduced into the oil and allowed to melt for 3 min. A Microtec homogenizer equipped with a NS-7 generator shaft (7.0-mm diameter × 100 mm) then dispersed the molten polymer at 12,000–24,000 rpm for 60 s, after which the tube was rapidly cooled in an ice-water bath (4 °C). The resulting polymer microbeads suspended in silicone oil were separated by repeated washing with hexane and centrifugation, repeated three times. The beads were recovered by vacuum filtration through a 0.45 -µm PTFE membrane, resuspended in hexane, filtered again through a 250-µm polyethylene mesh, washed with hexane twice, then air-dried for one day and stored at 4 °C. PCL microbeads could not be prepared using our homogenization-in-silicone-oil method.

The thickness of the samples, both before and after undergoing environmental degradation tests, was measured at five different points using a digital micrometer (Mitsutoyo) and averaged. For melt-pressed films, thickness measurements were taken at 5 locations, 4 corners and the centre of a 4 × 4-cm film, and the average value along with the standard deviation were calculated. Dimensions were measured to two decimal places in centimetres using a digital calliper (Shinwa), and mass was recorded using an electronic balance.

Film samples and microbeads were sealed in polyethylene mesh with apertures of 1 mm and 55 μm, respectively, and placed into PET chambers with through holes. The mesh aperture (55 μm) was chosen to balance particle retention with minimization of diffusion limitation and enclosure artefacts during long-term marine exposure. Owing to the small size of the microbeads relative to the mesh aperture, partial particle loss could occur, precluding reliable gravimetric mass-loss determination. Enzyme-free polyester samples and enzyme-embedded polyester samples were stored in separate containers to avoid any cross-contamination. Similarly, when films with CaLB and HiC enzymes embedded were settled simultaneously, they were also placed in separate containers. Each type of sample had at least a total of five replicates (with a minimum of four, one used for additional analyses).

Deep-sea water obtained from BMS was first autoclaved at 120 °C for 20 min. Square films of PBSA and PCL (2 cm × 2 cm each) were then placed in individual zip-top polyethylene bags, followed by the addition of 10 ml of a CaLB solution (100 LU ml−1) prepared with the autoclaved deep-sea water. After expelling the air, the bags were tightly sealed and transferred into a pressure vessel filled with deionized water. Two pressure vessels were used, one pressurized to 10 MPa and the other to a maximum of 25 MPa, and both were maintained at 4 °C for 1 month. Subsequently, the films were removed, air-dried, and weighed to assess changes in mass. A non-pressurized control experiment was performed in parallel at 4 °C. Each data set consisted of two replicate samples.

Tensile properties of the films were evaluated at room temperature using a universal testing machine (EZ-LX, Shimadzu), with a strain rate of 200 mm min−1 and an initial gauge length of 10 mm. The elongation at break, Young’s modulus and maximum tensile strength were determined from the average of five dumbbell-shaped specimens prepared following Japanese Industrial Standard (JIS) K 6251-5 1/3. The test specimens were prepared two days before measurement and stored in a desiccator at ambient conditions.

Microbeads were subjected to compression testing using a flat punch indenter tip on a micro compression tester (MCT-211; Shimadzu). Sizes of microbeads were determined by an optical microscope attached to the compression tester. A compressive force ranging from 0 to 200 mN was applied to each microbead at an increasing rate of 4.8 mN s−1. According to JIS Z8844:2019 for measuring fracture strength and deformation strength of microbeads, as plastic microbeads are soft and do not exhibit a definite crush point, compression strengths were assessed based on stress at 10% strain. Nine different and individual microbeads with sizes below 100 μm were selected and investigated.

PBAT films embedded with 0.5 wt% HiC were placed in a glass container, and 2 ml of phosphate buffer (pH 7.5, 100 mM) was added. The samples were incubated at 45 °C for 96 h. After incubation, the reaction solution was collected and heated at 100 °C for 15 min to denature the enzyme. The enzymatic degradation intermediates in the solution were analysed using a HPLC system equipped with a high-pressure gradient.

The HPLC system consisted of a CBM-20A communication bus module, a DGC-20A3R degasser for the water phase pump unit, an LC-20AD liquid chromatograph, a SIL-20A autosampler, an SPD-20A UV/Vis detector (UV detection was carried out at 254 nm), and a CTO-20A column oven, all from Shimadzu. For separation, a reversed-phase chromatography column (Asahipak ODP-50 4E) and a guard column (Asahipak ODP-50G 4A) from Shodex were used. The mobile phase consisted of water (1% formic acid solution) and acetonitrile, with an elution gradient starting at a 15:85 ratio and transitioning to 85:15 in 40 min. The flow rate was set at 1 ml min−1, and the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C. The hydrolysis products were identified and quantified using synthesized mono(4-hydroxybutyl) terephthalate (MHBT) and bis(4-hydroxybutyl) terephthalate (BHBT) as reference standards, together with terephthalic acid. All compounds were analysed using the same HPLC system. The quantification of adipic acid was performed using an ion-exclusion chromatography column (SH1011 from Shodex). A 0.01 N sulfuric acid solution was used as the mobile phase, with a flow rate of 0.6 ml min−1 at 60 °C.

#1309. Diesel prices are surging putting further pressure on Europe

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Trucks, farms and industry are powered with diesel. Now prices are soaring due to refinery disruptions, supply shortages and geopolitical tensions. A potential US export ban could make Europe's diesel crunch even worse.

Even if you don't fill your own vehicle with diesel, its price has a big impact on your life.

While gasoline is mainly a consumer fuel for cars, diesel powers much of the wider economy, from freight transport and farming equipment to construction and industry.

That is why diesel shortages or price shocks can have a bigger economic impact than higher gasoline prices by raising the cost of moving goods and operating heavy machinery.

As businesses become strained, these higher energy costs will be passed on to consumers and add to a growing cost-of-living crisis and inflation. So what's driving global diesel prices higher?

The United States and China are among the world's largest diesel producers. China keeps much of it for domestic use, leaving the US as the world's biggest diesel exporter.

Still, the weekly average price of a gallon of diesel is now $6.52 (€5.73) in the country, according to the US Energy Information Administration, a big increase from a year ago when it averaged $3.74 per gallon or two years ago when it averaged $3.53 a gallon.

President Donald Trump recently called on Ukraine's president to stop targeting oil refineries in Russia "because he's causing a shortage of diesel."

Russia is one of the world's other biggest diesel suppliers. But since 2022, some of its oil refineries have been attacked as many as 15 times, according to the International Energy Agency.

The agency estimates that diesel production in Russia is down by nearly 30% from 2025 levels. Moscow has now restricted diesel exports to protect domestic supplies.

As Russian production declined, the US went into high gear to help make up the difference, but even this was not enough. And on September 22, Trump announced that his administration was also considering a diesel export ban.

Such a move is likely an attempt to bring down domestic prices ahead of the November midterm elections since energy prices and affordability have become important campaign issues.

Leading energy lobby groups have come out against such a plan, and many industry experts think a ban would be slow to bring down prices or could backfire by pushing US refiners to reduce production, push up prices, or both.

For Europe, more expensive diesel could have dramatic consequences.

"Decades of policy incentives, such as tax advantages, left Europe's vehicle fleet far more diesel-heavy than regions like the US," said Georg Zachmann, a senior fellow at Brussels-based Bruegel think tank.

"As a result, the EU is structurally long on gasoline, which it exports, and short on diesel, which it must import," added Zachmann, who specializes in energy and climate policies.

In the EU, just over 38% of passenger cars are diesel and road transport, including freight, makes up 77% of total diesel and gas oil consumption, according to a briefing published by Transport & Environment, a Brussels-based advocacy group.

This reliance on diesel-powered transport "leaves drivers and businesses particularly exposed to oil and fuel price shocks," wrote Juliette Egal, the briefing's lead author.

The NGO calculated that EU drivers are paying €30 more for a 50-liter tank of diesel on average since the start of the war in Iran. An average German long-haul truck driver is paying an extra €236 a week.

Increased fuel costs for EU road transport add up to €270 million a day — of that, €203 million is for diesel, according to the briefing. Overall, the EU economy has seen additional costs of €40 billion from road diesel since the beginning of the war.

In the near term, Russia's crude oil processing volumes are the single fastest-moving lever, said Skip York, a nonresident fellow at Rice University's Center for Energy Studies in Houston, Texas.

"Each new wave of Ukrainian strikes shows up in diesel prices within days, unlike Hormuz, which has been running at a depressed but relatively stable flow rate for months," he added.

York points out that, perhaps surprisingly, crude oil production, the raw material needed to make diesel and gasoline, isn't the biggest constraint. That is why oil prices have moved modestly while diesel has soared.

"The oil bottleneck has shifted from 'not enough crude' in March/April to 'not enough refining capacity' since August," the expert told DW.

Now refinery disruptions matter most, he said, because "both the Ukraine drone campaign and the Hormuz conflict are knocking out working refining capacity faster than anyone can replace it."

Still, the Strait of Hormuz "is the bigger structural risk" for diesel prices in the long term.

Globally, refinery capacity has "dropped substantially, and diesel is particularly affected," agreed Zachmann.

Prolonged fighting in the Middle East or Russia could add to the problems for Europe and other diesel users. US export restrictions could make this situation even worse.

US diesel exports to Europe are up around 37% since last year and now account for about 8% of European demand, says York. This makes the country an important alternative to diesel from Russia and the Persian Gulf.

If the US blocks exports, it would be a big shock and mean even higher diesel prices, said Zachmann. The question is whether the EU would retaliate and export less gasoline to the US "making both sides worse off" by pushing up both US gasoline prices and EU diesel prices.

"Essentially, the market has fewer 'extra barrels' standing by," he underscored. "So, the loss of any barrel hits supply much more directly than it used to."

If anything, where St. Louis was back then is what American soccer would like to get back to these days, as more opportunities for young players are being created by the day.

Looking at the immigration history of Irish, Scottish, German, and Italian families into the area, it makes sense as to why soccer in St. Louis would have a more European flair than some of the other areas of the United States, but it's also something that helped lead to this early success. Even in their league play, players had to make the team and would regularly be recruited by stronger sides, helping spawn not only their Open Cup success but also sending players to the national team. When the United States went to the World Cup in 1950, St. Louis was well represented on that team before the St. Louis Stars came to be in the North American Soccer League, which was a precursor to Major League Soccer.

Those NASL years put St. Louis' Open Cup success on hold before the 80s got the city back to the summit of American soccer. St. Louis Kutis SC won the competition in 1986 before the Busch Seniors etched their own names in history two years later, defeating the San Francisco Greek Americans to win the Open Cup, but these were not both teams of semipros.

"These are guys that we were reading about in the papers and watching on TV, and all of a sudden we're playing against them. But, knowing the team that we had, knowing the talent that we had, the diversity of players on our team, we had a lot of confidence going in. We knew it was gonna be a battle, and we knew that nothing was going to be given to us," Tim Loughman, a defender on the Seniors, said.

While that's hardly the level of Auckland City facing Bayern Munich in the Club World Cup, despite the notoriety of the competition, what came next was that the Busch Seniors qualified for the Concacaf Champions Cup, where their first challenge was facing Chivas Guadalajara. St. Louis was actually able to defeat them in the home leg at World Wide Technology Soccer Park (formerly known as Anheuser-Busch Soccer Park, a soccer-specific complex where the team played) before heading to Mexico to play at Jalisco Stadium.

Some players weren't able to take time off work to head to Mexico for the return leg, and while it was a heavy defeat, it was a moment that showed where soccer could go for not only St. Louis but the United States as a whole.

"The atmosphere was incredible, and we ended up losing eight-nothing that game, the second leg, so we didn't go through. But the experience was incredible; the fact that we beat them in the first round shows what kind of special team we had," Loughman said. "But an incredible season all the way around, and it's a different dynamic today for the better, with the professional teams represented, and Joe and I take great pride, and so does our team, in being a part of that legacy, being able to bring that forward. And now our job is to support the St. Louis City team."

The importance of Anheuser-Busch and local companies has been critical for both the success of St. Louis soccer as well as U.S. Soccer, and without those early backers of the sport in the country, who knows if we'd be at a time when a 36-year-old coach in Yoann Damet would be able to engineer a 21-game unbeaten streak, the longest in MLS history, en route to this final.

Dave Lange, author of St. Louis Soccer: An Illustrated Timeline, has seen plenty of soccer in the area, and this current team has blown him away.

"The whole build-up, the passes, the finishing, I was just amazed; it was just the best brand of soccer I've seen in a long time in person in St. Louis, that's for sure," Lange said.

With not every MLS team taking part in the Open Cup, it has lost some luster in recent seasons, but it's still a critical part of U.S. Soccer, and next season will see all of the teams return to the competition. And it's also fitting that this is where St. Louis City SC could win their first trophy in club history, bringing everything full circle. While the '88 team may be happy to have won the Cup, they also want to see the day that they're not the last team from St. Louis to lift the historic trophy.

"The Cup for me is like part of history, you know, like the cup in every place in the world, everybody plays it. I played in the cup in France growing up. I think it's also an opportunity to bring together all the different levels of soccer in the country. That Cup in particular, for me, is unique; it's got a lot of history," Damet said. "But as every other cup, you know, like a cup is a trophy, it's another opportunity to compete to win something. So for me, as I said in January, I'm no different today, like it was always gonna be a competition that we were gonna care about and that we were gonna invest our energy and effort into. Right now, the history of the club is like nothing, so we need to keep striving to fill that trophy cabinet with something, and having that opportunity to be the first is an amazing opportunity that we've earned throughout our journey in that competition this year."

It's significant that so much of the preparation for this Cup final has involved that 1988 team, but as Lange states, this is part of the fabric of the city.

"There's always been a great awareness of that competition because it's part of that soccer fabric, and if you're from St. Louis, chances are you have some kind of connection with one of those teams," Lange said. "For example, my wife, whose grandparents came over from Sicily, her second cousin played on the 1950 Open Cup team, Simpkins-Ford, that won the Open Cup. So there are a lot of connections like that in St. Louis, somebody you know, somebody, or you're related to somebody who played at some high level of soccer."

Tim Ream's resurgence for the United States men's national team and Bosnia taking over the city during their World Cup stay have helped bring notoriety back to St. Louis as a soccer city, but their MLS team could take it to a new level, as even how they got to this point is impressive in itself, and they'll have to overcome star striker Rafael Navarro being unavailable for the final due to being cup-tied.

Marcel Hartel and Edward Lowen were loved players at the club, and their departures hit hard but the front office was able to ensure that Damet was backed by bringing in Navarro, Carlo Holse, and Alexandru Matan, and he has turned them into a serious force in MLS play.

"It was kind of a painful beginning because there was some emotional attachment to certain players who left, like Klauss. I think a lot of people were emotionally invested in him, and especially in Eduard Löwen, the midfielder, who had some personal issues with his wife passing away and him being so seriously ill the last couple of years, limiting his playing time," Lange said. "A lot of people had a lot of emotional investment in certain players who left, and it was hard to accept. I think everybody still misses those guys but they've seen that we're a much better team with what's happened in the last couple of months here with the players they brought in."

They've already accomplished so much by getting to this point, and it's not easy to get fans to connect with a team that has made so many changes, but that's where Damet and the front office are pushing the right buttons.

"I think the resurgence with this year's team in particular, being in the final, is a throwback to the early days of work ethic, blue-collar, team-oriented, with some additional talent that we picked up at the break here," Olwig said, explaining why the connection with this team has been so strong. "So, to me, it's refreshing to see the way the team's playing, because it reminds us, I think, Tim, you'd agree, of the old days of following St. Louis soccer."

A soccer team representing their area is critical to the game, and for St. Louis City SC, they're doing just that, so even if this final doesn't go their way, this isn't a team or a city who are going anywhere.
