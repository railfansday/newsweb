#4047. Amgen says drug combo cuts risk of death by 58% in patients with aggressive lung cancer

📅 24 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

A drug from Amgen could give patients with a notoriously aggressive form of lung cancer significantly more time to live when added to an immunotherapy from AstraZeneca, a study found.

Full results from a late-stage trial released Saturday show that adding Amgen's immunotherapy Imdelltra to AstraZeneca's Imfinzi drug slashed the risk of death by 58% in patients with extensive-stage small cell lung cancer, a fast-growing disease that has spread beyond the lung where it began.

The drug combination extended their median overall survival to 21.1 months compared with 12.7 months with AstraZeneca's drug alone, the trial found.

That means half of patients in the group that took the combination regimen were alive for at least roughly 21 months. The trial tested the combo as a maintenance therapy in patients who received initial treatment with Imfinzi and chemotherapy, and whose cancer had not progressed.

"I think there are moments in medicine when you see a piece of data that is so profound that it's going to transform the standard of care for patients, and I think, honestly, we're at that moment," said Dr. Paul Burton, Amgen's chief medical officer, in an interview.

The combination "has the potential to take on one of the most aggressive cancers that we deal with today, and almost convert it into a chronic disease," he said, adding that "these data say that's a possibility."

The results also suggest a greater commercial opportunity for Imdelltra, which posted $288 million in sales during the second quarter, up 115% year over year. The drug already generated $627 million in global sales in 2025 and appears to be on its way to becoming a blockbuster medicine, or hitting the $1 billion sales threshold.

Small cell lung cancer remains difficult to treat because, even if chemotherapy initially works well for most patients, the disease often returns or progresses within months, said lead trial investigator Dr. Jacob Sands, who is the associate chief of the Lowe Center for Thoracic Oncology at Dana-Farber Cancer Institute.

He said immunotherapy has helped a small subset of patients achieve years of disease control, but most have not experienced similarly durable benefits, highlighting the need for better treatments. There are roughly 31,000 new small cell lung cancer cases in the U.S. each year, and an estimated 60% to 70% of those people are diagnosed with extensive-stage disease.

Sands said the benefits in the trial were seen across subgroups of patients, including those whose cancer spread to the brain or liver. Disease that has spread to those organs is associated with poorer treatment outcomes.

He added that with longer-term follow-up data on the patients, the 21-month figure "may evolve further and hopefully will substantially improve," similar to what Amgen saw in a Phase 1 trial on the immunotherapy combination. In that early-stage trial on a smaller number of patients, a long-term follow-up showed the benefit was sustained for years.

Amgen said the findings also suggest that using Imdelltra earlier in a patient's treatment could meaningfully alter the trajectory of the disease. Imdelltra is currently approved for patients whose small cell lung cancer has progressed while they are on, or after, platinum-based chemotherapy.

Researchers specifically tested whether adding Imdelltra could help patients live longer than continuing Imfinzi alone, which is already approved as a first treatment for this type of lung cancer. Amgen said nearly 80% of patients who started taking both drugs were alive at one year compared with 53% receiving Imfinzi alone.

Imdelltra's "big impact" on controlling the disease and ultimately how long people live highlights the importance of including it in first-line maintenance treatment, Sands said. That refers to ongoing therapy given after an initial heavy treatment, such as chemotherapy or surgery.

Burton added that it positions Amgen's drug to be an "indispensable backbone therapy" for small cell lung cancer, regardless of the other immunotherapy that can be paired with it.

Imdelltra binds to DLL3, a protein on cancer cells, and CD3, a protein on T cells, which are a type of immune cell that can kill cancer. By binding to both, Imdelltra brings T cells into close contact with tumor cells, helping the immune system destroy the cancer.

Imfinzi, made by AstraZeneca, is an immunotherapy that blocks a protein called PD-L1, which cancer cells use to evade the immune system. The drugs work together in complementary ways: Imfinzi helps remove the brakes on the immune response, while Imdelltra brings T cells directly to cancer cells and helps activate them to attack the tumor.

The study, which was presented at a major cancer meeting called the European Society for Medical Oncology Congress, did not test whether the combination was better than other first-line treatment approaches.

Still, the findings suggest Amgen's drug could be another option offered to small cell lung cancer patients earlier in their treatment. Kate Brenan, a 44-year-old mother of two from Denver, sees that as a win on its own.

She was diagnosed with extensive-stage small cell lung cancer in early January 2024. Brenan underwent six rounds of a common combination chemotherapy, followed by chest radiation and later whole-brain radiation after the cancer spread.

In September 2024, she started Imdelltra and has had no evidence of disease ever since.

"I think that it's remarkable how quickly things are changing in the world of small cancer treatment options," Brenan told CNBC, adding that Imdelltra potentially becoming an earlier treatment option "can only be a good thing for the patients moving forward, and I'm really excited about that."

#3716. The iPhone Duo Just Passed 20,000 in Our Fold-a-Phone Testing Machine. Can It Beat the Galaxy Z Fold 8?

📅 24 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

We're folding Apple's iPhone Duo 900 times an hour to test how its hinge will last.

Apple’s first-ever foldable phone — the iPhone Duo — launched Friday and was met with long lines and product sellouts. We braved three hours at the Apple Store on Friday to snag our own testing model for our lab in Louisville, Kentucky, where I oversee hardware and software testing as director of lab operations.

Now the big question? How many times can we fold the iPhone Duo? As of 1:30 p.m. ET on Saturday, we’ve just passed 20,000 folds.

The goal is simple. On Friday afternoon, my team and I loaded the iPhone Duo into a proprietary machine we built to test foldable phones like Apple’s and Samsung’s rival Galaxy Z Fold 8, which we’ve also already started testing. I’ve overseen the methodology and build of this testing rig for months in anticipation of Apple’s long-rumored foldable iPhone. (Read on for how CNET tests foldable phones.)

We even named our machine to match its purpose: Meet the Fold-a-Phone.

Now, my team of engineers and testing experts will watch the iPhone Duo open and close, open and close, to rigorously and systematically monitor the durability of Apple’s hinge and two screens.

After all, repeatedly folding a phone with bare hands can only get you so far in understanding the complex forces at play between the magnetics that snap the screen shut and the all-critical hinge.

CNET’s Senior Technology Reporter, Abrar Al-Heeti, has done a beautiful job capturing the iPhone Duo’s pros and cons in her smart, thorough review. Our Fold-a-Phone machine will add a deep data foundation to complement her real-world experience.

Since Apple hasn’t publicly shared claims of the iPhone Duo’s foldable lifespan, CNET’s Fold-a-Phone machine is poised to provide a wealth of valuable data about Apple’s first foldable phone and competing models in the foldable market.

And yes, you’d better believe we will use our treasure trove of in-house data to share status updates and comparisons during our ongoing testing window with the Duo.

Join CNET’s iPhone Duo livestream on YouTube on Monday from 1-5 p.m. ET, where you can follow along live and ask questions about our iPhone Duo testing process.

The importance of this moment is hard to overstate. Apple’s appearance on the foldable smartfold frontier lags Samsung by seven years (the first Galaxy Fold went on sale in the US in September 2019). Motorola and other phone-makers have long experimented with foldable phones in every shape and size, including a trifold accordion foldable phone we first demoed in 2019.

CNET’s mobile experts, including Al-Heeti and Editor at Large Andrew Lanxon, have discussed the legitimizing effect of the iPhone Duo’s existence on the entire foldable phone landscape, which has previously felt like a new-wave fantasy rather than a mainstream riptide.

In many ways, Apple’s newfound competition with existing foldable phones completes the portfolio. That is precisely why we’re building the Fold-a-Phone now, and why our proprietary machine, methodology and test data will enrich our collective understanding of the capabilities – and yes, limitations – of foldable phones as our true next-generation mobile device.

Most current foldable phones are rated for 200,000 to 500,000 folds. As such, we knew we needed to create a robust and adaptable machine that could accommodate book- and flip-style foldable phones of any size, dimension and hinge orientation. (Note: The Fold-a-Phone was not built for trifold phones, which present a unique element to the engineering challenge.)

Moreover, we wanted to test the phone the way people would actually use it. No one just sits around opening and closing their foldable phone at top speed all day. Rather, you open it naturally, spend a few minutes watching a video, browsing a web page, or using an app, close it, and then put it in your pocket. This may happen dozens of times per day.

Mimicking this usage on the Fold-a-Phone limits undue stress on the phone hinge and avoids generating excess heat compared to a test built for speed.

We determined that a cadence of roughly 4 seconds could sidestep technical hurdles while also being reasonably fast. The foldable phone takes 1 second to open, 1 second to fully open, 1 second to close and 1 second to fully close. This cycle counts as a single completed fold. Then, the cycle repeats until either the hinge or the screen gives out.

In conceptualizing and building the Fold-a-Phone, our team has used standard, off-the-shelf components to simplify the machine’s development. The essential design consists of two armatures, distinctly designed to perform the opening and closing movements with both force and grace.

An industrial-grade stepper motor drives each armature, with the host software (running on a connected computer) deterministically adjusting the torque to complete each step of the process while also protecting the phone. If any of the several proximity sensors fail to detect the armature in the correct position, the machine stops immediately.

We also monitor the motors’ power usage to track how well the foldable phone opens and closes over time. We have set up cameras to capture images and video of the test from multiple angles, and we will analyze regular status updates from the computer to determine the current status of the phone and the test.

To ensure we never miss a major development, the entire rig is connected to an EcoFlow Delta 3 Plus power station to guard against electricity outages, and all the output data is saved to multiple locations.

To secure each phone to the machine, we use our CNET Lab Test-winning 3D printer, the Prusa Core One, to 3D-print a custom “sled” that matches each phone’s exact dimensions. We secure the phone to the sled with heavy-duty double-sided tape (yes, it works!) and then screw the sled onto the machine.

There’s always room for improvement. We’re constantly looking for ways to make the machine run more smoothly and quietly without disrupting the folding rhythm.

Apple’s presence is already changing the game and could very well spur innovation in ways we can’t predict today. But until then, our Fold-a-Phone machine will continue to test the iPhone Duo and its competitors, and CNET’s writers will report on how well these individual hinged devices stand up to the challenge – or fold under pressure.

The $400 Boox Palma 2 Pro is a smartphone-sized e-reader designed for people who want to read books, articles, and documents on a small E Ink display. However, it offers more than just a dedicated reading experience because it runs Android and supports Google Play, giving you access to third-party apps. Think of it as a cross between a Kindle and a compact Android tablet.

What drew me to reviewing the Palma 2 Pro was its size. When I’m not reading a physical book, I like to lie on my side and read at night before bed. Most e-readers are squarish and hard to hold without something like a PopSocket, and they can feel too big for reading while lying down. So even though I own a Kindle, I often ended up reading on my iPhone simply because it was more comfortable in my hand. But, reading on your phone for too long can of course cause eye strain. The Palma 2 Pro ultimately solved this problem for me during the two months I spent testing it.

The device’s front-lit screen lets you choose between cool and warm lighting. This was a big plus for me because when I’m reading in bed at night, I don’t want to disturb my sleeping husband with the light. While you can lower the brightness on a smartphone, the Palma 2 Pro can be dimmed to an exceptionally low level. Even when reading in complete darkness, I could see the screen clearly without straining my eyes or disturbing my husband.

Not only was the Boox Palma 2 Pro easier on my eyes, but it was also noticeably lighter than my iPhone, weighing just 175 grams and making it even more comfortable to hold.

And since it’s pocket-sized, I ended up carrying it everywhere, from the doctor’s office to the airport, especially since it fits in my small, everyday purse that isn’t big enough for a Kindle. So, in moments when I would normally scroll on my phone, I was getting some reading in instead.

Since it’s not a locked-down e-reader, you can download apps like Kindle, Kobo, and Libby, as well as apps for audiobooks, podcasts, read-it-later services, and email. This means that you don’t have to use the built-in reader app, and can choose to read on your preferred app.

Access to apps turns the compact E Ink device into a versatile tool for reading, research, and productivity. It’s worth noting that while scrolling through these apps, animations and some interfaces may feel sluggish because not all apps are optimized for E Ink. And while the device is capable of displaying color, images will definitely look less vibrant than they would a smartphone screen.

For me, the Palma 2 Pro became a dedicated device for reading and listening to audiobooks, which allowed me to enjoy the best of both worlds without the usual distractions that come with a smartphone.

The Boox Palma 2 Pro also features a rear camera for scanning documents, a built-in speaker for listening to audio without headphones, and a hybrid SIM slot for cellular data on the go.

The device is compatible with a stylus, so you can take notes and mark up PDFs. However, I can’t speak to that experience since I didn’t receive a stylus with my review unit.

As for battery life, the device lasted about 5–7 days, which worked for me but could be a downside for people who are used to Kindles, which can last for weeks on a single charge. Additionally, if you want to get more juice out of the device, you can just turn off Wi-Fi.

One downside I found, especially for an e-reader at this price point, is that there’s quite a bit of ghosting. Text from a previous page can linger on the screen when you turn to the next page or return to the home screen. There’s a refresh button in the navigation bar that quickly clears it, but it may get annoying for some users to repeatedly have to do that.

I think the Boox Palma 2 Pro is a great device for a very specific type of reader: someone who wants a smartphone-sized device for comfort, portability, as well as access to apps. If you’re someone who loves your Kindle, I don’t think this device is for you. But if, like me, you’re tired of reading on your phone and dealing with eye strain or headaches, the Boox Palma 2 Pro is a great option.

Of course, the price is quite steep for an e-reader, so you’ll have to decide whether the added versatility and portability are worth the extra cost. It’s worth mentioning that Boox is launching a non-Pro Palma 3 soon for $340 that features a black and white display and replaces the Palma 2.

Rather than downloading another app, a growing number of agents can simply be texted like an ordinary person.

You text it what you need, and it can remember context, connect to the apps and services you already use, and complete tasks on your behalf. That can mean scheduling an appointment, organizing a calendar, researching a trip, sending an email, making a reservation, shopping online, or reminding you about something days later.

While Instinct is one of the buzziest AI agents at present, thanks to its $10 billion valuation after its latest funding round of $1 billion, there are many others also making a play for this space.

Below are the most notable options so far, from general-purpose personal assistants to agents designed for families, travel, and work.

Ask Proxy’s biggest differentiator is that it “thinks like a woman,” meaning it aims to anticipate your needs before you even ask, the company explains on its website.

It works like a chatbot you can text like a friend, whether you need gift ideas, help planning an event, or recommendations for things to do that weekend. You can ask it to add important dates to your calendar, set reminders, or help with everyday decisions. You can also send it photos, like an empty table, to figure out how to decorate the table for a dinner party.

Unlike a typical chatbot, Ask Proxy also takes a proactive approach. It can reach out when it notices something you might forget or when it comes across a new activity, restaurant, or experience you might enjoy.

There’s also a work-focused version, which the company explains “gets employees back to work faster, while keeping personal data off company systems.”

The personal version costs $1 per day. For businesses, pricing starts at $15 per employee per month for large companies and $23 per employee per month for small teams with fewer than 49 employees.

Ask Proxy works over SMS. You can text “start” to 820-732-2625.

Caddy is an AI assistant that turns the information scattered across your phone into things you can actually act on. It lives in iMessage for iPhone users and RCS for Android users, so there’s no separate app or inbox to constantly check.

For instance, an email contains an appointment that needs to be added to your calendar or a friend sends a list of things to pick up. Instead of worrying about all the details getting buried, Caddy connects to your calendars and conversations, then identifies things that may require action. It can add events to your calendar, set reminders, keep track of follow-ups, and even do research for you.

Caddy has been available in public beta since April 2026.

Comma is designed to manage tasks across both work and everyday life. Comma carries tasks through to completion, checking its own work and determining when a task is finished.

Completed work is surfaced for review when a decision or approval is needed. It also proactively notifies users and recommends the next steps.

It can work across a user’s browser, computer, and files, while remaining accessible remotely through messaging apps such as Signal, Telegram, and WeChat. Comma maintains context and memory across devices and conversations, and can deliver daily briefings, flag urgent matters, and summarize recorded calls.

Fambot is an AI-powered “chief of staff” for families, designed to bring together the many moving parts of family life, including school communications, sports activities, meal planning, calendars, and other day-to-day responsibilities, and turn them into an organized plan.

The service is designed to work across both an app and text messages. Fambot is available on iOS devices, Android devices, and the web, and families can also interact with it directly over SMS. Every night, Fambot automatically sends a summary of the following day, including upcoming events, to-dos, and details such as school uniform or packing requirements. Parents can reply to those messages to ask questions, check information, or make changes to their calendar.

Fambot currently connects to Gmail, Google Calendar, Outlook, and WhatsApp, with support for Apple Calendar planned for the future.

The company launched in beta in early September 2026 and has raised $3.5 million in pre-seed funding. The service is currently free during the beta period, but Fambot expects to eventually charge roughly the equivalent of a Netflix subscription.

Folk is designed to be an AI assistant you can reach through iMessage, WhatsApp, and Telegram. It can remember personal context, manage reminders, research topics, track flights, handle email, and perform real-world tasks such as restaurant reservations.

One distinction is that Folk runs on its own private cloud computer. It can also run code and execute multi-step tasks rather than merely responding conversationally.

Folk launched its beta in May 2026, and it’s available for free but also offers a Pro subscription for $8.33/month if you want unlimited tasks running in the background.

Instinct first made headlines after raising $350 million at a $2.5 billion valuation. In September 2026, it raised $1 billion, valuing it at $10 billion.

The always-available agent can take actions on your behalf, rather than simply answer questions. Users can communicate with Instinct by text or voice and connect it to the apps and services they already use, such as email, calendar, Google Workspace, and more. The company says early users have used Instinct for tasks ranging from planning trips and buying groceries to booking tickets and canceling subscriptions.

In September, Instinct began giving users dedicated email addresses for their assistants. The idea is to let Instinct create and manage accounts on a user’s behalf without filling the user’s personal inbox. The agent can use its address to sign up for services, contact businesses, follow up on requests, and handle tasks that require an email account. (However, this level of autonomy has also raised concerns about privacy and security.) It’s also begun rolling out support for placing calls.

Powered by Hermes Agent, Iris is designed to let users delegate tasks to an AI assistant directly through iMessage. Users can connect the apps they use and hand over a task with a simple message. The agent then works through the required steps, while retaining relevant context from previous interactions. It’s also designed to follow up with users proactively rather than waiting for another request.

Martin is a general-purpose personal assistant reachable through SMS as well as phone, WhatsApp, email, Slack, and its own iOS app.

It can help manage calendars, email, reminders, tasks, notes, and communications. It can also act on behalf of users by sending messages or making phone calls, while providing daily summaries of what needs attention.

It’s a bit on the pricier side, with subscriptions starting at $21 per month.

Maverick describes itself as an “AI companion who lives in your texts,” acting as a personal assistant that helps manage everyday tasks directly from your messages.

It can fix typos, cancel subscriptions, schedule meetings from voice notes, and help group chats settle on a date for an event. One notable feature is that Maverick asks for your permission before taking action.

The platform also offers hundreds of integrations, including Discord, Gmail, and Slack, allowing it to connect with the apps you already use.

It’s currently in beta and free to use on iMessage. There’s also a Pro version for €27 per month (or approximately $30.25 USD), which includes 500 tasks a month,  20 connected apps, scheduled tasks, and memory.

Miso takes the personal-assistant concept and applies it specifically to travel.

It operates through iMessage and combines AI trip planning with support from a dedicated travel team. Users can arrange flights through messaging while Miso takes into account their travel preferences, loyalty points, and other priorities. Miso also gives travelers access to trip details and flight updates, along with personalized recommendations.

Ohai is another family assistant focused on helping households manage the constant stream of information and tasks involved in everyday family life.

Users can send messages, forward emails, or make voice requests, and the assistant can transform them into schedules, reminders, and plans. It can also coordinate calendars, household chores, and meals across multiple members of a family.

It includes a free basic option, but also offers different subscriptions based on family size, starting at $9.99 per month.

Similar to the other family-focused agents, the idea behind Ollie is that families already have the information they need, but it lives across school messages, calendars, task lists, and group chats. Ollie aims to bring those pieces together and keep them in sync. It can keep track of calendars, emails, reminders, tasks, and other household details, then deliver relevant information to users through text.

Its biggest differentiator from other agents is that Ollie is one of the first mainstream, family-focused AI assistants to achieve SOC 2 compliance, a widely used security standard.

Launched in June 2026, Ollie offers a free tier, as well as paid plans starting at $25 per month for 150 messages. A $100-per-month plan includes up to 1,000 messages.

Orbits also has an AI assistant that is focused on the household.

It works through both the Orbits app and text messaging, and can bring together calendars, lists, and conversations while also carrying out tasks for you. That can include searching the web, making purchases, booking restaurants, changing appointments, and requesting quotes for household services.

Additionally, Orbits can remind family members about chores, communicate with service providers, and help coordinate responsibilities beyond the immediate household.

Orbits is backed by investors like Andreessen Horowitz (a16z) Speedrun fund, N49P, and Garage Capital.

Users can interact with Pally as they would with a friend while giving it access to the apps they already rely on. The assistant can connect information spread across services such as WhatsApp, Gmail, calendars, and Google Drive, helping users keep track of plans, receipts, dates, files, and other details.

There’s a free plan available. It works over iMessage, RCS, and Telegram.

Poke launched in March 2026 as a general-purpose assistant that you can reach through text messaging. It can handle tasks involving calendars, planning, health and fitness, smart-home controls, photos, and other everyday activities.

Poke became particularly notable in June 2026 when Apple approved Poke as the first AI agent on the Apple Messages for Business platform. A month later, parent company The Interaction Company of California was acquired by AI coding startup Cognition in a deal valued in the low nine figures.

Rene is an AI personal assistant designed to be a user’s “best contact” rather than another productivity agent. It’s text-based and can turn natural-language conversations into tasks, reminders, summaries, and other follow-up actions. Rene also maintains context across conversations, allowing it to remember details and surface things that may need attention later.

Additionally, it has a browser, can write code, shop for you, as well as create slides and images.

Rene is currently available on iMessage, Telegram, and WhatsApp.

Instead of waiting for users to open an app, Skye is an “agentic homescreen” for the iPhone that analyzes information from connected services and surfaces what it thinks is relevant through contextual cards, suggestions, and daily briefings. The assistant can generate audio briefings, prepare information ahead of meetings, surface reminders, and identify potentially useful information.

The company, Signull Labs, raised about $3.6 million in seed funding in 2025. It entered private testing and beta in April 2026, and hasn’t yet announced a public release date.

Stanley is an AI content assistant aimed at content creators rather than general personal productivity. Available through iMessage, Telegram, and the web, it acts as a kind of AI head of content, helping users come up with ideas, turn them into posts, and maintain a consistent publishing schedule.

For instance, Stanley can connect to a user’s Instagram account, using that context to suggest content based on what the user has actually been doing. #4063. 6 ways bats are surprisingly human

📅 24 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Published Oct 24, 2026 1:30 PM EDT

Bat Week officially begins today. It’s the one week where Earth’s only winged mammals get top billing. Often associated with disease, vampires, and other generally unpleasant things, bats are actually important pollinators and play critical roles in our ecosystem.

“They’re just so cool and so interesting. During first forays into bat studies, it was just one question after another,” Rachel Page, an evolutionary biologist at the Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute in Panama, tells Popular Science. “The more I sort of scratched the surface, the more interesting it became—which is the fun part.”

One reason bats are so interesting to scientists like Page is that they are surprisingly similar to humans. Here’s how.

Sharing a meal with good company brings immense joy for us humans. For many vampire bat species (subfamily Desmodontinae), sharing food is a matter of survival. They survive exclusively on blood and can only go about two nights without a blood meal before they die. To get around this, they rely on their social networks.

“They have the equivalent of friends. Basically, if a bat comes back from a night of foraging and has not been successful, other members of that social group will share food,” Page explains. “They’ll regurgitate blood for the bat that came back hungry.”

If that donor bat has difficulty getting food later, the one recipient will often become the donor. “So there’s this very reciprocal relationship,” says Page.

According to Page, bats will have their distinctive personalities just like people. It’s present across species, but also individual bats as well.

“You have some species that are very feisty, and they’ll go out of their way to pick a fight, and then other ones that are much more passive, calm, and very gentle,” she says.

At the edge of Panama’s Soberanía National Park, tiny, fruit-eating bats will take over the roost of larger, carnivorous bats. The Seba’s short-tailed bat (Carollia perspicillata) weighs roughly the same as a strawberry. By comparison, the frog-eating, fringed-lipped bat (Trachops cirrhosus) weighs roughly as much as an apricot and twice as much as the short-tailed bat.

Page and her colleagues in Panama have witnessed fights between the two species from roosts set up throughout the forest.

“Basically, it was the male Seba’s short-tailed bat who was instigating the fight. He was coming over and was kind of boxing and threatening much larger bats with wide open mouths and lots of teeth,” Page recalls. “And he just kept at it, and then finally, this group of fringed-lipped bats left.”

Even in the thick jungles of Panama, bats can find motionless insects on leaves. Initially, scientists believed that the thick tree cover would give small insects an “acoustic camouflage” that would confuse a bat’s echolocation system. But they were wrong.

The bats can see through this acoustic camouflage by changing the angle at which they approach the still insect.

“If you approach a flat leaf at just the right angle, you can actually get an echolocation signature,” Page explains. “You can get an echolocation signal that jumps back at you, but only if you direct your echolocation call at just the right angle.”

That’s exactly what the bats were doing in order to find insects who tried to hide in leaves.

When it’s time for the babies to wean or leave the roost, mother tent-making bats (Uroderma bilobatum) will physically prod their young with their forearms.

“There is this interesting tug that you see across so many different species. For birds, when they’re ready to fledge, there’s this sort of uncertainty.”

When sick, humans and even non-human primates change their behavior. We isolate so that the disease does not spread, and will look to plants and other sources of medicine for relief. Bats appear to change their diet to less nutritious leaves. While this may sound backwards, it’s in the hope that they have medicinal properties.

In an ongoing study with a team from the Ukrainian Bat Rehabilitation Center, the Smithsonian team is looking at the bats from Barro Colorado Island in Panama.

“We have fecal samples from these bats, so we were able to see exactly what they are eating,” says Page.

In addition to changing their diets, vampire bats will self-isolate when they are sick and others will act in a way familiar to any human parent—continuing to care for our young, even when sick.

“You don’t do as many of the normal behaviors unless it’s taking care of your babies,” says Page. “And again, it’s like a human relationship. If your little kid has a fever, you’re not going to shut them in a room and leave them. You’re not going to desert them.”

To find dinner, the fringe-lipped bat (Trachops cirrhosus) will listen in on their prey’s mating calls. These frog-eating bats eavesdrop on the mating calls that the male frogs use to attract females. They then use the calls to assess if the frog is edible or poisonous before going in for the kill.

“They’ve really cracked this communication of another entire group,” says Page.

According to Page, it’s similar to being in a quiet room and hearing someone open up a bag of potato chips and is loudly eating. The noise makes everyone pay attention. “Bats attend to those acoustic signals of other bats, and that attention then helps them learn,” Page says. “They can learn those distinctions of palatable versus poisonous, and they can learn that socially as well from observing the other behaviors with bats.”

#4172. A hidden bias in human language challenges 70 years of science

📅 24 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Researchers at the University of Vermont have discovered a surprising pattern in human language that challenges a scientific theory widely accepted for more than 70 years. Their findings suggest that the meaning of words is shaped by something more fundamental than emotion: our understanding of power, danger, and order.

The study, published in Science Advances, introduces a new approach called "ousiometrics," which uses mathematical methods to measure the essential meaning of language. Its most striking finding is that the words people use show a consistent and widespread preference for safety over danger.

This hidden safety bias could transform how researchers study language, understand human behavior, and develop artificial intelligence systems that interpret what people say and write.

Scientists Discover a Hidden Safety Bias in Human Language

Since the 1950s, much of the scientific understanding of word meaning has relied on a framework developed through pioneering research by psychologist Charles Osgood and others. Known as the VAD model, it describes meaning through three emotional dimensions: valence (positive vs. negative), arousal (excited vs. calm), and dominance (controlling vs. submissive).

This approach became influential in psychology, linguistics, and artificial intelligence, particularly in systems designed to identify the emotional tone of written language.

However, the University of Vermont researchers found evidence that this long-established model overlooks important aspects of how meaning is organized.

Their analysis examined billions of individual uses of more than 20,000 words across a wide range of real-world texts. Using advanced computational techniques, the team identified a different underlying structure. They found that the three dimensions in the traditional VAD model are not independent of one another, as previously assumed.

Instead, their results indicate that word meaning can be more effectively described through three separate dimensions: power (weak vs. powerful), danger (safe vs. dangerous), and structure (ordered vs. chaotic).

The research, supported by the US National Science Foundation, Google, MassMutual, and other funders, found that this new framework accounted for more than 90% of the variation in measured word meaning. The traditional VAD approach explained approximately 72%.

That improvement could be especially important as large language models, automated content moderation, and other AI technologies increasingly depend on understanding human communication.

Why Human Language Favors Safety Over Danger

One of the study's most unexpected discoveries emerged when the researchers examined how words actually appear in everyday communication.

Across books, news reporting, social media, and spoken language, words associated with safety appeared much more frequently than those associated with danger.

This finding offers a new explanation for a familiar phenomenon called the Pollyanna principle, which describes the tendency for human language to lean toward positive rather than negative words.

For decades, researchers generally interpreted this pattern as evidence that people favor positive emotional expressions. The new analysis suggests that something more fundamental may be responsible.

According to the researchers, the apparent preference for positivity largely reflects an underlying preference for safety.

"The Pollyanna principle's positivity bias," the study concludes. "is, in fact, a one-dimensional projection of an underlying safety bias."

"This is a big observation that comes out of this work," said Peter Dodds, director of the UVM's Complex Systems Institute and senior author of the study. "Expressions of safety are crucial to all language."

Could Human Language Be Shaped by Survival?

The discovery raises an intriguing possibility: the structure of human language may reflect evolutionary pressures associated with staying alive.

From this perspective, words do more than communicate feelings. They also help people recognize threats, evaluate uncertainty, establish trust, and coordinate actions with others.

Being able to distinguish between safety and danger is essential to survival. The researchers suggest that this basic human priority may also influence how meaning is organized in language.

Throughout daily life, people constantly exchange information about whether individuals, places, situations, and behaviors are threatening or safe. The new findings suggest that these distinctions may be more central to communication than previously recognized.

They also offer a different interpretation of why positive language is so common. Rather than simply expressing happiness or approval, many positive words may convey a sense of security, stability, or predictability.

Julia Zimmerman, a postdoctoral researcher in UVM's Computational Story Lab and a co-author of the study, said the three newly identified dimensions reflect experiences that are fundamental to human life.

"Power, danger, and structure," she said, "are relevant to every person that's ever lived."

Researchers have also long recognized that people tend to favor language associated with goodness and low aggression. The team argues that these familiar tendencies may arise from the same deeper pattern.

"We now understand," the team writes, that these are "shadows of an underlying linguistic safety bias."

Although the results are consistent with a possible connection between language and survival, they do not establish that evolutionary pressures directly caused the safety bias.

A Discovery That Could Change How AI Understands Language

The findings have implications that extend well beyond linguistics.

Many artificial intelligence systems use sentiment analysis to determine whether language expresses positive, negative, or neutral feelings. Some approaches also draw on the traditional VAD framework to measure emotional meaning.

If that framework does not fully capture the underlying structure of language, systems built around it could overlook important distinctions in what people communicate.

The researchers suggest that incorporating power, danger, and structure could eventually help AI models interpret language more accurately and explain their assessments more clearly. This may be particularly useful when analyzing trust, risk, and decision-making.

In linguistics, the findings offer a different way to understand the foundations of meaning. Instead of treating emotional tone as the primary organizing principle, the new framework emphasizes distinctions related to strength, safety, and predictability.

Psychology could also be affected. Decades of studies have used the VAD model to investigate emotions, perception, and behavior. A different set of fundamental dimensions could encourage researchers to reconsider how some of those findings are interpreted.

The discovery may also interest neuroscientists. The human brain is highly sensitive to threats and signals of safety, and the newly identified linguistic bias suggests that these biological priorities may be reflected in language. This potential connection could help researchers explore relationships between brain activity and symbolic communication, although further research would be needed to establish them.

A New Tool Measures the Hidden Meaning of Words

To investigate these patterns, the researchers created analytical methods designed to measure meaning across enormous collections of written and spoken language.

One of their central innovations is the "ousiometer," a tool that rapidly evaluates the essential meaning of large bodies of text and produces an average meaning score.

(The word "ouisa" comes from Ancient Greek and is a root for the English word "essence.")

The ousiometer builds on an earlier tool developed by the research team called the "hedonometer" (a happiness meter). While that instrument measures the overall emotional tone of language, the new approach examines broader patterns of meaning.

The researchers demonstrated that the ousiometer can analyze material from remarkably different sources, including Jane Austen's novels, Arthur Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes stories, the New York Times, Wikipedia, Twitter, and transcripts of talk radio programs.

The tool can also track how meaning shifts as a story unfolds.

For example, the researchers analyzed an English translation of Victor Hugo's Les Misérables and mapped its changing meaning throughout the novel.

They called this changing pattern an "ousiometric trajectory." The visualization resembles the twisting shape of a multicolored protein, with the narrative moving across a grid based on four contrasting pairs: dangerous/safe, weak/powerful, gentle/aggressive, and bad/good.

By following this path, researchers can observe how the underlying meaning of the text changes from one section of the story to another.

Why Counting Word Frequency Changes the Results

Another important innovation involved distinguishing between words themselves and how often people actually use them.

In linguistics, researchers describe individual words as categories called "types," while each appearance of a word in text is known as a "token."

(For example, as a category "apple" is a type and every time the word "apple" is used in a sentence is a token.)

This distinction matters because a word that appears millions of times contributes much more to everyday communication than one that is rarely used.

Earlier research often gave each word equal importance, regardless of its frequency. The Vermont team instead incorporated how often words appear in real communication, allowing patterns to emerge that might otherwise remain hidden.

This approach was particularly important for identifying the widespread preference for safety-related language.

The ten-member research team was led by Peter Dodds and Chris Danforth, professors in UVM's College of Engineering and Mathematical Sciences. Their collaborators included researchers from the Santa Fe Institute, the Complexity Science Hub in Austria, Howard Hughes Medical Center, the University of California, Berkeley, the University of Adelaide, and MassMutual Data Science.

Rethinking What Human Language Reveals About Us

Understanding this hidden safety bias could have consequences for how researchers study communication across society.

If words consistently reflect a preference for safety, that tendency may influence how stories are told, how information spreads, and how people interpret events and experiences.

The findings could eventually inform research into political communication, mental health messaging, and AI systems designed to interact with people.

More broadly, the study challenges the idea that human language can be understood primarily through emotions such as happiness, fear, and excitement.

Instead, the researchers propose that meaning has a deeper structure connected to power, danger, and order. Their findings suggest that language may reflect the challenges humans face when navigating uncertain environments, maintaining relationships, and identifying threats.

By revealing this previously overlooked structure, the team offers a new way to understand what words communicate and why certain kinds of meaning dominate everyday conversation. Human language, their research suggests, may be more closely connected to our fundamental need for safety than scientists have traditionally recognized.

#4188. NASA’s asteroid Bennu samples reveal a 4.5-billion-year-old secret

📅 24 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Samples collected from asteroid Bennu by NASA are revealing surprising new details about how our Solar System formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago. A new chemical analysis challenges earlier theories about the asteroid's birthplace and suggests that Jupiter played an important role in shaping the materials from which Bennu and other ancient objects formed.

Bennu completes one orbit around the Sun every 1.2 years and makes a relatively close approach to Earth about every six years, coming within roughly 300,000 kilometers (186,000 miles). NASA's OSIRIS-REx mission traveled to the asteroid, collected material from its surface, and successfully delivered the samples to Earth in 2023.

On September 23, 2023, a capsule carrying approximately 120 grams of asteroid material landed in the Utah desert. Scientists around the world subsequently received small portions of the precious cargo for detailed study.

Among them was Maria Schönbächler, Professor of Isotope Geochemistry at ETH Zurich, whose laboratory received half a gram of Bennu material. Her team immediately began examining the samples for clues about the asteroid's history.

The research, now published in Science Advances, has uncovered a distinctive chemical signature that not only helps identify Bennu's origins but also provides new evidence about the processes that shaped the early Solar System.

Bennu's Chemical Fingerprint Reveals Its Cosmic Relatives

To investigate Bennu's origins, the ETH Zurich researchers examined isotopes of three elements: iron, titanium, and chromium.

Isotopes are different forms of the same chemical element that contain varying numbers of neutrons, giving them slightly different masses. By measuring the relative amounts of these isotopes, scientists can identify a material's chemical fingerprint. These patterns provide clues about where an asteroid's ingredients originated and, in some cases, when they formed.

The analysis revealed that iron and titanium are distributed remarkably evenly throughout Bennu's material. It also uncovered a close chemical relationship between Bennu, the asteroid Ryugu, and a rare group of primitive meteorites known as CI meteorites.

CI meteorites are carbon-rich remnants of the early Solar System that have undergone relatively little alteration over billions of years. They are exceptionally uncommon among meteorites recovered on Earth.

Bennu, Ryugu, and CI meteorites share strikingly similar isotopic fingerprints, suggesting that their original materials came from the same reservoir of ancient cosmic dust. Their chemical signatures also differ substantially from those of other known asteroids, meteorite groups, and planets.

These similarities offer an important clue about where these objects formed and what conditions existed during the Solar System's earliest stages.

Scientists Discover Bennu's Unexpected Birthplace

For years, researchers generally believed that primitive asteroids like Bennu originated in the outer Solar System, perhaps in the same distant regions where comets formed. They also suspected that Bennu's original material came together relatively late in the Solar System's development.

The new findings challenge both assumptions.

Instead, the researchers propose that the material that became Bennu, Ryugu, and CI meteorites accumulated near a boundary known as the water-ice line.

This boundary marks the region in the disk surrounding the young Sun where temperatures were low enough for water vapor to freeze into ice. Around 4.5 billion years ago, it may have been a particularly important meeting place for material traveling from different parts of the developing Solar System.

Dust originating in both the inner and outer regions mixed near this boundary. Water ice helped the smallest particles stick together, allowing them to gradually accumulate into larger bodies.

"Bennu is a hybrid: the material does not clearly match either the inner or the outer Solar System," says Schönbächler.

Its unusual chemical composition suggests that Bennu formed from a combination of materials originating in both regions rather than from a single, isolated source.

Jupiter May Have Played a Crucial Role in Bennu's Formation

The researchers also developed a scenario explaining how this mixing could have occurred, with Jupiter emerging as a key player.

Jupiter began forming extraordinarily early in Solar System history, within approximately one million years of the Sun's birth. At that time, the young Sun was surrounded by a rotating disk of gas, dust, and ice.

Within this disk, countless small particles gradually collided and stuck together, building larger objects through a process called accretion. Over time, some of these growing bodies became asteroids and planets.

As Jupiter rapidly increased in mass, its immense gravitational influence began reshaping the distribution of material throughout the disk.

The researchers suggest that the growing gas giant acted somewhat like a structural barrier, preventing most larger particles from crossing its orbital region while still allowing finer dust to move around it.

This fine material, originating from different parts of the disk, could then mix extensively near the water-ice line.

According to the proposed scenario, the parent bodies of Bennu and Ryugu, along with the objects that produced CI meteorites, formed from this thoroughly mixed material.

Jupiter may therefore have helped determine not only where these ancient asteroids formed but also which ingredients became incorporated into them.

Ancient Dust and Ice Reveal the Ingredients That Built Earth

The proposed formation scenario could also explain two of Bennu's most intriguing characteristics: its abundance of water-bearing material and its chemical resemblance to the Sun.

Near the water-ice boundary, ice could evaporate as temperatures changed, releasing water vapor that later condensed again in cooler regions. This process would have helped concentrate water in the material from which Bennu formed.

Meanwhile, Jupiter's influence may have limited the movement of larger particles, leaving the asteroid's parent material dominated by fine dust.

Because these tiny particles could circulate and mix efficiently throughout the disk, they preserved a chemical composition that closely resembles the overall elemental mixture found in the Sun.

The process is somewhat similar to the way fine household dust spreads and settles across many different surfaces rather than remaining in one location.

This extensive mixing could explain why Bennu's material retains such a representative sample of the ingredients present during the Solar System's earliest history.

"Bennu may offer our best glimpse of the original mix of chemical elements from which the terrestrial planets were ultimately built," as Schönbächler states.

Bennu is considered an exceptionally primitive asteroid because its material formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago and has experienced relatively little chemical alteration since then.

That makes its samples particularly valuable for reconstructing the conditions that existed before Earth and the other rocky planets finished forming.

By studying the asteroid's chemistry, scientists can better understand how the Solar System developed and which processes determined the composition of its planets.

Bennu's water-bearing minerals and organic compounds also make it important for investigating how early Earth obtained some of the chemical ingredients necessary for life.

Although these findings do not establish exactly how those materials reached our planet, they provide valuable evidence about where such ingredients originated and how they were distributed throughout the young Solar System.

A New Mission to Mars' Moon Could Reveal More

Despite the new discoveries, several questions remain unanswered.

"We are now wondering whether other asteroids have the same isotopic signature as Bennu and Ryugu," Schönbächler goes on to state.

Researchers also want to determine more precisely how Jupiter influenced the movement of dust and whether its gravitational effects were responsible for favoring the accumulation of particularly fine particles.

Additional asteroid samples could help scientists test the proposed formation scenario and establish whether Bennu's chemical signature is shared by other ancient bodies.

With the Bennu analysis now complete, Schönbächler is looking ahead to another opportunity to examine material from the early Solar System.

Japan's space agency, JAXA, is preparing a sample-return mission to Phobos, one of Mars' two moons. The mission is scheduled to launch at the end of October 2026, with the goal of collecting material from the Martian moon and bringing it back to Earth.

"It would be exciting to also obtain this material to analyse it in my laboratory," she says.

Schönbächler plans to apply to JAXA for access to the samples. However, the scientific community will need to be patient. The capsule carrying material from Phobos is not expected to return to Earth until 2031.

Those future samples could provide another important piece of the puzzle, helping researchers determine how different regions of the early Solar System exchanged material and ultimately produced the planets, moons, and asteroids we observe today.

Renting one is ranging from about $160,000 to $180,000 a month, according to IBA data. A competing engine for the A320neo family made by CFM International, a joint venture of GE Aerospace and France's Safran, is far from a bargain, renting in a similar range of $130,000 to $180,000 a month.

The problems with young retirements predate Spirit's collapse.

The 4-year-old Airbus plane that was torn down outside Phoenix in May is a key example. The aircraft was put in storage because of the engine recall, and it stayed until it was torn apart.

But the company's demise has brought more of its Airbus fleet to the market, forcing owners to figure out if it's worth the wait for engines or other parts.

CNBC visited AerSale's facility in Goodyear on a scorching day in late June, less than two months after Spirit collapsed, where dozens of the idle yellow planes were stored. Others won't make it back into the air if there's enough demand for the parts and too long of a wait to get the plane flying again.

So far, the nightmare scenario of mass victim takeovers and breaches hasn’t come to pass. The PHP Foundation says it hasn’t seen any successful exploitation of the bugs, collectively dubbed PHP2Shell, and many publicly circulating potential exploits appear to be ineffective. However, perhaps the most interesting role of AI amid the chaos this week is how advanced models helped researchers uncover practical exploitations for the vulnerabilities, which were known about, at least in part, for years. In other words, AI helped turn historic vulnerability into a current crisis.

So are we all about to be walloped by a wave of critical vulnerabilities that have been kicking around for decades?

Researchers at security company Calif were the first to identify the PHP2Shell vulnerabilities and create exploits in early August—partially using frontier AI tools as part of OpenAI’s Patch the Planet program, but also using expert human knowledge of PHP, its dynamics, and its weaknesses. Some of the underlying issues were identified in 2024 but were deemed too complex to exploit at the time. Following Calif’s discovery, researchers at OpenAI and the Google-owned security firm Wiz independently discovered PHP2Shell flaws using AI tools.

“The vulnerability had been known to the PHP team for years, but because it could not be leveraged by an attacker—at least, not a human one—it was not addressed,” says Sagi Tzadik, a security researcher at Wiz.

No software is perfect. There are inevitably flaws in even the most critical, foundational digital tools, but the expanding capabilities of AI models have made it faster to analyze and potentially exploit vulnerabilities. This puts even more pressure on under-resourced IT teams, developers, and admins to keep up. Microsoft’s security updates included a record 1,453 security vulnerabilities this month alone. Meanwhile, Bank of America recently hired 1,000 consultants to fix flaws discovered by Anthropic’s Mythos. Security researchers emphasize, though, that AI is ultimately not doing anything new, but rather magnifying existing dynamics of vulnerability discovery and coordinated disclosure.

“We'll definitely see more of these long-life bugs that are in every version of the software for the last decade or whatever,” says Jake Williams, vice president of research and development at Hunter Strategy and a former NSA hacker. “But I think there’s only so much of this low-hanging fruit. There isn’t much else that has the specific reach of PHP. It’s a pretty wild attack surface—most other tools aren’t exposed to the internet by design.”

Researchers note, too, that even with frontier models, most examples of sophisticated AI-aided hacking also involve human expertise. As Ryan Austin, the Calif researcher who first discovered the PHP flaws, wrote this week, “No model is simply capable of unearthing ‘all bugs’ with simple prompts. At least for now.”

In short, security engineer and researcher Niels Provos says, everyone can dial back their freak-outs, at least a bit. “To be honest, I am not really worried,” he says, about AI finding an overwhelming number of historic bugs that are newly exploitable—or new vulnerabilities that couldn’t have been practically exploited without AI. “People have now been looking with AI for over a year, and it required a highly skilled team to find this one. I guess we will see whether I am completely wrong, but I doubt it.”

#3773. New mRNA Vaccine Reduces Risk of Melanoma Recurrence by Over 40 Percent

📅 24 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

An experimental vaccine offers new hope to patients with melanoma, one of the most aggressive forms of skin cancer. The pharmaceutical companies Merck and Moderna released the results of a Phase 3 clinical trial that evaluated the efficacy of a personalized mRNA-based vaccine that, when combined with immunotherapy, reduced the risk of disease recurrence or death by 42 percent compared to treatment with the immunotherapy drug alone.

Georgina Long, medical director of the Australian Melanoma Institute and the study’s principal investigator, said in a statement that these results are “transformative” and “have the potential to make a foundational shift in how we treat patients with earlier-stage melanoma.”

The trial, known as INTerpath-001, included 1,137 people with high-risk melanoma who had undergone surgery to completely remove their tumors. The participants were divided into two groups. Those in the first group received only Pembrolizumab, a drug that helps the immune system detect and fight cancer cells, while those in the second group were treated with the same drug in combination with the mRNA vaccine, called Intismeran Autogene.

After monitoring the participants for an average of 24.6 months, the researchers determined that addition of the vaccine reduced the risk of cancer recurrence or death by 42 percent compared to Pembrolizumab alone.

The results also showed that the experimental treatment reduced the risk of the disease spreading to other parts of the body by 38 percent. At the end of the follow-up period, 82.2 percent of patients who received both treatments remained recurrence-free, compared with 70 percent of those who received Pembrolizumab alone.

“The results of the INTerpath-001 study represent a major breakthrough in the treatment of melanoma and, in particular, in the development of personalized medicine,” Eva Muñoz Couselo—who leads a melanoma research group at the Vall d’Hebron Institute of Oncology, in Barcelona and who was not involved in the study—told SMC Spain. “For the first time, a Phase III trial has demonstrated that an individualized therapeutic vaccine, designed on the basis of the molecular characteristics of each patient’s tumor, can significantly improve the outcomes of conventional immunotherapy.”

The laboratories explained that the vaccine was developed from a tumor sample taken from each patient, which was used to identify specific mutations that would create distinct proteins the immune system could learn to recognize in order to attack cancer cells. The scientists selected up to three of these molecular signatures per patient.

During the trial, some participants experienced mild side effects such as fever, fatigue, and pain at the injection site. These side effects were seen in earlier trials as well, and the Phase 3 findings demonstrate similar safety when the vaccine is used in the combination with Pembrolizumab.

“These results reinforce our conviction that mRNA has the potential to fundamentally change how we treat cancer,” Stéphane Bancel, CEO of Moderna, said in a statement.

The pharmaceutical companies emphasized that the vaccine is not designed to prevent melanoma but rather to reduce the risk of recurrence and death following prior medical treatment. They also noted that the therapy remains under investigation to validate its efficacy and obtain approval from health authorities before widespread use.

Most people diagnosed with melanoma have a high chance of survival, especially if detected early, but as one of the most common cancers, melanoma kills over 50,000 people globally each year. In theory, similar vaccines could be developed for other kinds of tumors in the future. Earlier this year, another trial demonstrated that personalized mRNA vaccines can greatly improve survival among patients with pancreatic cancer, one of the deadliest kinds of tumor.

“This is the major breakthrough, because conceptually it would make it possible to treat not only melanoma but also other tumors in which the immune system can be reactivated,” Marisol Soengas—a leading melanoma researcher at Spain’s National Cancer Research Center also not involved in the trial—told SMC Spain.

That doesn’t mean a cure for cancer is right around the corner. “The challenge is to ensure that this personalization can be scaled up to a large number of patients and that the cost is affordable for health care systems,” Soengas said. “It is logistically complex. It is literally a bespoke vaccine.”

#4068. Why volunteers are gluing seeds to clam shells in Connecticut

📅 24 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Published Oct 24, 2026 11:18 AM EDT

Environmental restoration sometimes requires out-of-the-box thinking. Along the waters of Long Island Sound in New York and Connecticut, experts are tackling the challenge of restoring crucial eelgrass habitats by gluing the plant’s seeds to clams.

Eelgrass (Zostera marina) is an important member of the ecosystem. It provides foraging areas and shelter for young fish and invertebrates, diminishes coastal erosion, draws in excess nutrients, filters water, and releases oxygen, for instance. Compared to the 1930s, eelgrass meadows in and around Long Island Sound have decreased by more than 90 percent, according to regional environmental action group Save the Sound.

To make matters worse, eelgrass is difficult to restore and there have been a lot of failed attempts, Bill Lucey, a Long Island Soundkeeper from the organization, tells Popular Science. In 2025, the team tried a different approach—recruiting clams as underwater gardeners. The strategy was originally ideated by Robert Vasiluth, the founder of Submerged Aquatic Vegetation EngineersEnvironmental (SAVE) .

The approach consists of attaching eelgrass seeds to the clam’s shells. When the clams are released into the sound, they bury into the sediment, taking the seeds down to the bottom and effectively planting them. When they conducted drone imagery to check in on their results this year, they found that the eelgrass meadow coverage had increased by 178 percent.

“It needs to survive for three years before we can really trumpet our horns and say it’s working, but the actual method of putting clams out and seeds attached to them really worked. The generation of the plants was incredible,” Lucey explains.

During their 2025 planting, the team used glue typically used in coral reef restoration to attach seeds to the clams. But then Connecticut College biologist Maria Rose yielded better germination rates when she used wet pieces of rice paper (the kind you make spring rolls with) to seal the seeds against the shell. The Save the Sound team asked its volunteers to do the same earlier this week.

“The problem with the spring roll wrappers is yesterday [Monday, October 19], when we deployed them for the first time in scale, they weren’t all staying on,” says Lucey. “So what we’ve started doing today [Tuesday, October 20] is putting a little pocket of seeds, 10 to 20 eelgrass seeds on the top of the clam, putting a little circle of this [coral] glue around it, and then putting the spring roll wrapper stamp over that and wetting it. And so it keeps the seeds moist, and the glue holds the spring roll wrapper to the clam till it dissolves, and then it’ll release the seeds next to the clam.”

Save the Sound is aiming to release 10,000 claims and 300,000 seeds this season.

#4346. The datacentre next door: on the frontline of tech’s latest David v Goliath battle

📅 24 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

The AI boom is pitting local residents against corporate giants seeking to power their energy-hungry networks – but one Welsh city is fighting back

Dale Johnston opens the gate to his neat suburban garden on the western fringes of Newport in South Wales. There’s a trampoline and a slide on the lawn for his grandchildren, and chairs for him and his wife. He should be enjoying the light draining from the perfect autumnal sky – but almost his entire view is blotted out by a vast expanse of prefabricated panels.

The 23 metre-high grey hulk looming over him and his neighbours is a hyperscale datacentre. It is heralded by the area’s Labour politicians as a job creator, yet all Johnston can think about is what he has lost: “Before, the sun used to come around here and it was lovely.” The way residents have been treated is “disgusting”.

Johnston, who used to work for a chemical company in the city, worries what their lives will be like when the centre is switched on: the 36 sets of air blast coolers, needed to stop the chips inside overheating, most likely generate a constant drone, while regular testing of 60 back-up diesel generators, to prevent costly interruption to the flow of data, could produce harmful fumes. He already has to keep his bathroom window closed to stop the low hum from another facility on the rapidly expanding 46-acre datacentre campus that borders the small enclave of 27 houses. His wife, a nurse who works night shifts, can barely sleep already. He shakes his head in despair: “We just don’t know what’s going to happen.”

Johnston can talk about little else, but he feels no one in authority is listening. He’s invited councillors and MPs backing the development to visit his garden. But “they won’t come around here to see what they’ve done”.

This is the frontline of Britain’s datacentre boom, which is pitting residents against giant US tech firms seeking to build a network of facilities capable of providing the immense computing muscle for powerful AI models. There are 315 hyperscale datacentres waiting to connect to the national grid, which carries electricity from power plants to homes and businesses (hyperscale typically means facilities with at least 5,000 servers and 10,000 sq ft in size). Combined, these new centres will require 73GW of power, 28GW higher than the typical peak energy demand of 45GW on a cold winter’s day.

Wales is one of the main development hotspots outside London. It already has the largest datacentre capacity beyond the capital, with new hyperscale facilities planned for former industrial and urban sites. The Keir Starmer-led Labour government viewed this as an inevitable revolution, which the country must embrace or be left behind. It established five AI growth zones to push more datacentres through the planning system and speed up connections to the national grid. The new prime minister, Andy Burnham, has signalled little will change, turning down calls for a moratorium and insisting datacentres bring economic benefits. But anti-datacentre groups springing up in villages, towns and cities across the country see it rather differently: as the imposition of a resource-hungry, polluting industry that increases profits for often US big tech, with little tangible benefit to local people or the country as a whole.

Digital storage is nothing new. The first home PC users saved files on floppy discs and hard drives; businesses used their own server rooms in the 80s and 90s, then moved to off-site facilities as their needs grew. But the development of cloud computing services in the 2010s, such as Google Drive, and AI chatbots in the 2020s, like ChatGPT, led to even greater information and processing demands, which the tech industry insists can only be met by larger and larger datacentres. This has driven huge speculative bets on hyperscale hubs, which investors and tech firms hope will one day reap the rewards of an AI-led transformation of the economy. Globally, it is estimated spending on new facilities could reach $7tn by 2030 – more than double the cost of the New Deal, the Marshall Plan to rebuild Europe after the second world war and the Apollo space programme combined, even allowing for inflation.

The US is at the forefront of the boom, already hosting roughly half of the world’s hyperscale capacity. But the industry is facing a furious backlash, with three-quarters of Americans now opposed to datacentre development and blaming it for increasing energy bills, threatening water supplies in drought-hit areas and spewing out toxic fumes. More than 500 counties and municipalities have passed permanent or temporary datacentre bans.

Loudoun County in northern Virginia sits in what is known as Data Center Alley because it is home to more facilities than anywhere else in the world. Greg Pirio’s life was turned upside down when a gas-powered datacentre opened 150 metres from his home last year. “This neighbourhood was calm and peaceful, and it’s turned into this nightmare,” he says on a video call. “Our quality of life has been degraded so much.”

The noise from the centre’s eight gas turbines is “like being at an airport with all these engines roaring,” Pirio says. “It has given many of my neighbours headaches. It has kept children up at night.” Pirio, who used to work for the Voice of America radio station, has also developed health problems since it opened, including dry eyes and chest tightness. Once he had to leave his house because he was struggling to breathe: “I know whenever the diesel generators are on, because I feel it in my lungs.”

The UK has not yet seen the same level of opposition as the US. But anti-datacentre groups are gathering momentum. Residents in east London are waging a high-profile campaign against a new datacentre on Brick Lane, including protesting outside the offices of Angela Rayner, the communities secretary, who overturned a council decision blocking the scheme.

Seasoned campaigner Owen Espley, who works for environmental charity Global Action Plan, is helping scores of residents’ groups organise against planned datacentres. “This summer has been wild. Every other week a new group has popped up. Petitions are getting thousands of signatures in days,” he says. “Communities are in uproar, and most politicians are playing catch-up.”

Espley has been taken aback by the gulf between the government’s conviction that there is no alternative and ordinary people’s deepening understanding of the complex issues at stake. “There has been a real grassroots questioning of datacentres that connects British Bangladeshis in Brick Lane with Hertfordshire villagers and communities in Northumberland, who want good jobs to come back,” he says. “It is astounding.”

In fact, there are many alternative pathways to market-­led expansion – from tougher regulation to prioritising publicly-owned data infrastructure – which serve communal goals, such as the heart disease and dementia research powered by Bristol University’s new Isambard-AI facility. “First, we need strong standards for private datacentres,” Espley says. “Second, we need industrial strategy to deliver our own public computer, rather than an assumption that US big tech building and owning everything is the only way forward.”

On the deprived eastern edges of Cardiff, the early morning sunshine is warming a car park dotted with scraggly weeds. The perimeter fence is overgrown with brambles, with patches of singed, blackened grass from a recent wildfire visible just beyond. It looks abandoned but soon cars carrying doctors, nurses and other NHS staff start arriving. They are here to catch free buses to Cardiff’s Heath hospital, the largest in Wales.

Hundreds are said to use the park and ride every day, but it is under threat because Labour-controlled Cardiff council wants to sell the land to a property developer, Curtis Hall, to build a hyperscale data facility. The centre will use up to 150 megawatts (MW) of electricity a year, more than triple the demand of a city the size of Cardiff.

The development is one of the most controversial in Wales. Council meetings have been disrupted and more than 1,200 planning objections lodged. Last month, the new Plaid Cymru-led government ordered the council to put the application on hold while it considered whether to take the decision out of councillors’ hands.

Campaigners are determined to keep up the pressure on the Welsh government. Sue Elias, a retired librarian with seemingly endless reserves of energy, dashes back and forth reattaching her “Ditch the datacentre” banner to the car park fence and taping leaflets to the bus stop glass. “What is going to happen to all the people who use the park and ride? It’s bonkers,” she says.

Elias has leafleted the park and ride every Wednesday morning for six weeks. Today she is collecting signatures for an open letter to prove it is well used – so far she has added the names of 1,300 staff and patients. “I’ve not done anything like this before but I’m told I’m a natural,” she says with a mischievous smile, before breaking off to speak to staff arriving for the next bus.

Air quality assessments commissioned by the developer estimate the emissions will be within safe levels. Curtis Hall says the scheme cannot proceed without 100% compliance with all environmental and public health regulatory requirements.
