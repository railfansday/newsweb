#799. Scientists find a youth protein that helps aging brain cells act young again

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

A protein associated with youth may help aging brain cells regain some of their lost abilities. Scientists at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai have discovered that TIMP2 plays an important role in keeping the brain's immune cells healthy, offering new clues about how the brain changes with age.

In a study published in Nature Communications, researchers found that removing TIMP2 caused brain immune cells, known as microglia, to develop characteristics linked to aging and neurodegenerative disease. When the scientists restored TIMP2 in the bloodstream of older mice, these cells became better at removing cellular debris and showed fewer molecular signs of inflammation and other potentially harmful changes.

The discovery suggests that proteins associated with youth may help preserve the brain's natural defenses as it ages. It also identifies TIMP2 as a potentially important factor in maintaining healthy immune activity in the brain.

Why the Brain's Immune Cells Become Less Effective With Age

Getting older is the greatest known risk factor for Alzheimer's disease and other neurodegenerative disorders. However, scientists still do not fully understand the biological processes that make the aging brain increasingly vulnerable to these conditions.

One important piece of the puzzle involves microglia, specialized immune cells that serve as the brain's maintenance and defense system. They remove cellular waste, help maintain the networks through which neurons communicate, and respond when brain tissue is injured.

As the brain ages, these cells can lose some of their ability to perform these essential tasks. They may also shift into states that promote inflammation and interfere with normal brain function.

"TIMP2 facilitates healthy function for the brain's immune cells," said Joseph M. Castellano, PhD, Associate Professor of Neuroscience at the Ronald M. Loeb Center for Alzheimer's Disease and The Friedman Brain Institute at The Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, and corresponding author of the study. "By supporting the ability of microglia to clear debris and limit maladaptive responses, TIMP2 may help restore aspects of microglial function that become compromised with age. Since our previous work identified TIMP2 as a regulator of synaptic plasticity through the extracellular matrix, these findings suggest that this factor sits at the intersection of several processes that are critical for normal brain function."

Synaptic plasticity refers to the brain's ability to strengthen or weaken connections between neurons, a process essential for learning and memory. The extracellular matrix is a network of molecules surrounding cells that helps support and regulate their activity.

To investigate how TIMP2 affects brain immune cells, the research team studied several groups of mice representing different biological conditions.

The study included Brittany Hemmer, PhD, the first author, who was a graduate student in Castellano's laboratory during the research.

Some of the mice were genetically modified to lack TIMP2 throughout their bodies. In others, the researchers removed the protein specifically from either microglia or neurons. Comparing these models allowed the team to examine how TIMP2 influences immune cell behavior in both healthy and aging brains.

The scientists used several advanced techniques to study the changes. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing allowed them to examine patterns of gene activity in individual cell nuclei. They also used detailed imaging, functional tests, and a technique called in vivo microdialysis, which collects and measures substances from the fluid surrounding cells in living brain tissue.

The results revealed striking changes when TIMP2 was absent.

Microglia developed several features typically associated with aging and brain injury. Their cellular activation markers changed, their ability to remove debris declined, and they showed molecular signatures linked to cellular senescence, a biological state associated with aging and cellular stress.

The effects extended beyond the immune cells themselves. Using in vivo microdialysis, the researchers also detected higher levels of proteins associated with inflammation and stress in the extracellular environment surrounding brain cells.

Together, these findings indicated that TIMP2 helps prevent microglia from shifting toward potentially damaging states.

Restoring a Youth-Associated Protein Improves Aging Brain Cells

The researchers next explored whether supplying TIMP2 could counteract some of the changes observed with age.

They administered TIMP2 through injections to older mice, allowing the protein to circulate throughout the body. The treatment produced encouraging results.

Microglia became more effective at clearing cellular debris and showed a reduction in molecular markers associated with inflammatory states. In other words, restoring the youth-associated protein helped the aging immune cells recover some of the functions that normally deteriorate over time.

These improvements suggest that TIMP2 influences how microglia respond to conditions within the brain. By supporting their protective activities and limiting potentially harmful responses, the protein may help maintain a healthier environment for neurons.

The results also point to a possible biological connection between factors circulating in the body that are associated with youth and the activity of the brain's innate immune system. This system provides a first line of defense against injury and other threats.

A Potential New Direction for Brain Aging and Alzheimer's Research

The discovery adds to growing scientific interest in whether biological factors associated with youth can influence the aging process.

Because microglia help maintain brain health and can contribute to harmful inflammation when their behavior changes, finding ways to preserve their normal function could have important implications for understanding age-related neurological disorders.

"While additional studies are needed, this work provides new insight into how youth-associated factors influence pathways involved in brain aging and age-related neurological disorders that may ultimately inform therapeutic strategies," Dr. Castellano added.

However, the researchers caution that these findings come from experiments in mice. It remains unknown whether TIMP2 produces similar effects in humans or whether manipulating the protein could eventually become an effective treatment.

Additional studies will be necessary to determine whether TIMP2 itself, or the biological pathways it controls, can be targeted to reduce harmful changes associated with brain aging.

For now, the findings provide a promising new explanation for how a protein linked to youth helps maintain the brain's immune defenses, and how restoring it may recover some functions that weaken with age.

#798. Too much Overlap? Gary Neville’s podcast empire knows no bounds

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

The Overlap has gone daily, with Steven Gerrard and Joe Hart among the latest additions to an ever-growing family

Viewed in 2026, Nathan Barley, Chris Morris’s 2005 sitcom, shows remarkable prescience about the last two decades of metropolitan life, from smartphone addiction and digital derangement to sheer existential vapidity. When Barley’s character referred to his occupation as “self-facilitating media node”, Morris and co-writer Charlie Brooker were way ahead on the creator economy. What the pair failed to predict was ex-footballers joining the sector in droves. These days, if you are a former footballer without a podcast or YouTube channel, then did you ever really play the game?

Setting aside the Gary Lineker Goalhanger empire, manspreading its centrist-dad agenda into issues way beyond guffaws with Micah Richards and Alan Shearer, there is Gary Neville to rule them all. Manchesterism’s second-most influential practitioner has been on heavy manoeuvres. Unlike Lineker’s productions, the Overlap’s output continues to stick largely to football; a rugby podcast was mothballed in February, though its cricket equivalent continues.

Having snatched Wayne Rooney from the BBC in the summer for a Manchester United-based two-hander with the YouTuber Mark Goldbridge, last week the Overlap went daily, extending the output to 4pm Monday to Friday. An online launch had Jamie Carragher baiting Goldbridge, real name Brent Di Cesare. The former policeman shunned a “no comment” approach during his interrogation, biting back during an exchange that had Neville choking on his Huel in laughter. Clipped up, the send button pressed, job done.

The Overlap has gained a TV family dynamic, a chorus line of familiar characters. Neville and Carragher are mummy and daddy bear, football’s Richard and Judy. Jill Scott is the dotty aunt, Roy Keane the irascible uncle, smirking along with Ian Wright in picking over the latest gossip. It has been highly popular, occasionally agenda-setting, softening Keane’s image, even making a cultish figure of production hand Kiaran Saunders, supplier of biscuits and blueberries. Neville’s empire-building, though, is boundless, his tanks bulldozing into the already packed football podcast space, with newsletters to follow. The Overkill, anyone?

The family has been extended. Mondays have Steven Gerrard reviewing the weekend’s action with Becky Ives, this week offering a defence of Florian Wirtz: “Don’t give up on him.” Tuesdays have Kelly Cates and Ashley Young in tandem. Tim Sherwood and Jamie Redknapp are Wednesday’s pairing. Not the first time the Sky mainstays, one-time Tottenham midfield partners, have diversified. In the mid-2000s, they ran Icon, a lifestyle magazine initially only available to professional players. A combination of high-end advertising, onanistic photography and Redknapp’s purring interviews with A-list sportspeople fell flat, even after it became available in airport first-class lounges.

Two decades on, such cologne-fragrant exclusivity has been swapped for the ubiquity of Bury’s most famous son piling it high. The quarter-zipped ex-Spurs duo are not alone as 1990s footballers taking the Neville shilling. Mark Crossley and Duncan Ferguson make an unlikely – but promising – pairing. Previously most famous for being the only goalkeeper to save a Matthew Le Tissier penalty, Crossley has made a successful after-dinner speaking career of Brian Clough anecdotes and “young man” impressions. “Big Dunc” completely refused to speak to the media when a player but he was a hit within ITV’s World Cup 2026 coverage.

On last week’s edition Ferguson suggested Gianluigi Donnarumma might be over the hill. “He’s 27, actually,” said Crossley before a classic game’s gone discussion of video assistant referees ruining the sacred art of smashing into the opposition at goal-kicks. Just what the listener came for, eating perhaps into the Under The Cosh’s market share of real football man podcasts. Whether Ferguson’s appointment as Glentoran manager breaks up the band is unclear, though management never halted Robbie Savage’s many media ventures.

Two further goalkeepers have signed up. Last week’s Friday edition had Joe Hart and Peter Schmeichel entering the Manchester City charges chat. While admitting to mistakenly burying the lead when Lisandro Martínez was willingly dishing the dirt on Manchester United while serving as CBS’s Champions League interviewer in Madrid last Tuesday, Schmeichel gave Hart a far harder ride. “It’s tough to be a Manchester City person,” Hart said. “I love the club … we’re in it together.” Schmeichel stayed insistent on City’s owners: “They’re letting Manchester down, they don’t understand what this place is. They have built a lot of towers and homes and all that but they don’t get this place.”

“I’ve worked closely with Khaldoon [al-Mubarak] and he does get this place, that’s my reference point,” said a hesitant Hart. “He’s an incredible person.” More good, strong, clip-up content.

The City affair will ask former players to intersect with uncomfortable issues for some while yet. That Neville’s further business interests include building projects running parallel to the Abu Dhabi-funded developments in Manchester is another example. Overlaps have become unavoidable in more ways than one.

#797. All you need to know about the 2026 Ballon d'Or

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

The 2026 Ballon D'or ceremony will hand out 13 trophies to athletes and coaches across both the men's and women's game

Back for its 70th year, the Ballon d'Or ceremony will once again recognise the best footballing talent in the world.

Run by France Football magazine since 1956, it is widely viewed as the pinnacle of individual prizes for professional footballers worldwide.

As ever, the shortlists of contenders for the biggest awards of the night have been a hot topic of debate among football fans.

The 2026 Ballon d'Or ceremony will take place at 20:00 GMT on Monday, 26 October, at London's Palladium Theatre.

It is the first time the event will be held in London, a move which Uefa says is to pay tribute to the inaugural winner, England's Stanley Matthews in 1956.

The ceremony is traditionally held in France, and has taken place in Paris' Theatre du Chatelet since 2019.

A dual voting system is used to determine Ballon d'Or winners.

For the men's award, a finalised shortlist of 30 players is decided by a team of journalists from both France Football and L'Equipe newspaper and published a couple of months before the ceremony.

This shortlist is then ranked by 100 journalists from around the world, with their rankings combined to decide the winner.

Jurors are instructed to take three factors into account when ranking players. The Ballon d'Or regulations list the following in order of importance:

Individual performances, decisive and impressive character

You can read more about the process, and how it works for the women's award, in our Ballon d'Or voting explainer.

Harry Kane, Lamine Yamal and Kylian Mbappe are favourites to rank highly for the men's prize after their performances for club and country

A ceremony that lands in the months following a major tournament always provides extra intrigue - and tournaments don't come more major than the World Cup.

It means that jurors can take international performance into account when ranking the shortlisted players for the men's prize.

With Spain victorious in the 2026 World Cup, it could boost the rankings of the six Spanish internationals on the shortlist.

But outstanding individual performances for club and country, even without a return of silverware for the latter, also puts players like Harry Kane, Kylian Mbappe and Erling Haaland into contention.

England captain Kane scored 73 goals across club and international competitions in the 2025-26 season. While jurors are told to only take the most recent season into account when ranking players, Kane's astonishing 156 goals in 158 games for Bayern Munich since 2023 has lifted him to seventh in the German club's top scorer list. He has led the Bundesliga's scoring charts for three consecutive seasons, as well as being Europe's top overall club goalscorer for 2025-26.

Kane also led his country to their best World Cup finish since they won the tournament in 1966. England won the third-fourth place match to win a bronze medal and he finished fifth top scorer for the tournament.

The teenager has twice won the men's Kopa Trophy for best player under the age of 21, taking the prize at both the 2024 and 2025 awards. Having also placed second in the main rankings in 2025, he is once again up for contention in the both the main prize and under-21 categories. The 19-year-old was a key part of both Spain's World Cup-winning side and Barcelona's La Liga and Spanish Super Cup-winning season, despite missing a number of matches through injury.

Yamal's 24 goals and 18 assists across all competitions for Barcelona included eight in the Champions League, putting him ahead of Mbappe as the competition's highest-scoring under-19.

While France fell short of both making the World Cup final and winning the third-place match (losing 6-4 to England), captain Mbappe took home the tournament's Golden Boot after finding the net 10 times, becoming the contest's all-time top scorer in the process.

And despite a lack of silverware for Real Madrid, Mbappe scored 42 goals in 44 appearances for his club across all competitions in 2025-26.

While his years of complete Ballon d'Or dominance may be in the past, Messi has still put his name in the mix for a high ranking after captaining his Argentina side to second place in the World Cup.

Eight goals in the competition put him in second place behind Mbappe on the top scorers list, with both players joint-top for assists with four each.

And with 29 goals in 32 appearances helping Inter Miami to their current second-place position in 2026 (with the most recent season running from February to December), individual and club performance are both still a factor for the footballing legend.

The 2024 Ballon d'Or winner has had consecutive seasons marred by injury since taking home the trophy. Ruled out for 220 days for Manchester City after tearing his anterior cruciate ligament during the 2024-25 campaign, and a further few months out in the 2025-26 season with knee problems, the Spain captain has not had nearly as much game time as most of his fellow nominees.

However, leading your side to a Word Cup win could carry a fair amount of voting heft with the jurors. Rodri was also named the best player of the tournament. It is unlikely to be enough to seal the main prize, but it has been a positive year for him.

Manchester City's star striker helped guide his country to new footballing heights, scoring seven goals to finish as the tournament's joint-third top scorer as Norway reached their first ever World Cup quarter-finals.

Haaland was also the Premier League's top scorer for the 2025-26 season with 27 goals, amid a total of 38 club goals across all competitions - including becoming the fastest player to reach 50 Champions League goals, taking just 49 games to do so.

Barcelona have dominated the women's Ballon d'Or in recent years - but could that change in 2026?

With no major international competitions in the women's game during the current voting period, jurors can still look to European qualifying matches as well as club performance when allocating their votes.

The current Ballon d'Or holder Aitana Bonmati has been sidelined with injury for the majority of the season following her win, meaning she will not be able to defend her title for a record fourth time.

When it comes to club and success, it's hard to ask much more of Alexia Putellas than captaining Barcelona to a continental quadruple in 2025-26, winning the Liga F, Cope de la Reina, Spanish Super Cup and Champions League.

She became Barcelona's joint-second top scorer of all time after scoring two goals in the Champions League semi-final in May, and earned the player of the tournament award in the process.

The two-time winner could equal her former team-mate Bonmati's record of three awards, having also been a key part of Spain's Nations League-winning side.

The WSL top scorer for a third consecutive season, Khadija shaw appears lightyears ahead of her peers when it comes to goalscoring.

Her 21 league goals - eight goals more than closest rival Alessia Russo - and four assists helped Manchester City to their first title in a decade, topped off with two goals in the FA Cup final to seal the domestic double.

The PFA and FWA women's footballer of the year also remains Jamaica's all-time top goalscorer as she captains them through their 2027 World Cup qualification matches.

Despite two months rules out with injury, Ewa Pajor's strong showing during Barcelona's trophy-laden 2025-26 campaign keep her in contention for this year's Ballon d'Or.

Pajor finished top scorer in the Champions League with 11 goals, leaving her joint-second on the all-time list with former team-mate Putellas. She also scored 16 goals in Liga F to finish second top scorer of the season.

Lyon secured a domestic treble in 2025-26 thanks in no small part to Melchie Dumornay, winning the league title as well as the Coupe de France and Coupe de la Ligue.

The France midfielder scored 22 goals and provided 12 assists overall. Despite losing to quadruple-winning Barcelona in the final, Dumornay scored five Champions League goals and was named in the team of the tournament.

The incredible beauty of Antarctica is evident from space .

Antarctica is a magnificent sight, and that beauty is visible all the way from space.

Orbiting planet Earth, the European Space Agency's (ESA) Copernicus Sentinel-3 mission captured a close-up of West Antarctica.

Winter is ending in the Southern Hemisphere, meaning this is the peak of icy weather and sea ice in the Arctic.

In this image snapped by Sentinel-3, you can see a massive swath of ice which includes Elephant Island on the left-hand side. An ice-covered island off the Antarctic coast, Elephant Island floats in the Southern Ocean. It got its name both from the island's shape, which some liken to that of an elephant's head, and the fact that the island is home to a community of elephant seals.

While a seal-inhabited island on the left floats near the Antarctic coast, across the water is a vast, frozen landscape. In just this snapshot, you can see about between 746 and 1,056 miles (1,200 to 1,700 kilometers) of the icy continent.

This image was captured on Sept. 30, 2026. In the Southern Hemisphere, that was just past the end of winter. The absolute peak for sea ice in Antarctica fell just a couple weeks before, on Sept. 14, according to the National Snow and Ice Data Center, ESA reported.

So in this one snapshot, we can see a near-maximum for sea ice in the Antarctic, spanning hundreds of miles. In observing our own planet from space, we are able to peer across vast landscapes, capturing important observational data. This is especially critical for the Antarctic as, with the progression of climate change, this sea ice continues to dwindle, adding to sea level rise among other serious consequences across the planet.

#795. Tesla cancels Cybertruck launch in Australia and New Zealand

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Tesla's Cybertruck will no longer be released in Australia and New Zealand. The company announced its cancellation in these regions in an email to customers on Friday.

"After careful consideration, plans to produce the Cybertruck for Australia and New Zealand have been discontinued," Tesla wrote. "As a result, we're unable to fulfil your reservation and it will be cancelled."

Drivers in Australia and New Zealand had been able to preorder Tesla's Cybertruck since it was initially unveiled in 2019. At the time, the company planned to start production in late 2021, eight years after CEO Elon Musk first teased the Cybertruck in 2013.

Now five years past the Cybertruck's previous manufacturing date, it seems that Australian and Kiwi customers' patient waiting has not borne the results they were promised. The Cybertruck has been removed from both Tesla's Australian and New Zealand websites, which now only offer the Model 3, Model Y and preorders for the Roadster.

Those eager to secure a Cybertruck had been required to pay a preorder deposit of $AU/NZ150. Fortunately, Tesla has stated that all such payments will be refunded. The company has also said that customers whose preorders were cancelled will be given a one-year subscription to Tesla's Full Self-Driving (Supervised) system — provided they buy a new Model 3 or Model Y instead.

(Despite its name, the Full Self-Driving (Supervised) system does not enable Tesla's electric vehicles to autonomously drive themselves. Rather, it is a driving assistance feature that offers some automation, and still requires a driver to actively pay attention and control the car.)

While some enthusiasts have been disappointed by the cancellation of their Cybertruck preorders, many other EV users are celebrating that it won't make it onto Australia and New Zealand's roads. Even users of the r/TeslaAustralia subreddit have shed few tears over the Cybertruck's cancelled plans in the country.

The Cybertruck has been widely derided as ugly since its introduction, with people likening it to everything from a child's drawing to low-poly video game graphics. Many have also been unimpressed by negative reports on the Cybertruck's build quality, the vehicle having been subject to multiple recalls in the U.S. where it is already available. Now drivers in Australia and New Zealand won't have to worry about it as well.

Using NASA’s SPHEREx spacecraft, astronomers have studied a host of "failed stars," also known as brown dwarfs.

These strange cosmic bodies straddle the line between planets and stars, possessing a mass between 13 and 80 times that of Jupiter (0.013 to 0.08 times the mass of the sun). They get the nickname failed stars because they form like stars from collapsing clouds of gas and dust but fail to gather enough mass to trigger the fusion of hydrogen to helium in their cores, the process that powers actual stars.

This formation process also means that brown dwarfs are cosmic loners, wandering the darkness of space alone, bereft of the light of a parent star. That description might sound like lyrics from a Type O Negative song ("Black No. 1," anybody?). And that's apt, according to the leader of the new study, which reveals that, chemically speaking, the atmospheres of these dark and cloudy worlds are not unlike those of our solar system's gas giant planets.

"They’re kind of goth," Zafar Rustamkulov, of the California Institute of Technology (Caltech), said in a statement. "Unlike exoplanets, free-floating brown dwarfs are completely independent celestial objects that will fade into eternity alone. We’re still learning how complex they are."

Rustamkulov added that, from Earth orbit, SPHEREx — short for Spectro-Photometer for the History of the Universe, Epoch of Reionization, and Ices Explorer — sees wavelengths of light that are nigh-impossible to see with telescopes on the ground because water in Earth’s atmosphere absorbs them. That is important for bringing brown dwarfs into the light.

"We are picking up light from the deep, red clouds of brown dwarfs all over the sky," he said.

Brown dwarfs have been somewhat mysterious since their discovery in the 1990s, due to the fact that the small amount of nuclear fusion that occurs within them causes them to emit no more than a faint glow. That means that our understanding of their makeup, their storms, and their evolution really only comes from theory.

To help lift this observational darkness, the team turned to data from SPHEREx. They studied 37 nearby brown dwarfs in a spectrum of light including 102 wavelengths, from the deepest red our eyes can see to the invisible infrared light. These local failed stars spanned the full temperature range for brown dwarfs — from 4,000 degrees Fahrenheit to minus 10 degrees Fahrenheit (2,200 to minus 20 degrees Celsius).

Considering that the temperature of the sun is about 10,000 degrees Fahrenheit (5,500 degrees Celsius), you could definitely say brown dwarfs run cold.

The spectra of brown dwarfs seen by SPHEREx allowed the team to determine that their atmospheres are chemically rich in water, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide and methane.

"We’re seeing the signatures of these molecules and how they change from object to object across the entire temperature regime," said team member J. Davy Kirkpatrick, also of Caltech. "Our paper concentrated on just three dozen, but we have thousands more that we are in the process of analyzing.

"I really want to see what bounds the universe places on the variety of brown dwarfs."

Brown dwarfs have been routinely observed by other telescopes, including NASA's James Webb Space Telescope and the agency's now-retired Spitzer Space Telescope, but the wide wavelength coverage of SPHEREx has made it particularly useful in observing these failed stars. That's good news for astronomers waiting for change in the atmospheres of brown dwarfs as they age and grow cold.

The researchers found that some brown dwarfs observed by SPHEREx are at a stage of extreme change in which their clouds are clearing, creating atmospheres rich in methane.

The team may have discovered significant new details about brown dwarfs, but they are still on the proverbial mystery street when it comes to these strange celestial objects,

"The state-of-the-art models are capturing the general chemical trend, but when it comes to these cloudy transitions, the models are struggling to match the data," said Rustamkulov. "No two brown dwarfs are alike. Even at the same temperature, their spectra look quite distinct."

"This journey has turned many of us into accidental meteorologists. We know how hard it is to predict weather on our own planet, and we realize it's going to be just as challenging to explain the phenomena we see in these bizarre, cold objects," Kirkpatrick said. "The findings are a call to action to explore even more of these dark worlds."

As astronomers resond to that call to action, I will ponder how I could have worked Bauhaus' Goth classic "Bela Lugosi is Dead" into this article.

The team's research was published on Sept. 28 in The Astrophysical Journal.

#785. 125 years of Thomas Mann's 'Buddenbrooks'

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

This over 1,000-page masterpiece offers a panoramic view of society in the 19th century and exposes the collapse of a merchant family: unrecognized sons, disillusioned daughters and the loss of middle-class ideals.

Who would have imagined it in the beginning? A 26-year-old man without a high school diploma, but greatly ambitious, writes a two-pound book with a melancholy undertone. "I'm not exactly delighted," his publisher initially responded. But he ended up publishing the novel anyway, more out of goodwill than conviction. And the rest is history.

For many, Thomas Mann's Buddenbrooks is considered THE German social novel. It is indeed a classic, and one that garnered the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1929. Global fame, an international best-seller, and required reading for many German students. Anyone who mentions Thomas Mann thinks of Buddenbrooks in the same breath. Why is that?

Does the explanation lie in the autobiographical connections between the work and the author?

Thomas Mann was born in Lübeck to a merchant family, but his artistic aspirations did not fit in well in the middle-class mainstream. This struggle inspired Mann's first work. The inability of this sensitive bohemian to carry on the family's time-honored business after the death of his father was sniffed at and derided.

To view this video please enable JavaScript, and consider upgrading to a web browser that supports HTML5 video

Reflecting the situation of his novel's character Hanno, Mann had a privileged life in a wealthy family, but one that was at risk of collapse. The aspiring novelist also had to withstand disappointing the expectations of others. He was forced to defend his dream of an artistic life. Yet as much as he was inspired by real life, he forcefully altered these biographical details to place his unique literary mark on his writing.

But does that really matter in light of people who choose not to make the distinction between an author's life and his work? Even though the name "Lübeck" does not appear in the novel, citizens there felt markedly insulted after the publication of Buddenbrooks in 1901.

That likely lies in the fact that the image the author portrayed was not one of an idyllic northern German city, but one of a place that had difficulty blending tradition with modernity.

This contradiction is also seen in each of the figures in the novel. Nothing is like it appears. Mann lays down this foundation of this skepticism from the very beginning, in a scene taking place on a very ordinary Thursday in October 1835. The Buddenbrook family comes together for their ordinary late lunch.

"There they all sat, on heavy, high-backed chairs, consuming good heavy food from good silver plates, drinking full-bodied wines and expressing their views freely on all subjects. When they began to talk shop, they slipped unconsciously more and more into dialect, and used the clumsy but comfortable idioms that seemed to embody to them the business efficiency and the easy well-being of their community. Sometimes they even used an over-drawn pronunciation by way of making fun of themselves and each other, and relished their clipped phrases and exaggerated vowels with the same heartiness they did their food."

There sits the leader of the family company, Johann Buddenbrook the Elder, and his wife, his son and counsel Jean, along with his family. The grandchildren are also there: Tony the dreamer, the quarrelsome rebel Christian, and Thomas, the eldest grandchild.

It is Thomas who is supposed to later take on the responsibility of continuing the family tradition, regardless of the cost. Mann weaves his narrative work starting with this main family, and then across several generations.

But appearances in the family are deceptive. The reader senses it's all headed toward a downfall. The new house is taken over from a family that was once as illustrious as the Buddenbrooks, but faced financial ruin and had to leave.

Johann and his family aim to make a better go of it. They make decisions on what is demanded economically, but fail — financially, interpersonally, and mentally.

Tony and Thomas end up falling in love with people not befitting their social standing, but do not have the courage to stand up to their parents. They enter marriages of convenience, yet it is these partnerships that paradoxically set disaster in motion.

From an early age, Thomas Mann was influenced by Schopenhauer's pessimistic notion of the will being a blind force at work in the world. Thus, there is likewise no escape for the Buddenbrooks. They speak of emotions and morality, yet it is money and economics that steer the ship. Failure, decline and even death seem to be the only viable outcome.

Mann also punctuates this hopelessness in a stylistic manner, by consistently repeating entire passages word for word, or evoking motifs such as the family registry or the estate in all their various forms.

The extent of this demise appears linguistically molded and exaggerated. The Buddenbrooks, this proud north German family of refinement, actually speaks the vernacular studded with empty French expressions. "Je, den Düwel ook, c'est la question, ma très chère demoiselle!"

The lowly servants, the courtesans, and the demi-monde: all of them speak the language of the street, raw and unpolished. In a virtuoso manner, Thomas Mann orchestrated this mass of voices into a panorama of society in the 19th century. The snobbishness on the part of the Buddenbrooks thus appears as a relic of a long lost time. It's a symbol of their inability to adapt to change and place their faith in progress.

Yet the fact that this extensive work spanning several generations does not get weighed down by aesthetic excess is purely due to Mann's brilliance as an author. He is able to bring every one of his numerous characters to life in words. Life that is full of complexity, contradictions and ambiguities. Some of these characters accompany the reader page for page; others appear only briefly.

And yet one begins to contemplate how someone like Grünlich could become a dodgy, unscrupulous husband and catches oneself wanting to chase the fearful Hanno out of the music conservatory and yell to him: "Go, throw yourself into life! You are not a failure." It is precisely this empathy that spurs one on to read through the night and prompts one to chuckle at all the intricate sentences and philosophical digressions.

Thomas Mann was born into a privileged Lübeck merchant family in 1875. He died in Zurich, Switzerland in 1955. "I am a townsman, a citizen, a child and a great-grandchild of German bourgeois society," the author once wrote about himself. Mann received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1929 for his debut novel, Buddenbrooks. He also garnered numerous other prizes and honors for his extensive work, including Death in Venice (1911), The Magic Mountain (1924), and the tetralogy Joseph and His Brothers (1933-1943). He emigrated to Switzerland in 1933, and to the US in 1938. From there, he made his famous radio commentaries in which he agitated against the Nazis. He returned to Switzerland in 1952.

The moon is shrinking, and scientists are narrowing in on exactly how much our natural satellite has dwindled over the years.

The moon is thought to have formed from a violent collision between a massive object and our young Earth roughly 4.5 billion years ago, and its interior is still gradually cooling off from this ancient collision. As it cools, the moon contracts, leading to a long, slow shrinking process. Now, in a new study, scientists think that they have figured out how much smaller the moon is actually getting.

In this new study, scientists analyzing images snapped by the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter Camera (LROC) have spotted over 10,000 young faults, or cracks across the moon's surface. This helped them to figure out how much the moon has contracted over time, and they now estimate that the moon's radius has decreased by about 148 feet (45 meters) over the past 400 to 800 million years.

"The number of these young faults revealed in LROC images is much greater than I ever imagined," study lead Thomas Watters, a geologist and scientist emeritus at the Center for Earth and Planetary Studies at the Smithsonian's National Air and Space Museum, said in a statement. "As the interior of the moon continues to cool and contract, it is expected that the growth of existing faults and formation of new faults continues today."

With these LROC observations, researchers in this study saw a variety of different types of cracks and landforms across the lunar surface. This included lobate scarps, or long, curved structures that form when the moon's surface contracts, cracks and is pushed upwards; and small mare ridges, or narrow "wrinkles" in the lunar surface, across the dark plains on the moon.

Tectonic activity on the moon looks a lot different than it does on Earth. On our own planet, there are about 15 major tectonic plates. On the moon, there are no tectonic plates at all. Instead, the moon has a single-plate body meaning its outer shell is a single piece only broken by faults or other such cracks created by the moon contracting. This means that instead of faults or cracks appearing at tectonic plate boundaries, they are instead scattered all over the moon.

By mapping these thousands of different features, all created from the moon cooling and contracting over time, the researchers are piecing together lunar history. In addition to finding the extent to which the moon has shrunk over time, they also found that some areas of the moon are covered in many more faults than other areas, and the areas with fewer cracks and faults have thicker moon crust.

This led them to conclude that not only are these areas with thicker crust more stable, but they suggest that a great deal of the widespread cracks in the lunar surface were created later in the moon's history as, in its earlier years, its thick crust would have insulated the moon's warm interior and prevented cooling, contracting and cracking.

This work was described in a study published Oct. 14 in The Planetary Science Journal.
