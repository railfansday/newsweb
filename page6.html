#3727. Goldman Sachs puts $500M into small-business AI with four AI labs

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Goldman Sachs chief executive David Solomon said there is very little that needs frontier models. Most AI-using owners in Goldman’s survey spend under $100 a month on it.

The Goldman Sachs Tower in Jersey City, New Jersey, in 2006

Goldman Sachs is putting $500M into the next phase of its 10,000 Small Businesses programme, including a new AI institute built with Anthropic, Google, Meta and OpenAI. At the launch, its chief executive said there is very little that needs the frontier models those partners build.

David Solomon spoke at the bank’s New York headquarters on Thursday, Nick Lichtenberg reported for Fortune, which was in the audience.

“Frontier models and how frontier models develop is going to be a very, very interesting thing. But there’s very little that we do where we need frontier models.” David Solomon, Goldman Sachs, as reported by Fortune

Solomon said frontier models matter for cybersecurity, defence and health care, such as drug discovery. Those debates are “really separate from using the base tools that exist to support your businesses”, he said. In Europe, AI leaders are arguing over whether the continent should even build frontier models.

The $500M will fund education, access to capital and support for another 10,000 businesses, Goldman said in its announcement. The new Small Business AI Institute will teach owners about AI, publish research on how they use it, and use AI to widen access to capital.

Each partner brings its own piece. Anthropic will run a learning series, hands-on workshops for alumni and joint research. Google will offer its Grow with Google AI Professional certificate to almost 20,000 owners and employees, with three months of Google AI Pro at no cost. Meta will provide tools and training through Muse for Small Business. OpenAI will hold workshops with the programme’s community college partners.

Nearly 20,000 entrepreneurs have completed the programme since it started in 2010, under an earlier $1.25bn commitment.

Solomon’s advice to owners was to take their time, according to Fortune.

“At the moment, this is an expensive technology. I don’t think that you have to look at the world and say, ‘I’ve got to be the best at this; I have to have this figured out in the next three weeks.’” David Solomon

He said the return should come from productivity in areas such as sales, customer experience, manufacturing and procurement. Goldman’s own chief information officer, Marco Argenti, has said AI turns developers into managers of their own AI agents.

A Goldman report released with the announcement shows most owners spending little so far. Babson College surveyed programme alumni in August and received 2,473 responses. Goldman says they may not represent all alumni.

Of those owners, 86% use AI in their business, up from 56% in 2024. Among AI users, 73% spend less than $100 a month, and 16% use only free tools. Only 18% use agentic AI, and 15% say it is fully built into their core operations. Of the owners using AI, 84% report no change to their workforce because of it.

For millions of families, caregiving is not just a personal responsibility; it is a growing affordability challenge. And the issue is emerging in the 2026 midterm elections.

Some 59 million Americans provide care for an adult family member, neighbor or friend, according to a recent report from AARP, a nonprofit, nonpartisan organization that advocates for people age 50 and older.

Many of those caregivers face mounting financial pressures as they balance the cost of helping loved ones with their own household expenses, jobs and retirement security. A new study from Edward Jones found that Americans caring for aging family members spend an average of $8,737 per year out of pocket and that their personal retirement savings and careers bear the brunt.

Results of a new AARP survey show that in 43 competitive congressional districts, most of the likely voters age 50 and older said they're more likely to vote for candidates who back policies that support unpaid caregivers helping loved ones. Because voters 50 and up comprise one of the most important voting blocs, the survey shows that support could make a difference in closely contested races.

Among those older surveyed voters, 77% said they would be more likely to vote for a candidate who advocated for a tax credit for unpaid family caregivers who help pay for a loved one's care. Nearly as many, 75%, said they would be more likely to vote for a candidate who advocated for support for unpaid family caregivers helping loved ones remain in their homes.

Fabrizio Ward and Impact Research conducted the survey for AARP, interviewing 1,368 likely voters age 18 and older, including 848 voters age 50 and older, from Oct. 1-5. The margin of error for the 1,368 sample was plus or minus 2.7 percentage points. The data was weighted to represent the voting population of the targeted congressional districts nationwide.

Voters ages 50 and above "are going to be the deciders in the election," said Matt Hogan, a partner at Impact Research, a public opinion research and consulting firm. "So for candidates looking to win over this critical voting bloc, a focus on helping caregivers would go a long way in helping them make inroads."

"After we set aside the necessities, leaving out medication [and] doctor's appointments, she only has around $400 to cover food," Graves said.

The financial impact of caregiving can extend well beyond immediate out-of-pocket expenses.

Caregivers are more likely than non-caregivers to have fewer financial assets and report debt problems, according to the Employee Benefit Research Institute's 2026 Retirement Confidence Survey. About one-third, 34%, of caregivers reported having less than $10,000 in savings and investments, compared with 25% of non-caregivers. More than two-thirds, 69%, of caregivers said debt is a problem, compared with 57% of non-caregivers.

These financial pressures can make it harder for caregivers to build savings, manage household expenses and prepare for retirement.

The challenges are especially significant for Americans in the so-called sandwich generation, who may support aging parents while also raising children or managing other family responsibilities, said Keesha Middlemass, a political science professor at Howard University.
