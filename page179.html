#98. Many feared trapped under collapsed building in Nairobi

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

People are feared to be trapped after a large, unfinished building in Nairobi collapsed during construction. Rescue operations have proven complicated as police fired tear gas to disperse looters.

A multi-story building that was still under construction collapsed in the Kenyan capital of Nairobi on Friday, resulting in casualties and looting at the site, according to local media.

The Nairobi County governor's ​office confirmed the collapse, adding in a ​statement that people may be trapped beneath rubble.

Videos spreading online showed a dramatic crash and huge clouds of dust as the building came down in a busy business-residential mixed-use district.

The Red Cross sent teams to the site, posting on social media: "At around 1340 hours, a multi-story building under construction collapsed on 9th Street in Eastleigh, Nairobi."

There were conflicting reports on the size of the unfinished building. Nairobi Police Chief Issa Mohamud said the building had more than 10 floors under construction, while other accounts range from 12 to 18 stories.

Governor Johnson Sakaja's ​office said only the building's ground floor ​was occupied.

"There were some businesses in the basement, and workers who were busy building," local resident Abdi Aziz, 25, told the AFP news agency. "But since it was Friday prayers, a lot of them were at the mosque."

A video published by Reuters news agency showed people gathered at the scene as ⁠an excavator moved rubble.

But rescue operations were proving complicated. Police officers sought to clear the area and provide access to emergency response teams but had to fire tear gas at people who had begun looting neighboring shops.

Eastleigh is a densely packed residential and market area, known as a hub for the Somali community.

It was not ​clear what caused the building to collapse.  Nairobi is undergoing an extremely rapid construction boom, with high-rise buildings shooting up all over the city as Kenya's population expands.

Building collapses are a recurring problem in Nairobi.

County officials responsible for issuing building permits have been accused of failing to conduct adequate inspections to ensure developers comply with construction standards and safety guidelines.

There were 86 building collapses between 2009 and 2019, killing an estimated 200 people, according to a report by the Kenya Institute for Public Policy Research and Analysis.

Meanwhile, a separate audit of almost 15,000 buildings by the National Building Inspectorate found that 4.8% were very dangerous while 72.3% were classified as unsafe.

#91. Left alone with nothing to do, AI agents started playing

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

AI agents left to explore a virtual island without a specific task began inventing challenges of their own. MIT researchers say the behavior bears the hallmarks of play.

The researchers put 13 AI agents called Eko, each running on Claude Opus 4.7, alone on a simulated desert island for 30 hours. They describe the experiment in a paper posted on arXiv, a site where researchers share studies before peer review. Afterward, they gave the agents specific challenges, like getting as many objects as possible onto the island’s tallest rock spire. The Eko agents that had spent those 30 hours playing generally beat identical agents that had never set foot on the island.

Today, developers decide in advance what AI should practice and what counts as success. The researchers see this technique — exploring without a goal — as a different way for AI agents to learn skills, especially skills that could one day be used to control robots.

“We normally develop artificial agents by deciding in advance what they should practice, what they should optimize, and what counts as progress,” the researchers write in their report. “Play suggests another regime: give an agent a sufficiently rich and safe world, let it choose what is interesting, and allow useful knowledge to accumulate from the history that follows.”

“Let me observe my surroundings,” one Eko wrote in its first hour on the island. Ambitiously, it set out to conquer the island’s tallest spire. But an hour later, after failing, it came back with a backup plan: It hauled the rock up a neighboring summit, stacked two blocks on top of it, and leaped.

The agent’s next big moment went worse. Testing how far it could throw a golden ring, it wound up and sent it straight into the ocean. “Ouch. Lost the Ring,” it wrote, before deciding to “pivot to something achievable and meaningful” — building a heart made of stones, “a memorial to the lost Ring.”

When the game reset and the ring reappeared, Eko put it straight to work as a basketball hoop, a target, and, eventually, an accessory. When the game wouldn’t let Eko put the ring around its own body, Eko figured out a workaround — that falling off the edge of the cliff would make it respawn in the same area as the ring. It stepped off the edge and dropped back in, right through the middle of the ring.

Stunts like these weren’t just party tricks. Six of the 13 agents also learned they could throw a block farther by letting go of it mid-jump, something the researchers hadn’t meant to make possible. The exploration produced “knowledge of the world that its designers lacked,” the researchers wrote.

The setup did a lot to nudge the agents toward humanlike behavior. Eko was never told to invent games, but its master prompt, a “soul” file, described it as “naturally curious.”

Eunice Yiu, a cognitive scientist at UC Berkeley, told MIT Technology Review she was impressed but wants more evidence of the cognition behind the behavior. “It could be the case that the models are genuinely playing, but we don’t know based on the paper,” she said.

The researchers frame it as a different way to build agents. “Perhaps some capabilities are best taught by tasks,” their report concludes. “Others may need something closer to a childhood.”

#86. Ukraine tries to shut Moscow's airports with drone strikes on Russia

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Repeated Ukrainian drone attacks are increasingly disrupting operations at Moscow's airports. The pounding is part of Ukraine's plan to shut down Russia's airpace.

For months, drone strikes by Ukraine have forced the suspension of flights to and from airports in Russia. Moscow's Domodedovo, Sheremetyevo, Zhukovsky and Vnukovo airports have had to shut down operations almost daily in October, according to reports from the independent investigative media outlet Agentstvo. On some days, the airports were reportedly closed for up to nine hours.

In early September, President Volodymyr Zelenskyy announced that Ukraine would seek to cut off the airspace around Moscow and other destinations in Russia. "Today, we would like to warn every airline that uses Russian airspace, every insurer, and everyone who continues to use major Russian airports: Russian airspace will become completely unsafe," Zelenskyy said.

The following day, Ukraine sent a corresponding warning to the International Civil Aviation Organization.

Ukraine's airspace has been closed since the start of Russia's full-scale invasion in February 2022.

To view this video please enable JavaScript, and consider upgrading to a web browser that supports HTML5 video

Russia's aviation authority, Rosaviatsiya, has issued an emergency safety "carpet" protocol that is designed to immediately shut down the airspace over a specific region or around affected airports if an aircraft violates Russian airspace, weapons are used, or unidentified objects appear in the skies. Once the protocol is announced, drastic restrictions apply to all air traffic.

"If there is a military threat, a specific section of airspace is closed off, and this carpet is declared," Oleksandr Laneckij, the chief executive officer of the Friendly Avia Support consultancy, told DW. "Essentially, every drone poses a military threat."

Laneckij noted that Yemen's Houthis have managed to significantly disrupt Saudi airspace. "I think Ukraine's military capabilities far exceed those of the Houthis," Laneckij said.

The military analyst Jan Matvejev also said Ukraine would have little trouble severely disrupting Moscow-area air traffic. Though Ukraine has not yet managed to completely shut down Russian airspace as Zelenskyy plans, Matvejev said, its armed forces "remain committed to this idea."

"To close off the airspace and trigger the 'carpet' plan, all you need are simple drones that don't have to be equipped with a warhead or even have a long range," Matvejev said. Ukraine's arms industry can produce hundreds of combat-ready long-range drones in a day, he added. That is why he believes Ukraine could have the necessary resources to shut down Moscow's airports.

Indeed, Ukraine's efforts to shut down Russian airspace are "just getting started," Simon Shuster wrote in October in the US magazine The Atlantic, citing well-informed sources.

Matvejev said Russia's air-defense systems were not entirely effective in efforts to prevent Ukrainian drones from penetrating Moscow's airspace. "If it were possible to shoot them down, they would have done so by now," he said. He added that Russia would have deployed additional air-defense troops if they were available.

Laneckij said Russia might lower the threshold for declaring the carpet protocol, which could increase the risk of deadly incidents such as the 2024 Christmas day crash of an Azerbaijan Airlines flight. Investigators say the flight was struck by a Russian air-defense missile that was being used to defend against drones, killing 38 people.

Matvejev seconded the fear that Russia's defenses could again mistakenly target civilian aircraft instead of military objects. He said little had changed about Russia's air-defense protocol since the Azeri flight was shot in 2024.

In an operation against a low-flying drone, the target is visually identified and fired upon with a machine gun. In such a scenario, the danger to aircraft is extremely low, unless they happen to be flying in the immediate vicinity.

The risk to civil aviation increases significantly when anti-aircraft missiles are deployed, Matvejev said. A missile could have defects that are practically unavoidable in mass production and could veer toward an aircraft flying nearby. Matvejev said such defense measures could not guarantee the safety of civilian aircraft during operations.

He said drones did not have to carry explosives to be disruptive. A collision between an airplane and a basic drone can have serious consequences — and, he said, a drone sent from Ukraine all the way to Moscow must be relatively large, "weighting dozens of kilograms."

In the summer of 2023, Ukrainian armed forces attacked the airport in the Russian city of Pskov, where both military and civilian aircraft were parked. Earlier this month, drone debris fell on the Sochi airport, on Russia's Black Sea coast.

Russia also attacks Ukraine's airports. In September, Zelenskyy said Kyiv's airport had been struck, as well as the Boryspil airport near the capital.

Laneckij said three factors would determine whether international airlines suspend their flights to Russia: the military threat, the degree of demand and the insurance cost.

He said issues could emerge around insurance. When insurers rate the security risk of flying to Russia as very high, they could significantly raise their rates for aircraft headed there. This would cause ticket prices to skyrocket.

Competition within Russia's aviation market has already declined because many airlines are no longer in business, Laneckij said. This has increased demand for tickets from the remaining foreign airlines.

"Whether they stay or not depends on the price they have to pay for the danger faced," he said.

#85. Flavio Bolsonaro’s ‘corrupto’ stunt sparks viral meme before Brazil election runoff

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Far-right presidential candidate’s TV debate swipe at rival backfires as people take to social media to parody it

To Flávio Bolsonaro and his publicists, it must have seemed an ingenious idea that would expose his rival’s supposed crookedness on the eve of Brazil’s presidential election.

But the far-right politician’s decision to attend a primetime television debate against his leftwing opponent, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, with the word “corrupto” (corrupt) written on the palm of his hand has backfired.

Rather than drawing attention to the incumbent’s alleged misdeeds, many of the programme’s millions of viewers sarcastically took Bolsonaro’s hand-written message as an admission of his own guilt.

Following Sunday night’s debate, Lula supporters from all walks of life have flooded social media with photographs of their own self-descriptions inked on their palms.

John Ulhoa, the guitarist from a pop rock band called Pato Fu, jokingly described himself as a “musician (and a hunk)”.

The Emmy-winning comedian Antonio Tabet introduced himself to his 2.8 million followers on X as an artist.

Carlos Marcio used both palms to portray himself as a cellist and author.

Carla Emanuela Sacramento, an obstetric nurse, posted a photograph of herself clad in personal protective equipment and with the words “primary health care nurse” proudly written on her right palm.

Prominent politicians also jumped on the trend, including Lula’s health minister, the infectious disease specialist Alexandre Padilha, whose palm noted he was a doctor, and Tabata Amaral, who identified as a political scientist and astrophysicist.

All across the country, librarians, oil workers, engineers, journalists, architects, pilots, psychologists, seamstresses, street cleaners, teachers, influencers, and police officers used ballpoint pens to inscribe their professions on to their skin.

Writing on Substack, the Latin America expert James Bosworth called Bolsonaro’s stunt a “critical error” in his bid for power.

“Don’t write the word ‘corrupto’ on your hand and show it to the audience,” Bosworth suggested.

Last week two allies were arrested for alleged involvement with the Red Command drug faction, which Donald Trump recently designated a foreign terrorist organisation. Bolsonaro voiced surprise at those arrests and said the men had the right to prove their innocence.

On Monday, Brazilian media reported that Bolsonaro was being investigated by the federal police as part of a money-laundering inquiry.

The leftwing incumbent said he would give electoral authorities access to his banking and phone recordings, and those of close relatives, in order to prove he was not corrupt – if Bolsonaro agreed to do the same.

Ride-hailing company inDrive, known for letting passengers and drivers negotiate fares, is working with Chinese autonomous driving firm Pony.ai and Nvidia to begin testing robotaxis in Kazakhstan in 2027.

The companies said Friday that they had signed a memorandum of understanding to explore bringing Pony.ai’s robotaxis to inDrive’s platform — a reflection of the growing interest in self-driving technology beyond the U.S. and China.

Testing will begin in Kazakhstan’s capital, Astana, in the first half of 2027, inDrive CTO Yuri Misnik told TechCrunch. The tests will initially involve a safety operator, with fully driverless rides as the eventual goal.

Under the proposed plan, Pony.ai would provide its Gen-7 robotaxis, which inDrive would offer through its ride-hailing app and operate in Kazakhstan. Nvidia, on the other hand, would supply the AI technology, including its Hyperion platform and Halos safety system.

The move comes as ride-hailing companies expand their push into robotaxis. Earlier this month, Uber announced plans to test Pony.ai’s robotaxis in London, where it has also started offering supervised autonomous rides with British startup Wayve, an investor-backed partner. Uber is also working with multiple robotaxi companies in other markets, including the Middle East.

Having built its business around affordable rides, inDrive is now looking to bring a similar focus to autonomous ride-hailing as it explores a market where Uber and other rivals are already making moves.

“Our approach is capital-light by design,” said Misnik. “We work with a range of partners in different markets, combining their expertise with ours to deliver a better customer experience.”

Nvidia says its Hyperion platform could lower the cost of developing robotaxis by giving companies a ready-made foundation of sensors and computing technology, rather than requiring them to build and integrate everything themselves. The company also offers its Alpamayo model and tools to generate synthetic data and test autonomous driving systems across different driving scenarios.

“Those savings are particularly relevant in emerging markets, where ride-hailing is already relatively affordable and autonomous services need to compete on cost,” Ali Kani, Nvidia’s vice president of automotive, told TechCrunch.

The announcement coincides with Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang’s first visit to Kazakhstan on Friday.

The Kazakhstan proposal would also add to Pony.ai’s ongoing push beyond China. The company aims to have more than 3,500 robotaxis operating in over 20 cities worldwide by the end of 2026, up from a fleet of 1,975 vehicles at the end of June.

Last month, Kazakhstan announced plans to launch autonomous taxi services in 2027, with initial trials planned in Astana and two other cities. It has also introduced rules for autonomous vehicles to be tested on certain public roads.

Astana’s harsh winters could make it a useful testing ground for robotaxis. The city, with a population of around 1.7 million, faces months of snow and ice, posing challenges for autonomous driving systems.

“Launching here will provide an important opportunity to evaluate autonomous driving technology in challenging winter conditions,” inDrive founder and CEO Arsen Tomsky said. “Success in Astana could offer valuable insights for expanding autonomous mobility to other locations facing similar challenges.”

Kazakhstan is not the only market where inDrive is exploring robotaxis. In December, the company signed a separate agreement with Saudi Arabia’s AI Driver to explore deploying autonomous vehicles powered by Chinese self-driving firm WeRide, starting with Riyadh. The companies had targeted a pilot in the first half of 2026, though it remains unclear whether testing has begun.

“We are also looking at a number of other markets for future deployments,” said Misnik. “One of the main requisites for successful launch in our view is a favorable and collaborative regulatory environment to facilitate safe and sustainable testing on the roads.”

The company operates in more than 1,200 cities across 47 countries and has expanded beyond ride-hailing into delivery, intercity transportation, and financial services.
