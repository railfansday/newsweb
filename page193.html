#3730. Daylight Saving Time Ends Next Week. Here’s When the Clocks Go Back

📅 24 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Daylight saving time ends next weekend, with the clocks officially changing at 2 a.m. on Sunday. While it’ll still be light enough for trick-or-treating on Halloween, the time change will hit the day after, when most of the US adjusts back one hour.

The time shift is notorious for disrupting sleep patterns and schedules. Some politicians are advocating for the abolition of time changes and the permanent adoption of daylight saving time, or DST. The House passed the Sunshine Protection Act in July, but it still needs Senate approval and the president’s signature to become law — meaning that, for now, we still have dark winter hours coming.

If you, too, dislike how early it gets dark each day through winter, just remember that brighter evenings will return in mid-March.

Daylight saving time in the US ends at 2 a.m. local time Sunday, Nov. 1. It will return at 2 a.m. local time on Sunday, March 14, 2027. The end of DST is when we “fall back,” whereas in March, we “spring forward.”

The US isn’t alone in observing daylight saving time. Check out this list of other countries that use DST, along with their start and end dates.

The official US time website shows where time zone boundaries are located.

The exact start dates for daylight saving time and standard time float a little. DST kicks off on the second Sunday of March and ends on the first Sunday of November with the return of standard time.

We spend about eight months of the year in DST. We can thank the Uniform Time Act of 1966 for bringing some order to what had been a complicated history of time changes in the US.

“Motivated by transportation improvements, this act mandated standard time within the existing time zones and established a permanent system of uniform DST, including the dates and times for twice-yearly transitions,” the Bureau of Transportation Statistics states in a history of time zones.

The country’s time zones date to the railroad boom of the late 1800s. DST formally entered the chat in 1918, but its application was inconsistent until 1966. The country tried year-round daylight saving time in January 1974 under President Richard Nixon to address the energy crisis. It didn’t go over well. Congress and President Gerald Ford restored standard time in October 1974.

If you truly hate time changes, then consider moving to Arizona (except for the Navajo Nation) or Hawaii. Those states are on standard time all year long, and their residents don’t have to deal with the physical and mental health impacts of the change. Puerto Rico, Guam, the Virgin Islands, American Samoa and the Northern Mariana Islands also skip DST.

Organizations such as the National Sleep Foundation, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Society for Research on Biological Rhythms are united in calling for permanent standard time, saying it’s better for human biology.

“There’s a mismatch between the outside world and our internal clocks during daylight saving time that can result in some negative health consequences,” says Joseph Dzierzewski, senior vice president of research and scientific affairs at the National Sleep Foundation.

Dzierzewski cites mental health concerns and upticks in cardiovascular events and drowsy driving. He also calls out safety issues with young children walking to school or sitting at bus stops in the dark. Those safety issues were a big reason why permanent DST didn’t last in 1974.

Time changes aren’t popular. An October 2024 YouGov poll (PDF) of more than 1,100 US adults found that 63% would like to see the changing of the clocks eliminated. Only 17% wanted to keep time changes, and 20% weren’t sure.

Efforts to end the time changes have so far failed to pass into law. The bipartisan Sunshine Protection Act, which would have made daylight saving time permanent, passed the Senate in 2022 — the new version of the bill passed the House earlier this year and was sent on to the Senate to review in July.

While many people agree on eliminating time changes, the schism between daylight saving time and standard time remains. Standard time may have a bit of an image issue compared to DST.

“Part of the issue is that people associate daylight saving time with summer. People love summer, right?” Dzierzewski said. “But the simple fact of the matter is, it would still be summer if we were on permanent standard time.”

President Donald Trump criticized time changes in a 2024 Truth Social post: “The Republican Party will use its best efforts to eliminate Daylight Saving Time, which has a small but strong constituency, but shouldn’t! Daylight Saving Time is inconvenient and very costly to our Nation.”

Bills and resolutions at the state level haven’t had real-world impacts yet. Politicians are still split on whether they want permanent daylight saving or standard time. Dzierzewski is hopeful that permanent standard time will win out.

“This is the first time in several years where I’ve seen more legislation introduced at a state level in support of permanent standard time as the solution,” he said.

If you’d like to see time changes end and want to advocate for either daylight saving or standard time, contact your state and national representatives and express your opinion.

Daylight saving time is happening, so now it’s a matter of figuring out how to deal with it. It’s not just about the day of the time change.

“You’re in a better situation to cope with the effects of the clock change if you have good sleep health generally,” Dzierzewski said. He recommends exposure to bright light in the morning, physical activity during the day, eating meals at regular times and practicing a relaxing wind-down routine at night.

The majority of Americans struggle to get the National Sleep Foundation’s recommended 7 to 9 hours of sleep for most adults. A CNET survey found that more than half of US adults use some method to cope with sleep-related challenges. Check out some expert-backed tips for improving your sleep quality.

“If you’re doing these healthy sleep behaviors, you’re probably more resilient to some of these changes,” Dzierzewski said. “If you have poor sleep to begin with, you’re probably going to feel it a little bit more.”

Establishing a better sleep routine can take time. There are two things you can do in direct response to the time change. Dzierzewski suggests adjusting your bedtime and wake-up time gradually ahead of the change. For example, you could shift your schedule by 15 minutes per day and ease yourself into DST. He also recommends getting a good dose of bright morning light on the day of the time change to help set your internal body clock.

Think of DST as another kind of wake-up call, one that can motivate you to evaluate your sleep routine and quality and make positive changes. Start with these six simple habits. And don’t forget to change your clocks. Do it the night before, and you won’t have to worry about it later.

#4281. Is your Medicare Advantage plan ending next year? Here's what to know

A slew of Medicare Advantage plans are disappearing for next year, leaving seniors in many areas with fewer options. In 193 counties across the country, no plans are offered at all.

Medicare's annual open enrollment period kicked off October 15 and runs until December 7, and seniors enrolled in those privatized Medicare plans that are closing in certain markets should carefully review their options.

Medicare Advantage means private insurers run Medicare for older Americans who choose it over traditional Medicare, where the federal government itself administers the program. More than half of the 70 million Americans in Medicare choose Medicare Advantage. It's popular because it covers services like vision and dental, often with lower premiums than traditional Medicare. It also often folds in the "Part D" drug benefit, simplifying that decision.

But a downside of Medicare Advantage is that companies can pick and choose where they offer plans. If a certain community is not profitable, a company can leave that market. And that has been happening on an increasingly large scale the last three years, according to research published in JAMA.

Big swaths of rural America are losing Medicare Advantage choices and so are some major metro areas.

Last year, 2.9 million seniors were in Medicare Advantage plans that closed up shop, according to JAMA. This year, an analysis of insurance company announcements by An Arm and A Leg podcast, shows at least 2 million people will be affected. Other preliminary estimates range from 3.2 million to 5 million seniors. About 35 million people are enrolled in Medicare Advantage.

According to an analysis of federal data by the health policy research organization KFF, the 193 counties that will have zero Medicare Advantage plans are mostly rural. Last year that number was about 120 counties without a plan offered.

KFF's numbers also show that there will be far fewer plans offered in some metro areas. Dallas, Minneapolis, Seattle, Houston and Portland all have between a quarter and a third fewer Medicare Advantage plans for seniors.

If your plan is ending, and you don't choose another, the insurance company will assign you to another Medicare Advantage plan or the government will enroll you in traditional Medicare — but without a supplemental or drug plan.

If you want to switch from Medicare Advantage to traditional Medicare and your plan is ending, you can enroll in a Medicare supplemental plan — also known as Medigap — without the same restrictions that you would normally have. If you have a pre-existing condition that has prevented you from enrolling in Medigap before, that could be good news.

Another wrinkle this year is that insurance companies can cap enrollments on some of the Medicare Advantage plans that they do offer.

Which means: If you wait to sign up, a plan you want might already be full.

The companies are supposed to enroll people on a first-come, first-serve basis — and not cherry-pick customers based on health conditions, age, or demographics.

#3534. What’s It Like Inside a Data Center?

📅 24 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Northern Virginia is laden with structures that house data for Fortune 500 companies. I went inside just one of those monstrosities that bothers its neighbors.

Northern Virginia is laden with structures that house data for Fortune 500 companies. I went inside just one of those monstrosities that bothers its neighbors.

The sun was beating down as I made my way through a quiet community in Ashburn, Virginia. The roads were wide and lined with cars; there was a nice central playground with no one playing in it; the houses seemed monstrously large; and there were ample trees dotting the clean-cut lawns of the neighborhood.

In so many ways, it felt like your typical US suburb, until you looked past the thick wall of trees that defined its boundaries. The white walls of a large facility peeked out from above the deep green foliage. It ran on in the distance, the large houses making it impossible to see its full extent. Towering above it a little farther away were the high-voltage transmission lines that fed it with electricity. The trees acted like a shield, allowing those who lived in the community not to think about what stood on their doorstep and the growing demands that came from the surrounding industrial expansion. I’m talking, of course, about hyperscale data centers.

No location on the entire planet has more data centers than Virginia. The state holds more than 13 percent of global hyperscale capacity—double that of Beijing, which comes in second. There were nearly 600 data centers within the state’s boundaries in 2025, about 150 of which were hyperscale—the monstrously large variety that have become much more common since the advent of generative AI—and most of them were clustered in an area in the north of the state around Ashburn, which has since been dubbed “Data Center Alley.”

Data centers are unavoidable in Northern Virginia. When I sat on a bench next to a neighborhood lake, a hyperscale data center owned by Microsoft stared back at me from the opposite bank. As I drove through industrial parks, they were dotted throughout. When I pulled into a shopping area to grab a bite to eat, facilities that powered Amazon Web Services loomed behind the strip mall. I could even feel their influence in the street names: Cloud Plaza, Performance Circle, and, worst of all, Growth Mindset Lane. We can thank Microsoft for that one.

In short, they were everywhere when I visited in 2025, and following them wherever they set down were the high-voltage power lines needed to keep them running. Data centers were remaking the fabric of a whole region; there seemed to be no end in sight for their expansion. They had become a major industry, and neither worries over local resource demands nor growing concern from residents seemed able to stop the ambitions of some of the most powerful companies on the planet.

It’s hard to generalize about the data centers themselves. They come in many shapes and sizes, operate at various scales—from rooms in a nondescript building to entire parks of warehouse-sized facilities crammed with thousands of servers apiece—and seem to be getting bigger with every passing year. Some of them exist to serve the companies operating them; others exist as places where many different groups can rent space for servers of their own. Certain facilities are cooled with air-conditioning, while others depend more on water-based cooling, and even the way those systems are implemented can differ. I guess what I’m trying to say is that one description of a data center will never cover the gamut: There is some standardization, but there is also a lot of room for operators to put their own stamp on how their facility or network of locations is going to work.

The interiors of some Google data centers have clusters of brightly colored tubes running throughout them—echoing the primary colors present in the company’s branding—while the average Microsoft or Amazon facility might appear more utilitarian. Digital Realty is one of the largest data-center operators in the world. The company has two major lines of business: It builds entire facilities that then get leased to other companies, including the major cloud providers, but it also offers colocation services. Those are facilities where Digital Realty runs the data center, renting space in racks to other companies that they can then fill with their own servers. I stopped to walk the length of one of its facilities in Northern Virginia. The simplest way to describe it is to say that it looks like a massive box. The company’s logo was visible near the upper portion of the building, and it had some different shades of gray and blue across its exterior.

The data center seemed quiet that weekend afternoon, though even on a weekday there weren’t likely to be many people there. Data centers don’t need many employees to keep them clean, secure, and running; the bulk of the jobs they provide are created in the construction phase and mostly disappear once that’s over. The parking lot at the front of the building was surrounded by a large black fence that went around the entire property. I wasn’t trying to get in, but I did wonder if there was a security officer behind a desk somewhere inside the building peering at me through the video feed of one of the facility’s many security cameras. Data centers are notoriously well-guarded, to keep their data safe and to assuage the worries of their clients.

There wasn’t much to see as I walked along the fence, the spikes at the top of each pole warning me not to try anything rash. It took nearly five minutes to walk from one end of the building to the other, and this wasn’t even one of the bigger ones I’d seen that day. At the far end, a little beyond the fence, I walked onto a wooden platform where I could make out a serene pond obscured by tall trees and plants. The chirps and trills of birds and insects were just barely audible over the buzz of the air-conditioning system on the facility’s roof. The pond appeared impossible to access as its lifeless neighbor encroached upon it.

When I pulled into an outdoor shopping mall, I parked across from the shell of a structure that was soon to become a data center. Some tech companies will take over an existing facility and convert it into a data center, but most end up being built from scratch on vast tracts of newly cleared land. Towering outer walls of concrete reached far above even the retail stores that surrounded me. Each one was held up with steel braces as they waited for the installation of roof trusses that would be used to bind the various pieces together. After that, it would all be sealed against the elements with some utilitarian exterior cladding, then fitted with the cabling, temperature-regulation systems, power backups, and everything else needed to get thousands of servers running and wired to the Internet.

It’s not always easy to know when a data center is slated for a community before it starts rising out of the ground—or, when residents do get that information, who is actually behind it. Consider Lowell Charter Township, which sits about 20 miles outside Grand Rapids, Michigan. In 2025, its residents learned that a new data center was planned for their area, and they saw their local leaders moving fast to rezone the land and get any barriers out of the way to make it happen. Yet no one would reveal which company would ultimately run the facility.

The public was not happy, and near the end of the year so many angry residents crowded into a planning-commission meeting that officials were forced to put the project on ice. It was only then that Microsoft finally came out from behind the curtain to reveal itself as the proprietor.

Situations like this are not uncommon, though they rarely end in the outcome that the residents of Lowell achieved. Major tech firms use shell companies to hide their involvement in data-center projects until it’s too late for locals to stop them, just as Google did in The Dalles, Oregon, when it used the name “Design LLC.” Amazon has gone by “Razor5 LLC” in Indiana, and Meta deployed “Sidecat LLC” in Ohio and “Degas LLC” in Wisconsin.

Tech companies need a lot of data centers to fuel their ambitions. They do not need nearly as many offices where engineers create the products that justify the need for data centers. When I saw that shell of a data center in Virginia, I was also seeing the phase in which it would provide the most employment. During construction, a data-center project tends to employ over 1,000 people—even more for the monstrously large hyperscale facilities—but once they’re done, the employment numbers tend to drop into the dozens. These are not high-paying engineering jobs: Many of the workers are doing security, cleaning, or conducting maintenance on the systems. Is that really worth hundreds of thousands, if not millions, of dollars per job in tax breaks and incentives? A growing number of Virginians didn’t think so.

The rush to build out data centers in response to the generative-AI boom has led to a renewed rush for land, this time led by the companies competing in the AI race or trying to profit from powering it. With time of the essence, access to electricity has become one of the defining metrics of a site’s viability. Companies can’t wait years for capacity to be built or grid connections to be made. In some cases, that competitive pressure has led companies to bypass some of the demands they typically make of local governments—as was the case when xAI, Elon Musk’s AI company, did not demand property-tax abatements from Memphis, Tennessee. Musk wanted the data center up and running as quickly as possible. But the construction boom has also lifted some surprising companies along with it. Construction firms clearly benefit from all the additional projects they get hired to complete, while electricity companies and computer-hardware manufacturers find there’s a much greater demand for what they’re selling.

Communities like the one I visited in Virginia were feeling the brunt of the AI boom. As the data centers got closer, some property owners even started begging government officials to rezone their land for industrial use so they could sell and move away from it all. In Northern Virginia, the signs of that opposition are increasingly common. I spotted large banners hanging from gazebos along a central stretch of road in Loudoun County, warning that towering high-voltage transmission lines would slash the values of people’s homes. “Who pays? Loudoun families,” the banners read. “Who profits? Data centers and Dominion”—the local electricity provider and utility.

Residents also have to deal with tremors through their homes during the construction period, followed by the incessant hum of air-conditioning units once the center is up and running. Some people have to contend with the bright lights that stay on all night to illuminate the exterior of the facilities. It’s easy to dismiss some of these concerns as typical NIMBY complaints, but most Virginians were not initially opposed to data centers. For a long time, there wasn’t much of a concerted campaign to stop data centers at all. That only began to change as they started encroaching on communities and people experienced the tangible impacts in their lives.

In early 2026, a cold snap hit many parts of the United States. Once the grid was stressed, data centers in Virginia were given permission to run fully on their diesel generators, with little regard for the air-quality impacts of burning all that fuel. They did not have to “reduce their consumption, take some of the AI offline,” said Chris Miller, the president of the Piedmont Environmental Council. Instead, the decision was made “to expose the general public to an increased level of public-health hazard” from the burning of all that diesel fuel. It’s no wonder that as data-center construction ramped up in 2024 and ’25, opposition began growing around it.

Between 2024, when I’d first spoken to Miller, and early 2026, when we reconnected, there had been what he described as a night-and-day shift. People were feeling the effects of data centers near their communities, and they were also getting confirmation that what they were feeling was real. Over that time, a lot more data became available on the facilities’ water use and electricity demands, and media reports shed further light on how they were contributing to higher electricity prices in places like Virginia. People were finally waking up to what was going on around them.

When you’re stuck on the outside of a data center, it’s easy to imagine the interior as sinister: ominous, towering black boxes filled with servers whose small lights and indicators give off an eerie glow in the dimmed light of a chilly server room. But the truth is that they’re typically quite mundane and utilitarian spaces. In bigger facilities, the floors can be concrete, but many others have elevated floors covered with removable tiles, while fluorescent lights keep things well lit—at least when humans are inside. Miles of cables running overhead and down into the racks enable rapid data transfer and the servers’ ultimate connection to the wider Internet.

There’s no question that the sensitive data held inside must be protected, and the extent of the effort to do so must be visible to clients. But it’s not a form of theater that ends at the door. Stringent security practices are present throughout the facility, beginning with the people who are allowed through the door. I had a hard time getting inside a major data center: The most recognizable companies rejected my requests, if they responded to them at all. Several of the experts I spoke with told me that’s typical. Since the generative-AI boom has increased the scrutiny placed on data centers, getting inside their walls has become much more difficult.

When I finally visited a data center for a tour, the extent of the security measures struck me more than anything else. Security officials already had my name when I arrived at the gate. Once I got through the door, I needed to check in and hand over my passport before I could go any further. No photos were allowed. As we made our way through the facility, it became clear that you couldn’t simply walk through any of the doors. Each one of them had its own digital lock, meaning the executive escorting me had to tap us into room after room as he explained how the different parts of the facility worked.

When you enter the server room of a data center, the warm air produced by all of the computer hardware envelops you, but far more striking is the incessant metallic hum that feels like an auditory assault—the product of all the servers and whirring fans in the room. Rows of tall lockers (called “racks”) fill the facility, each containing dozens of long, thin servers. Depending on the size of the data center, there can be hundreds, thousands, or hundreds of thousands of servers throughout the building. The server racks stand seven feet high, with bundles of colored cables connecting to the hardware and running along the back of each rack. When the servers are cooled with air-conditioning, you can feel the difference in the heat they produce simply by standing on either side of a rack.

Standing inside a data center, I couldn’t help but think of the vast network of supply chains that make them possible. The Internet has extended to touch every corner of the world, but so too do the trade routes whose finished goods eventually sit within the facility’s racks. The many thousands of servers overwhelmingly arrive from factories in Asia, with supply chains that snake out to mines across the world where the minerals and metals essential for their production originate.

The Internet can seem like an immaterial thing when we log on to social-media platforms or do Google searches that send us to any number of websites. But structuring data centers as real-estate-investment vehicles required defining and treating the network in a wholly different way. It’s an important element of the business of the Internet that often gets ignored, but it has significant implications for the Web itself. Those corporate structures shape the incentives that drive the companies ultimately building out the foundations of the digital world.

#4134. Orangutans Prefer Things Associated With Laughter Even When the Rewards Are Identical

📅 24 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

An unusual experiment suggests laughter can change how orangutans remember and value their experiences.

Give an orangutan a choice between two places to find a snack, and you’d expect it to pick the one that has offered the best treats. But what if both places have been equally rewarding? What could possibly tip the scales?

According to a new experiment, the answer might be laughter.

Scientists played recordings of orangutans laughing before six captive apes searched for food. Days later, when the researchers gave them a choice between equally rewarding options, the orangutans substantially favored the one they had learned to associate with laughter.

Positive emotions may influence how orangutans remember experiences and assign value to them. But how can we tell whether animals experience joy, and what does that joy actually do?

The experiment involved six orangutans, ranging in age from 8 to 51, living at zoos in Belgium and the United States.

Researchers led by Daan Laméris of Indiana University first taught the apes to forage for treats, including almonds and cashews, hidden beneath materials such as wood chips, shredded paper, or yarn.

The orangutans quickly learned which materials concealed food. In a preliminary test, four of the six also demonstrated that they could remember which materials had previously yielded larger rewards.

Over four days, the orangutans learned to associate two different materials with identical food rewards. Before searching through one material, the apes listened to five minutes of recordings of unfamiliar young orangutans laughing and squeaking during play. Before searching through the other, they heard ordinary wind-like sounds.

On the fifth day, the researchers brought back both materials and gave the animals 30 opportunities to choose between them, with equivalent reward schedules.

All six orangutans favored the material associated with laughter. Four showed a statistically significant individual preference. Across the group, the laughter-associated material attracted roughly 67% of choices.

The sounds didn’t appear to make the apes learn any faster. Rather, laughter seemed to change how much they valued what they had learned.

“It is surprising to find these effects with orangutans, because they are the least social of all the great apes,” Laméris said in a statement. “So even among relatively nonsocial animals, laughter is important.”

Erica Cartmill, the study’s senior author, compared the phenomenon to preferring your grandmother’s pancakes. They likely aren’t objectively better than most other pancakes, but because of the happy memories associated with eating them, you’re likely to subjectively favor them.

Animal behavior research has traditionally devoted considerable attention to negative emotions, particularly fear, stress, and suffering, and how they change how animals manifest. Positive emotions such as joy have received comparatively less scrutiny, partly because they are so difficult to measure.

After all, an orangutan cannot tell scientists that it’s happy.

But researchers can investigate whether experiences associated with positive emotions change how an animal behaves later.

A growing body of evidence suggests they do. In a 2025 study involving bonobos, researchers found that the apes tended to behave more optimistically after hearing recordings of laughter, although the findings were not conclusive. Previous experiments have also linked play and laughter-like vocalizations to more positive expectations in rats.

The orangutan study suggests that playful sounds can influence the value animals attach to remembered experiences. One possible explanation is emotional contagion, the phenomenon in which another individual’s emotional expression influences our own feelings.

However, the experiment included only six orangutans, and scientists didn’t directly measure their emotional states. The animals’ preferences are consistent with laughter producing a more positive mood, but they cannot prove that the orangutans actually felt joy.

Still, the implications are intriguing. The same mechanisms might help explain why humans develop lasting preferences for experiences or subjects associated with positive feelings, even when the objective rewards are no different.

For Cartmill, the larger ambition is to understand animal lives at an intimate level.

“Part of this project was to ask, ‘What are the moments that help shape the lives of the animals we share a planet with?’” she said. “It isn’t just about surviving to reproduce, but it’s actually about creating a narrative that also involves friendships and memories and building toward the things that give life meaning.”

The answer, it seems, may involve something as simple as hearing someone else having a good time.

The study was published in the journal Biology Letters.

“Now you know what it’s like to be in the eye of a storm,” said our hiking guide, Audun Rasmussen, with a grin.

I tried to nod, but the wind whipped it into a wobble. We were nearly 2,000 feet up a steep, multi-edged mountain face, and simultaneously getting pelted by rain. #4144. Physicists Trapped a Single Antimatter Particle for Months. Now Comes the Hard Part

📅 24 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

For the first time, scientists can observe and control the quantum behavior of a single antimatter particle.

Physicists have trapped a single particle of antimatter for months and, for the first time, demonstrated that they can control its quantum behavior with extraordinary precision.

The particle is a positron, the antimatter counterpart of an electron. When the two meet, they annihilate each other, making positrons exceedingly difficult to study because, you know, electrons are everything.

Researchers at Northwestern University overcame this problem by suspending a single positron in a vacuum using electric and magnetic fields, then cooling it to nearly absolute zero.

“We actually coaxed this very fast positron to sit there and behave for months at a time,” said Gerald Gabrielse, the Northwestern physicist who led the research.

You hear the word ‘breakthrough’ thrown out a lot, but this really is one. It could allow scientists to compare matter and antimatter more precisely than ever before. Any unexpected difference could challenge our understanding of fundamental physics and offer clues to one of science’s biggest mysteries: why the universe is made almost entirely of matter rather than antimatter, even though the two were supposed to have been produced in equal quantities just moments after the Big Bang. The answer to this question may explain why anything exists at all.

Every familiar particle of matter has an antimatter counterpart. Electrons have positrons, protons have antiprotons, and neutrons have antineutrons. Their masses match those of their ordinary counterparts, but certain properties, including electric charge, are reversed.

Positrons are particularly difficult to study because electrons are everywhere. An encounter between the two usually ends in mutual annihilation, and there goes your experiment — and it’s not like you can just try the next moment again. Antimatter costs an estimated $62.5 trillion to $100 trillion per gram to produce, making it the most expensive substance on Earth.

“If you want to hold on to antimatter, you have to hold it in a container without walls,” Gabrielse told Scientific American.

To accomplish that, Gabrielse and his colleagues, including doctoral student and first author Thomas Myers, devised an elaborate procedure that begins with radioactive sodium-22.

When sodium-22 undergoes radioactive decay, it can release a positron traveling at tremendous speed. Capturing such a particle requires slowing it almost to a standstill without destroying it.

The researchers first passed the positrons through a cold tungsten crystal. Collisions inside the crystal robbed them of energy, although many particles were annihilated along the way.

Some of the surviving positrons then picked up an electron, forming an unusual, loosely bound particle pair called positronium. In this highly excited state, the pair could travel together long enough to enter the trap but still far enough apart not to annihilate each other.

This seemingly counterintuitive step solved an important engineering problem. Because positronium is electrically neutral, it could slip through a tiny opening in the trapping apparatus without being deflected by the surrounding electric fields.

Once inside, those fields separated the electron from the positron. The electron escaped, leaving the positron safely confined.

The apparatus, known as a Penning trap, combines a powerful 5.6-tesla magnetic field with electric fields to hold the particle away from any physical surface.

Meanwhile, a specialized refrigerator cools the chamber to just 0.3 degrees above absolute zero, approximately minus 272.85 degrees Celsius. At this temperature, thermal radiation is almost completely eliminated, and the near-perfect vacuum minimizes the risk of collisions.

The entire process is excruciatingly painstaking. According to the authors, loading a single positron currently takes between eight and eleven hours.

But once captured, the particle can remain suspended for months.

Scientists have confined antiparticles and even entire antimatter atoms before, so that’s not the novel thing about this work.

What makes the Northwestern experiment different is the degree of control over a solitary positron’s quantum behavior.

Inside the trap, the particle circles in the magnetic field, behaving somewhat like a tiny artificial atom. Its motion can occupy only certain allowed energy levels, much as an electron in an atom can occupy distinct quantum states.

The researchers call this system a one-positron quantum cyclotron.

By delivering carefully tuned microwave pulses, they could make the positron jump from its lowest energy level to the next one. They could also flip its spin, an intrinsic quantum property that determines how the particle responds to a magnetic field.

Most importantly, they could detect these changes without disturbing the quantum states they wanted to measure.

“It’s a real kick to stand in my lab and watch a positron making transitions — ‘quantum jumps’ we call them — between its lowest energy states. How much more fundamental, how much neater can it get?” Gabrielse told New Scientist.

“This has the potential to become one of the most precise measurements of a fundamental constant in physics,” Stefan Ulmer, a physicist at the Japanese research institute RIKEN who was not involved in the experiment, told New Scientist.

All this effort is directed toward one of the most important questions in physics: Is an electron truly identical to its antimatter counterpart in every way that physics predicts?

One of the most important properties researchers want to compare is the particles’ magnetic moment, which describes the strength and orientation of their magnetism.

In 2023, Gabrielse and colleagues reported an extraordinarily precise measurement of the electron’s magnetic moment, achieving an uncertainty of just 0.13 parts per trillion.

Measuring the positron with comparable precision would allow the researchers to compare the two particles much more rigorously than before.

Historically, positron measurements have lagged behind electron measurements. The most precise direct comparison dates to 1987 and found agreement within 2.1 parts per trillion.

According to an independent assessment by physicist Paolo Crivelli of ETH Zurich, the Northwestern apparatus could make a comparison more than 30 times more precise. However, the team must still complete the demanding measurements and account for potential experimental errors.

The stakes for theoretical physics are grand.

The Standard Model, physicists’ remarkably successful description of fundamental particles and three of nature’s four fundamental forces, incorporates a principle known as CPT symmetry.

The principle states that the laws of physics should remain unchanged if three transformations occur together: particles are exchanged for antiparticles, spatial directions are reversed as though viewed through a mirror, and time runs backward.

Among other things, CPT symmetry requires the electron and positron to have magnetic moments that are equal in magnitude and opposite in sign.

Even a tiny discrepancy would challenge some of the deepest foundations of modern particle physics.

And it could be relevant to an even older puzzle. According to our current understanding of the early universe, the Big Bang should have produced matter and antimatter in nearly equal quantities. As they encountered one another, most would have been annihilated.

Yet somehow, a tiny excess of matter survived. That remnant eventually became every galaxy, star, planet and living thing we know.

Physicists have proposed mechanisms that might explain this imbalance, but none has been conclusively established by experiments and observations.

Experiments elsewhere are also on the lookout. At CERN, for example, the ALPHA collaboration has spent years studying antihydrogen, the antimatter version of hydrogen. In May 2026, researchers announced a hundredfold improvement in the precision of a key antihydrogen measurement. The results remained consistent with ordinary hydrogen.

So far, matter and antimatter have stubbornly obeyed the expected symmetries wherever these experiments have looked.

Nor has the Northwestern team found such a difference yet. The present achievement is the ability to trap, manipulate and observe a single positron’s quantum states. The decisive magnetic-moment comparison still lies ahead.

Nevertheless, the experiment has opened a new avenue for testing nature at an extraordinary level of precision.

“Current theories suggest that there will be no difference in the two measurements,” Gabrielse said.

“If it turns out they are not equal, that would be the biggest discovery in physics in the last century.”

The findings were reported in the journal Physical Review Letters.

Some of them are easy to troubleshoot — try a different plug or cable and see if that fixes the issue. Others may be more difficult to resolve.

A very common reason for faulty charging is that your phone’s charging port is clogged with fluff, dirt and other debris from inside your pocket, preventing your cable from connecting properly.

This can easily happen with any charging port, whether that’s Lightning on the iPhone 14 Pro or older iPhones, or USB-C on the more recent iPhone 18 Pro lineup and Android phones such as the Google Pixel 11 Pro or Samsung Galaxy S26 Ultra.

However, this is one of the simplest and cheapest charging problems to fix. Here’s how you can do it at home.

Day by day, this won’t be much of a problem, but those tiny bits of dust and fluff will build up inside your charging port over the months or years you have your phone, compacting and building up each time you plug in your charging cable until it forms a solid barrier that stops your charger from going all the way in and allowing it to connect and charge.

A wooden cocktail stick is a great tool for the job as the wood won’t damage the internal parts.

If you’ve had your phone for more than a few months and you’re gradually finding the charger becoming less and less stable (particularly if you have to wiggle it around to get it to begin charging or if the cable won’t go in all the way) then it’s highly likely that dirt in your port is the culprit.

Unclogging your phone’s port is simple. You’ll need a cocktail stick, toothpick or other thin item that you can poke into the port to scrape out the grime. Wood or plastic is better, as it’s less likely to scrape against anything inside and potentially cause harm. I’ve done this using a metal SIM removal tool, a sewing needle and the pin on the back of an earring, and while it worked well, it’s not always the safest option for your phone.

Gently work on the sides of the port, too, but be careful not to scrape hard against any of the metal charging connectors.

Soon, you’ll have pretty much all of it out. It’s difficult to check for sure if you’ve gotten rid of it all, as it’s tricky to see inside the port, even if you have a good light handy. But once you’ve got a good amount of debris out, you can try your charging cable again.
