#960. How a multibillion-dollar oilfield has ignited the UK-Argentina feud over the Falklands

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Argentina and the UK once fought a war over the Falkland Islands. Now, the multibillion-dollar Sea Lion oil project has reignited old tensions, with Buenos Aires seeking to put a stop to the plans.

Home to fewer than 4,000 people and located in an isolated corner of the South Atlantic Ocean, the Falkland Islands don't often feature in global business headlines.

However, a UK-Israeli oil exploration project in the North Falkland Basin is stoking long-running tensions between Argentina and the people of the archipelago.

Argentina has laid claim to the islands, which it calls Las Malvinas, for two centuries. In 1982, the dispute over the islands' sovereignty led to a war between the UK and Argentina, though British control was quickly restored after Argentine forces invaded the islands.

A 2013 sovereignty referendum on the islands returned a 99.8% vote in favor of remaining tied to the UK as a self-governing British Overseas Territory.

The Sea Lion oilfield project has brought the issue back to the surface. Last month, Argentine President Javier Milei threatened to launch an international legal challenge against the UK unless the project was stopped within two weeks.

The oil project is being run by Israeli company Navitas Petroleum and the UK's Rockhopper Exploration and has the full support of the Falkand Islands Legislative Assembly, which has pursued the project since 2010. The companies plan to begin extracting oil from the Sea Lion oilfield by early 2028.

Milei says the project is "the illegal plundering of our resources" and that he will take the matter to the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea, an independent judicial body responsible for the application of the United Nations' Convention on the Law of the Sea.

Stacy Bragger, a member of the Falklands Islands' 12-member assembly, told DW that the dispute was "nothing new" and that Argentina is using the oil project as "a vehicle to pursue their sovereignty claim."

He thinks Milei may be stepping up rhetoric ahead of the 2027 elections in Argentina.

"When there's elections coming up, the Falklands is a very convenient tool to use in order to rally people together and distract them from domestic problems," he said.

Henry Ziemer, an analyst with the Americas program at the Center for Strategic and International Studies in Washington, says the Falklands is "a perennial sore spot in Argentine politics" and that since the 1980s, various governments have raised the issue in an attempt to rally support and to "stoke the fires of nationalism."

Howerver, he thinks Milei's intervention is noteworthy given that he had previously been open to better relations with the UK on the issue. He agrees that it may be a strategy to "boost approval ratings at a time when the government is under a lot of strong domestic pressure."

Even if Milei sees political gain in the issue, the possibility of the Sea Lion project being successful would significantly raise the economic stakes for all concerned.

The project has a budget of $1.8 billion (€1.6 billion) and could generate revenues of more than double that for the Falkland Islands. The companies hope that at its peak, the field will produce up to 50,000 barrels per day and could last for up to 35 years.

That would substantially increase the Falklands' current annual GDP, which is almost entirely dependent on the volatile fishing sector and tourism. The administration is even looking into the possibility of creating a sovereign wealth fund if the oil project is successful.

"It's a very significant project for us with a lot of potential for our future," said Bragger. "It's been a big effort to get to this point and assuming all goes to plan, we want to retain as much value as possible."

However, beyond playing to domestic politics, Milei may also spy an opportunity for Argentina to position itself as an energy player amid the war in Iran and the troubles in the Strait of Hormuz.

Argentina's own domestic oil sector is growing, with production surging at Patagonia's Vaca Muerta, one of the world's largest shale developments. Were Milei's legal efforts to prove successful, there may be further gains.

"When natural resources overlay existing territorial disputes and bad blood, that tends to galvanize a lot of conflict," said Ziemer.

However, Bragger counters that Milei and Argentina have no legitimate claim to either the Falklands or whatever oil wealth may be extracted over the coming years.

"They don't respect our right to self-determination," he added. "They don't respect our right to develop our own resources. They just want to colonize us."

Milei plans to put pressure on the Falklands by stepping up existing economic sanctions. Andrea Clausen, chief executive of the Falklands since 2025, recently described this campaign as "economic warfare."

Many international suppliers and shipping companies already refuse to supply the Falklands for fear of angering Argentina. Now, Milei's government has initiated sanctions against 60 people involved in the Sea Lion project.

In a televised address last month, the Argentine president also vowed to sanction the companies involved in the project. Such a move could cast doubt on the project, which previously collapsed in 2014 and 2020, due to financing troubles, oil-price weakness and delays caused by sanctions.

Navitas this week confirmed that two contractors had pulled out of the project due to sanctions concerns, and that it was replacing them. There are also concerns that Milei could try to garner international support in favor of his position, with US President Donald Trump recently suggesting that US support for the status quo on the islands was "under review."

Bragger played this down, pointing to recent comments from US Secretary of State Marco Rubio confirming support for the Falklands. Ziemer said it would be "very shocking" if the US came down on the side of Argentina.

While uncertainty reigns over whether the oil project ultimately gets going or not, there is little expectation that the dispute could lead to a return of armed conflict like in 1982.

"That's a step too far, and the situation would have to dramatically deteriorate before we would see the potential for military conflict," said Ziemer.

#958. Goal, assist... goal-kick - Kane underlines Ballon d'Or credentials

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Even by Harry Kane's remarkable standards, this was mightily impressive.

There were fewer than 25 minutes on the clock during Bayern Munich's thrilling 2-2 draw with Arsenal in the Champions League when the forward took matters into his own hands in a bid to break the deadlock.

As the Premier League champions prepared to defend a Bayern goal-kick, Kane seized the initiative by launching his side up the pitch with a raking 80-yard pass.

It was a moment of remarkable ingenuity from the England international, one that almost ended with Bayern taking the lead.

"Under Vincent Kompany, he has been given a level of trust that he has probably never had before under any other manager," former Manchester City defender Nedum Onuoha told BBC Sport.

"For all the things we've seen him do and the positions we've seen him roam into, nobody has seen him take a goal-kick before.

"But even someone like Manuel Neuer, who is his senior and has been at Bayern for a large part of his career, sees Harry Kane put the ball down and thinks, 'OK, sure'. He lets Harry go for it because he thinks whatever he sees is going to be correct.

"At the same time, there were Bayern players running forward because they knew that the ball could come.

"For him to be taking a goal-kick is not something you may want to see very often. You don't necessarily want your number nine to be THAT deep, but the nature of his game when he is playing for Bayern is to roam, and today it was a huge benefit to the team."

Although Kane was denied the opportunity to add another outstanding assist to his already impressive catalogue, he did not have to wait long to make another decisive contribution.

After Eberechi Eze had put Arsenal ahead, Kane helped Bayern restore parity, bending a sumptuous pass around Jurrien Timber for Alphonso Davies to finish.

The England captain then fired the hosts in front from the penalty spot midway through the second half, taking his career tally of goals against Arsenal to 16.

But Declan Rice struck late on to earn Arsenal a share of the spoils.

Kane recorded 40 touches in Wednesday's 2-2 draw with Arsenal

For Kane, it was another mesmerising performance that underlined his credentials as the frontrunner for the 2026 Ballon d'Or.

The former Tottenham Hotspur captain, 33, could become the first Englishman since Michael Owen in 2001 to win the prestigious individual award, with Barcelona's Lamine Yamal and Paris St-Germain forward and current holder Ousmane Dembele among the favourites.

Kane was named on the 30-man shortlist in September, with the winner due to be announced on Monday following a vote by an international jury of 100 journalists.

"He is the clear winner. His numbers speak for themselves. The year he has had has been remarkable," Arsenal and England midfielder Declan Rice told TNT Sports.

"When you play with him, you realise how good he is, and when you play against him, you see how much of a pain it is.

"He is ridiculous, such a good player, and I am lucky I can play with him, not so lucky I have to play against him."

By scoring against Arsenal on Wednesday night, Kane became only the second player in Champions League history to find the net in 10 consecutive matches.

The only other player to achieve the feat was five-time Ballon d'Or winner Cristiano Ronaldo, who scored in 11 successive matches for Real Madrid between June 2017 and April 2018.

"Kane certainly looks at home at Bayern. He understands the team and its system, and they all understand him. They know that when he gets the ball at his feet, wherever that may be, something good can happen," Onuoha added.

"I think all the Bayern players are really playing into that, but then Harry steps up and scores a penalty as well.

"Having freedom is probably the best way to describe what he has got playing for Bayern, because he will work hard defensively, which allows him the freedom to be creative when he is in attack. For now, he certainly is thriving."

Kane's goal against Arsenal took his tally to 156 in 158 appearances since joining Bayern Munich from Tottenham in 2023.

It was another night to remember for the England captain, but the best could yet be to come, with the Ballon d'Or ceremony in London looming.

"It would be amazing to win," said Kane afterwards. "The voting closed a long time ago so there's nothing I can do, just wait and see what happens."

#956. Wigan pine for FA Cup glory as not even victory can lift them off foot of League One

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Gary Caldwell, club captain in 2013 and now manager, needs much more than the weekend win against Reading to disperse the clouds

If the 2013 FA Cup final represented a blip for the Sheikh Mansour-era Manchester City in their quest for total domination of English football, it was a historic moment for their conquerors, Wigan Athletic.

Memories of that famous day at Wembley still endure for Latics supporters despite the relegation from the Premier League that followed. Yet watching the modern‑day Wigan, who remain bottom of League One despite a 1-0 win against Reading on Saturday, makes the toppling of City in a Cup final seem an epoch ago.

The club, perhaps understandably, continue to hark back to their halcyon days. Last season they brought back Gary Caldwell – club captain in 2013 – for his second stint as manager, having led the club to promotion from this division in 2015-16. Choosing to swap Exeter for a return to the Latics was seen as a risky move by Caldwell, considering the shoestring budget imposed upon him by Mike Danson, the owner who is keen to sell.

Finances are ultra-tight at the Greater Manchester club amid dwindling crowds and losses on the pitch and on the balance sheet. The local businessman Danson and his family saved Wigan from liquidation in June 2023 after mismanagement from previous owners brought a points deduction and relegation from the Championship. Three successive mid-table finishes in League One under their stewardship have brought a semblance of stability, but this season they have plummeted.

The problem is the Dansons have been unable to find an ambitious buyer to take the club to the next level. Now the prospect of a first season in the fourth tier since 1996‑97 is a distinct possibility, although victory against Reading – the Latics’ second league win of the campaign, sealed by Scott Hogan’s 71st-minute strike – made the picture a little rosier.

“Some of our play was tremendous,” Caldwell said. “Our spirit, our character, our determination not to let the ball go in the net was fantastic. It was pretty stressful towards the end. We need to try and build on this. You have to keep the players believing and committing. There’s still so many aspects of our game that need to be better, but a result makes you feel better about yourself psychologically.”

The presence of Leam Richardson in the opposition dugout at the Brick Community Stadium gave fans more reason to reminisce about better days. Richardson, who claimed the League One title as Wigan manager in April 2022, was sacked in November of the same year just 16 days after signing a three-year contract.

Caldwell has also found himself under pressure. The former centre‑back has been criticised by fans for his team’s uninspiring style of play, which has produced just six goals in nine games, the fewest in League One. They went 447 minutes without a league goal until Hogan, the veteran forward, converted after Christian Saydee’s cross was parried out by the Reading goalkeeper, Joel Pereira.

And while Wigan’s keeper, Joe Whitworth, was regularly called into action to see out the win, the home team were still good value for three points, allowing Caldwell and his squad to breathe. The manager’s backers will rightly point to the lack of spending by the club in the summer, with the £1.5m generated by the sale of Sam Tickle to Bristol City not yet reinvested on players and an overreliance on young loanees to prop up the squad.

Danson and the club’s hierarchy have issued rallying cries to supporters to return to the stadium in an effort to generate more revenue, thus making the club more appealing to new owners, but why should fans part with hard‑earned cash to watch such insipid football?

Before the game, Caldwell said: “We also recognise the need for points is big, for our supporters to have that feelgood factor, and be able to leave the stadium in a much better frame of mind than they have been.”

The Scot got what he needed against Reading, yet the sense of chaos above Caldwell was evidenced by the abrupt departure of Sarah Guilfoyle from her role as managing director on the eve of the new season, after a 10-year stay at the club. And there are plenty who feel the owners ought to bear the brunt of criticism.

The Wigan Athletic Supporters Action Group is demanding answers from Danson on why selling the club has been such a struggle and the lack of investment. Other fans are more resigned to the idea that some short-term pain may be necessary for long-term gain; most agree a sale would be transformative.

For now, despite a rare victory against a hitherto in-form Reading, the short-term struggles look likely to continue. The hope is that a hero of 2013 can perform miracles on the pitch, despite the constraints off it.

#953. iPhone Duo 2 prototypes are already circulating at Apple, report says

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

The iPhone Duo doesn't officially come out until tomorrow, but on the Bloomberg "Power On" podcast, Apple oracle Mark Gurman is already reporting on the iPhone Duo 2 and the iPhone Duo 3.

Early reviews for the iPhone Duo are out now (Mashable Senior Editor Stan Schroeder said in his Duo review that it's the "most interesting iPhone since the first one"), and some reviewers have complained about its reliance on Touch ID. Many iOS users have gotten used to Face ID in recent years, but the Duo uses a Touch ID fingerprint sensor instead.

But according to Gurman, Bloomberg's consumer technology managing editor, prototypes of the iPhone Duo 2 with an under-display FaceID sensor are already circulating inside Apple.

"I’m told there are iPhone Duo 2 prototypes at Apple already that have an under-display Face ID sensor and camera on the outside screen, so that is something we could see," Gurman said on his "Power On" podcast. "So, I would anticipate this Touch ID to Face ID thing is fixed by the first update or the second update, so in the next two to three years or so."

Gurman reports that Apple is experimenting with an under-display Face ID sensor and camera for the Duo, which would be placed on the outer display. He said this is one of "a few different features that Apple is already looking at for the iPhone Duo 2 and the iPhone Duo 3."

Gurman has an impeccable track record when it comes to Apple scoops, and it sounds like Apple is already working on fixes for some of the issues early reviewers experienced with the new foldable.

"Perhaps the biggest issue for me was the lack of Face ID, which was replaced by Touch ID on the side button," Schroeder wrote in Mashable's iPhone Duo review. "The Touch ID works well, but you do need to navigate your finger to it often, and having the phone simply recognize your face as it does on other iPhones would be easier."

The iPhone Duo is available for preorder now and is set to be released on Friday, Oct. 23.

Chancellor Friedrich Merz received relatively new British Prime Minister Andy Burnham with military honors in Berlin.

During Burnham's visit, the new Treaty on Friendship and Bilateral Cooperation between the UK and Germany — signed last year by previous Prime Minister Keir Starmer — officially comes into force.

#947. James McClean: ‘Wearing the poppy was just never going to happen’

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

The former Republic of Ireland international on growing up in a community still reeling from the Troubles and the controversy that scarred his career

“The perception of me is that, basically, I am a madman,” James McClean says calmly. “I’m outspoken. I’m a loudmouth. I’m very opinionated and I hate all things British. That’s from people who don’t know me. The reality is quite the opposite. I’m pretty boring. I go training, go home and switch off. A lot of my days are quite bland, quite basic.”

McClean sounds as sane as he looks gaunt on a bleak Monday in Derry. He spent 14 years in English football and the fact that he is now at war with his own club, Derry City, can’t obscure the fact that he is the fifth-most-capped Republic of Ireland international, having played 103 times. “People define success in different ways,” he says, “but I’m the only footballer from the north of Ireland to win over 100 caps for Ireland. I played 150 Premier League games over five seasons, 800 professional games and won a lot of individual accolades. I count that as success and don’t look back with regrets.”

But there’s sadness, too, in the way his life and career were scarred by his refusal to wear the poppy when playing games around Remembrance Sunday in England. “It’s completely changed me as a person,” McClean says. “It’s made me a bit of a recluse because I don’t trust a lot of people now. I’ve been stung bad.”

McClean’s autobiography, The Walls, is one of the best sports books of the year. It’s a powerful and sometimes disturbing read elevated by McClean’s willingness to show himself in an unflattering light.

I have written a lot about Derry and boxing, and Bloody Sunday, but it helps to be back here to understand how McClean can respect the millions of soldiers who died in two world wars while refusing to forget how the British army killed so many Irish people in this city.

He comes from Creggan, the estate affected most on Bloody Sunday in 1972 when 14 unarmed civilians were killed by the British army in Derry. Six of the fatalities were from Creggan. In 2010, after the Saville report finally concluded that all the victims posed no threat, the British prime minister David Cameron apologised for the “unjustified and unjustifiable” massacre.

McClean was born in Derry in 1989 and he writes that “learning how to make a petrol bomb was one of my first life lessons. I was 11 before I’d properly mastered it.”

He pauses when asked how he would feel if any of his three daughters or his son, who is 11, ended up making petrol bombs. “I’m very thankful they don’t have to. It’s a different era now. I just want them to be proud to be Irish.”

The 37-year-old spells out the contrast with his youth. “I grew up in a very Republican community still dealing with the effects of the Troubles. You would see gunmen on the street. People got shot, kneecapped. To get access to my street you had to go through a lane where you sometimes saw masked men doing punishment beatings. But you weren’t scared. You knew you’d done nothing wrong. That was the norm … but it’s not normal.”

His biological father, Joe Kelly, was an alcoholic in Creggan and McClean spoke to him only once – when he shouted at him in the street to stay away. But he remembers a happy childhood and, when his professional career began with Derry City, “I was very carefree”.

McClean was 22 when Steve Bruce signed him for Sunderland in August 2011. He made his Premier League debut that December after Bruce was replaced by Martin O’Neill. McClean soon became a regular starter and that first season in English football was full of joy and praise. He was fast, skilful and disciplined. Apart from wasting money his biggest vice was typing his name on Twitter where he would revel in glowing reports of his play.

“It was amazing,” McClean says. “I never thought about the politics when I first got to England so up until November 2012, when the poppy thing started, I never experienced any bother.”

Everything changed when he told Sunderland he wouldn’t wear the poppy because it also represented the British army which had occupied Derry for so long. “There was never a decision for me to make,” he says. “Wearing the poppy was just never going to happen. It was a very difficult year and I felt a lot of that could have been handled better by the club. But Sunderland came out with a statement before the game which drew all eyes on me. It was there to protect the club. They basically said this is James’s personal decision but we, as a club, wholeheartedly support the poppy appeal.

“I wanted to make my own statement but I was constantly told by the club that: ‘No, no, it’ll die over. Just leave it.’ I’d just turned 23 and I thought the club had a duty of care to look after me and that’s what they were doing. I’d won the player of the year the previous season but I felt they hung me out to dry.”

McClean recalls that: “The next game was away to Fulham on a Sunday, on Sky. A threat was made that I would be shot if I came on to the pitch. I remember [his wife] Erin panicking. It sounds extreme and people might say: ‘Oh, wise up, you’re not going to be shot on TV.’ But the night before the game I was assigned two police officers to stand outside my door. So the Met police took it seriously and that made it more real.”

O’Neill brought him on in the second half and his every touch was booed – by the Fulham and Sunderland supporters. The vitriol poisoned his world but McClean kept searching for his name on Twitter. He became furious whenever, after a mutually respectful encounter with a member of the public, the person would go online to abuse him.

Sometimes McClean would track the person down and demand an apology. “Maybe that madness was always there, but it got stronger,” he notes.

McClean moved to Wigan before joining West Brom where, in “the best kind of madness” he earned £42,000 a week while he had his best spell in the Premier League. But as one poppy season blurred into another, the death threats kept coming, as did the warnings that Erin would be raped. McClean says: “My reaction was always to fight fire with fire. I went into some games in the wrong frame of mind. It wasn’t: ‘Can I help the team?’ It was: ‘I hope I score today so I can shush them up.’”

McClean admits: “There were days where I went looking for the conflict.” He posted a photograph of himself listening to his favourite Wolfe Tones song, The Broad Black Brimmer. “I knew exactly what I was doing,” McClean writes. “It was an IRA song – and I was sure it would rile a certain type of person up.”

By the time he reached Stoke, for whom he played 111 games between 2018 and 2021, his problems in English football reached a new low. “Brexit made the idiots louder” and “the poppy season seemed to be getting longer every year”. He had also made a stupid mistake during lockdown when he posted a photograph of himself, wearing a balaclava, with his two eldest children below a caption which read: ‘Today’s school lesson – History.’”

McClean thought he was being funny but his choice not to wear the poppy, in contrast, was heartfelt. “I thought others might have followed suit but it never turned out to be the case.”

Did he feel that a more rounded understating of his stance had emerged by the time he enjoyed his happiest seasons in English football, while playing for a Hollywood-tinged Wrexham from 2023 to earlier this year? “A wee bit, aye. When you explain it to footballers you’ve played with, and to people on a personal level, it opens their eyes and they say: ‘Oh, I understand that.’ But the overall British population? I don’t think so.”

Reflecting on his Premier League years, McClean marvels at the brilliance of Kevin De Bruyne and Eden Hazard. “You had to accept there were lads capable of doing things that were beyond you,” McClean writes. “But there were also games where I thought I was coming up against a physical strength that I found suspicious. Carbonylation is an important process used to produce aldehydes, carboxylic acids and esters1,2,3,4,5 that is usually catalysed by homogeneous metal complexes with ligands and iodide promoters6,7,8,9,10,11,12. Koch has reported that protons of mineral acids can also catalyse carbonylation13. Previous studies have demonstrated that Brønsted acidic zeolites can act as a heterogeneous, Koch-type catalyst for carbonylation of alcohols and olefins, but the products in this case are limited to tertiary carboxylic acids14,15,16,17,18. #941. Early-onset cancer rising worldwide: Causes, risk, prevention

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Across Latin America, early-onset cancer rates have risen, especially of bowel, breast and cervical cancers. But it is a worldwide trend.

The numbers are striking. Some cancer rates have doubled in the past 20 years. It's just not yet clear whether we're seeing a rise in actual cases or whether we're seeing more early-stage cases because of better awareness, diagnosis and treatment in hospitals.

Take, for example, colorectal (or bowel) cancer: In 2001, about one in every 26,000 people under 50 in England were diagnosed with it each year. By 2023, the rate of diagnoses was about one in 12,000. Twice what it was at the turn of the century.

That's according to the World Cancer Research Fund (WCRF), an international network of charities, which analyzed registry data from England, the United States and the Netherlands. It found the number of new cancer cases had risen for seven cancers in England alone.

So, rates have doubled, but the numbers overall are still small, said Amy Berrington de Gonzalez, a professor of clinical epidemiology at the Institute of Cancer Research in London.

"Cancer is still very uncommon among young people," Berrington de Gonzalez told DW. "Three times a small number is still a small number."

But she and other scientists still want to know why this is happening, because it is a worldwide trend. Latin America is a case in point.

A 2025 study analyzed registry data from 50 countries and territories up to 2017. It found early-onset colorectal cancer rising in 27 of those regions.

In Latin America, rates rose in Chile, Argentina, Ecuador, Costa Rica and Puerto Rico. Chile's rise — at almost 4% per year — was among the highest.

In Brazil, the evidence comes directly from hospitals. At the A.C. Camargo Cancer Center in Sao Paulo, the number of colorectal cancer patients under 50 grew by 7.6% per year between 2000 and 2023. That's a five-fold increase.

The number of patients over 50 grew even faster — at 8.1% — or about six-fold.

But since these figures came from a single hospital, they do not show whether cases are rising across Brazil. They may only reflect that this one hospital is treating more patients.

"We need better cancer registration systems," said Berrington de Gonzalez, "especially in countries like Brazil and Mexico, where obesity rates have also been increasing."

Marc Gunter, professor of cancer epidemiology at Imperial College London, said he expected Latin America to follow richer countries as diets there become more Westernized and lifestyles more sedentary. "I think it's already happening," he said.

In another study in 2025, involving 42 countries, Berrington de Gonzalez compared data from two age groups — 20 to 49 years and 50 years and older — between 2003 and 2017.

Six cancers — thyroid, breast, colorectal, kidney, uterine and leukemia — had risen among the younger groups in most countries. All but colorectal cancer were seen to rise in older adults, too.

In fact, colorectal cancer stood out. In about a third of the countries, it rose faster in younger adults than in older ones. "It is the one cancer that seems to be increasing in younger people whilst decreasing in older people," said Berrington de Gonzalez.

This has consequences for research. If cancers are rising in all age groups, the causes are likely to be widespread, so studies looking only at young people risk chasing complicated explanations with too little data to test them, she said.

A US study of pancreatic cancer in adults aged 15 to 39 years found the rise was almost entirely made up of early-stage tumors.

The authors said this suggested that instead of the number of cancer cases going up, it was the number of early diagnoses that had gone up. And that can be explained by better awareness in the community, with people getting checked more often.

But the data for colorectal cancer looks different: Some US studies show it rising in advanced stages of the disease as well, which suggests an increase in the number of cases, not just the number of diagnoses.

Obesity is an established cause of at least 13 cancers, according to the WHO's International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC).

According to a 2021 review of existing studies, young adults with obesity were almost twice as likely to develop colorectal cancer before the age of 50.

Obesity has also spread fastest among the young. The WHO reports that obesity rates have doubled among adults since the 1990s and quadrupled among adolescents.

Some also say ultra-processed foods and microplastics cause cancer. But Gunter said the evidence was inconclusive.

"There's a lot of hype," Gunter said, adding that more research was needed. Berrington de Gonzalez agreed: "Diets have changed, but we don't yet know the role they might play."

Gunter said individuals should not be blamed for living in societies built around convenience foods and desk jobs, and that lasting change needed public health policy.

But he also said that young people could lower their cancer risk by maintaining a healthy weight, moving more, eating more fiber and cutting back on processed meat.

"Going for a walk, being more physically active, eating more fruits and vegetables," he said. "Those things are maybe not so trendy, but they are really important."

For a 30-year-old reading this, Berrington de Gonzalez's message was one of reassurance: Cancer at a young age remains relatively rare.
