#3442. Republicans are spending millions and still may not win districts drawn to favor them

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

North Carolina Republican congressional candidates state Rep. Jennifer Balkcom and Laurie Buckhout (right) are embraced by President Trump during an election rally on Oct. 20, 2026, in Greenville, N.C. Buckhout is running in North Carolina's 1st District, which was redrawn to heavily favor Republicans and is now a toss-up. Anna Moneymaker/Getty Images hide caption

📲 Get the latest on the midterms & more in the NPR App

Starting last year, President Trump and his political team put pressure on Republican-led states to redraw congressional district lines to favor Republicans. It was supposed to build a firewall for Republicans hoping to avoid the historical pattern of heavy midterm losses for the president's party.

"It just has not turned out that way," said Nathan Gonzales, editor of Inside Elections.

Instead, Republicans are spending millions of dollars on House seats that were supposed to deliver easy wins and may end up losing them anyway.

Among those districts is North Carolina's 1st Congressional District, which the state legislature aggressively redrew late last year to try to make it a reliably Republican district. Under the new lines, Trump would have won the district by more than 11 percentage points in 2024. Now it is widely considered a toss up.

Trump recently headlined a rally in eastern North Carolina where former Republican Party chairman turned U.S. Senate candidate Michael Whatley got most of the attention.

It was cold and getting late when Trump finally got around to mentioning two congressional candidates sitting in the VIP section.

"Come up. The two of you, we have to get them in," Trump said of Laurie Buckout, the Republican nominee in NC-01 and Jennifer Balkcom, the Republican running to represent NC-11 on the opposite side of the state. "We have to vote for them. These are two special people. Come on up."

On stage, Buckhout, a retired Army colonel, who ran and lost in the district two years ago, leaned into the partisan advantage Republicans should have in the district, saying it's "simple math."

"There are more of us," said Buckhout. "All you've got to do is get out the vote. God bless you. Mr. President."

But concern about turnout is exactly why Trump was in the state, begging voters to cast their ballots as if he were on it. Trump told the rally crowd that House Speaker Mike Johnson had asked him to give an extra boost to Buckhout and Balkcom, describing the situation as "tough."

"He said 'if you could, give them a special call out tonight, because they're going to have close races'," Trump said.

Buckhout and her team didn't respond to requests to participate in this story. But, if this redraw was meant to notch an easy Republican win, it hasn't worked out. This is now one of the most expensive House races in the country, with more than $42 million spent on advertising between Sept. 1 and Election Day, according to an NPR analysis of AdImpact data.

A volunteer snaps a photo of Democratic Rep. Don Davis with Onslow County Board of Commissioner candidates Rebecca Cooper and Michel Leandre Jr. outside an early voting location in Swansboro, N.C. Onslow is one of six counties added to North Carolina's 1st District in the latest redraw of congressional district lines. hide caption

One recent morning, Democratic Rep. Don Davis, was greeting voters at an early voting location in Swansboro, at the far southeastern tip of the district, an area he hasn't represented before.

As Davis introduced himself to a voter walking out of the polling place, the man told him he wasn't in his district.

"Oh, I'm sorry," Davis said, laughing and noting this has been happening to him a lot lately.

"This is my third run on a third new map," said Davis.

Davis has been a top target for Republicans in multiple rounds of redistricting. This time his home county was drawn out of the district and he got all or part of six new counties. He says issues in eastern North Carolina, though, are universal.

"You hear so much about affordability, inflation and costs but I believe this is becoming for many about survivability," said Davis, referencing farmers, truckers and fisherman hit by high fuel prices. "Just how they're able to make it and survive during these tough times."

Davis is a happy warrior, even joking about his predicament with a volunteer handing out flyers in front of the polling place.

"I just don't know how many other ways you could redraw it now, other than maybe, you know if you just start annexing Virginia or something or maybe taking it into the Atlantic Ocean or something," he said adding with a laugh, "I'll just swim and talk to the fish you know."

Republicans and Democrats alike acknowledge Davis is a formidable campaigner, making this race more competitive than it should be just based on the way the lines were drawn.

"I think they believed that they had beaten Don Davis with the redistricting pen," said Morgan Jackson, a prominent North Carolina political consultant, who is running Democrat Roy Cooper's U.S. Senate campaign. "As it turns out, Don Davis has always been a tough out. He's a local politician who runs really strong."

Incumbent Democrats in several redrawn districts around the country decided to stay in and fight and now look well positioned to win, said Inside Election's Gonzales.

"Republicans gained an advantage in 15 congressional districts nationwide but what we are seeing this close to Election Day is that some of those that were supposed to be easy victories are not turning out that way and the party is having to pour in millions of dollars to win districts that were supposed to be easy victories," said Gonzales.

A Republican strategist involved with midterm races, but who wasn't authorized to speak publicly, pushed back on the idea that the redistricting push has been a bust, saying "redistricting creates new battlefields, it doesn't create outcomes." That's a lot less confidence than Republicans were projecting about these districts just a few months ago.

Two Florida congressional districts redrawn to boot democratic incumbents are nearing a combined $90 million in ad spending. In Texas, districts drawn to have a 10-point Trump advantage have shifted towards Democrats and a previously safe Republican district that had some Democratic voters added to it in the redrawn maps now appears to be at risk as well.

"I think at the end of the day, this will go down as a dummymander rather than a gerrymander," said Jackson.

#3430. Court ruling fails to end wait for thousands of immigrants stuck abroad without visas

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Gloria and Dustin Hoffman take a selfie with their kids. Gloria Hoffman hide caption

Late last year, Dustin Hoffman and his wife Gloria started the paperwork to secure a visa for her to live in the U.S.

The couple, along with their 5-year-old daughter Chaya and Hoffman's 12-year-old step son Israel, lived together in Argentina, where Hoffman had moved in 2013 after doing a deployment with the U.S. Army in Iraq.

"My daughter is growing up and she's very smart and my stepson he's very smart," Hoffman said. "But the resources in Argentina for an autistic child and the school system in Argentina … I don't know. I just feel like I needed to give my daughter the same opportunities that I had growing up."

But nearly a year later, the kids and Gloria are stuck in Argentina in bureaucratic limbo due to continuing delays in the processing of their visa.

That's because this January the Trump administration banned the processing of immigrant visas for citizens of 75 countries. Many of the migrants were already in the process of getting a visa. Some already had the visa approved; they were just waiting to get the physical visa delivered to them.

Hoffman's wife is from Colombia, one of the countries on the list.

A federal judge this August ordered the ban to be lifted, but Hoffman's family still has not gotten their visa, and the State Department continues to ask them to provide additional evidence to prove they have the financial means to live in the U.S. without becoming a public charge.

Attorneys representing migrants say the Hoffman family's case is emblematic of a wider problem: They say the Trump administration is not complying with the federal court order and is instead slow-walking the processing of the visas.

"So while technically the ban doesn't exist, it doesn't matter practically if people are still not being able to get their green card interviews or have their interviews not be finished and not get their immigrant visa stamps foils and be able to come into the United States," said Alexis Axelrad, the president elect of the American Immigration Lawyers Association.

The visa ban affected more than 280,000 immigrants who had applied for visas by the time the policy went into effect in January.

According to an analysis by the libertarian think-tank Cato Institute, the ban targeted thousands of close family members of U.S. citizens and permanent residents, including spouses and minor children. The Cato report called it "one of the most extreme legal immigration policies in U.S. history."

Now stuck in their countries unable to come to the U.S., many are rethinking what to do.

The State Department declined to comment for this story, telling NPR "the matter referenced is subject to pending litigation." On its website, the State Department acknowledges the visa ban is no longer in effect due to the court ruling.

This photo taken on June 3, 2026, shows an entrance to the U.S. Department of State building, in Washington, D.C. Li Rui/Xinhua via Getty Images hide caption

The agency told NPR in August it had paused the processing of visas as it launched an in-depth "training program" for embassies and consulates on identifying immigrants who could become a burden on taxpayers — a public charge.

This means even immigrants with visas approved would have to go through additional screening.

This pause is uncommon, a former State Department consular officer told NPR. She asked not to be identified because she fears it could affect her job prospects.

"I've never seen a substantial pause on visa processing … I really haven't," the former consular officer said. "The department is doing this because this is what the policy is from the president … to reduce illegal immigration, remigrate people and only accept refugees from South Africa."

But families such as the Hoffmans are stuck in limbo. He says he misses his daughter, stepson, and wife every day. He said his daughter cries "all the time" and asks for her father. His wife is overwhelmed.

"Let me put it this way: Our family is a family. We need to be there for each other," Hoffman said. "Just like any family, it's not functioning very well while we're separated."

During his first term, President Trump said he wanted to attract the brightest immigrants to come to the U.S.

Right-wing organizations, including the America First Policy Institute, have also endorsed a "merit-based" immigration system.

"America's immigration system should prioritize immigrants with education, skills, and well-paying jobs waiting for them in the U.S. because these individuals are most likely to contribute to America's society and economy, benefitting all Americans," the organization wrote in March 2026.

But the battle over visa processing has affected many immigrants who fall into the high-performing category.

One of them is Dr. Fernando Lizcano, a Colombian national whose research focuses on major cardiovascular health challenges in the U.S., including obesity.

He applied for an EB-1A visa, which is reserved for immigrants "of extraordinary ability," including "an outstanding professor or researcher," according to the U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services.

"My intention is to continue with my academic biomedical research in the United States," Lizcano told NPR from his office in Bogotá. "(I) have been exploring opportunities also where I could combine all my experience in endocrinology with molecular biology, translational research."

Because USCIS deemed him qualified for an EB-1A, all that was left for him was to apply for the visa itself and get it stamped in his passport.

But he is still in Colombia, awaiting for the State Department's final approval.

The waiting game is taking an emotional toll on many of the immigrants — and their loved ones — whose visas are now up in the air.

Hoffman, the U.S. veteran awaiting his wife's visa to be approved, is worried about his own mental health. He told NPR his doctors have increased his psychiatric medicines.

"I was OK before all this started happening," he said.

He works as an Uber driver, and with Trump's war in Iran, gas prices have skyrocketed, cutting into his income.

He's struggling to pay both his mortgage in the U.S., and rent in Argentina for his family.

"We're living on a dream and a prayer right now," Hoffman said.

#3421. Loggers say Roadless Rule repeal won't solve their biggest challenges

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Logs from a salvage timber sale after a recent wildfire in the Willamette National Forest in Oregon. Kirk Siegler hide caption

EUGENE, Ore. - President Trump is widely expected to soon open up some 44 million acres of the country's last pristine national forests in a move he says will bring timber jobs back to struggling small towns and help prevent wildfires in overgrown lands.

Trump signaled the move with executive actions last year aimed at dramatically boosting timber sales on U.S. Forest Service land and reversing the agency's 25-year-old Roadless Rule, which blocked most new logging on undeveloped national forestlands.

Today with the formal repeal of the Roadless Rule rule expected early next year, the picture on the ground in timber country looks messier. Those who make a living in the forests say the problems are much bigger than access to more trees.

"I'm not saying these EOs [Executive Orders] are useless, they set the tone," says Sean Smith, CEO of the family owned Starfire Lumber in Cottage Grove, Ore.

Smith, standing in a bustling lumber yard with the sawmill cranking behind four story high stacks of freshly cut timber, says the industry has more pressing challenges now, starting with the record high price of diesel fuel. Economists largely blame that on Trump's war with Iran. And then there's inflation, which is driving up the costs of just about everything.

"Steel, you know, we have to buy sawblades, we have to buy ball bearings," Smith says. "We have to buy a million different parts to keep this mouse trap going."

So repealing the Roadless Rule - which has largely banned new road building and logging projects on some 44 million acres of pristine national forests since 2001 - is lower on the priority list.

Sean Smith, owner of Starfire Lumber in Cottage Grove, Ore. says opening up local roadless forests to forest health projects could create a more reliable local supply for mills. Kirk Siegler hide caption

Smith, a burly man with a dry sense of humor and a neatly trimmed salt and pepper beard, does support the reversal saying it will eventually give industry a more reliable local timber supply. He says a lot of the nearby Willamette National Forest, roaded or not, is dangerously overgrown. That built-up fuel makes these forests more prone to catastrophic wildfires.

"This is not a 'gee I sure want more cheap timber volume,' you know, this is not me promoting an agenda," Smith says. "This is a guy who lives here and is tired of seeing these beautiful places burn up."

Trump says a boom is coming but the industry is a fraction of what it was

The administration alongside the timber industry and some local officials are pushing for the repeal, saying it will give the Forest Service more flexibility to carry out wildfire prevention, including tree thinning, on land that's largely functioned as wilderness since 2001.

But under recent Republican and Democratic administrations, the Forest Service has struggled to get those projects going on forests that already have roads. Lawsuits and red tape are an ongoing challenge, as is finding enough workers.

"Right now, there's not a ton of people that want to wake up at 2 a.m. and drive a log truck for the same price that you could go work at McDonald's for," says Jeff Brink, a second generation logger near Eugene, Ore.

Brink says the industry is a fraction of what it was when his dad and uncles started Brink Land Development in 1981. The company is getting by ok, for now he says, due to a steady stream of forest restoration projects on private land as well as some recent salvage logging timber sales from nearby wildfire burn scars.

But for many smaller, less established loggers already struggling, Brink says, labor shortages and high diesel prices could be the tipping point.

"For an industry already operating on low margins, this is a tough one," Brink says.

Due to tightening environmental laws and other market forces including cheaper Canadian lumber imports, timber sales on U.S. federal land dropped by close to 90% since the 1990s. Brink says today the infrastructure just isn't there for the forest work that needs to be done, let alone a promised timber boom.

"Rural Oregon is struggling and all the mills that aren't along the I-5 corridor or near urban centers that can source wood are closing," he says.

Since the 1990s, an estimated 400 mills across the broader Northwest have closed, amounting to the loss of about 35,000 jobs. Seven mills have closed in Oregon since 2024 alone.

Industry experts project that overturning the Roadless Rule could lead to a five to ten percent boost in federal timber sales. That's hardly enough to boost much enthusiasm on the ground.

"We're in an industry that for decades has been in a state of contraction," Brink says.

Several national environmental groups have also already signaled they plan to sue once the administration formally repeals the rule, which is expected to happen by early next year.

Some fear a revival of the bitter Timber Wars

Timothy Ingalsbee turns his hybrid station wagon onto Forest Service Road 2408, near the scene of an infamous year-long tree sit and blockade that he helped lead against a Forest Service old growth timber sale in the nineties.

"The industry [back then] was headed for a crash, they had exhausted the supply," Ingalsbee says, as he stops the car and gets out at a vista.

Today, his hair cropped shorter and greying, Ingalbee notes that many of those towering, old growth trees, their bark cloaked in moss, have burned in recent wildfires. Ingalsbee, a retired Forest Service firefighter, today runs a national firefighter safety and advocacy group.

"The claim that they're going to do all this new road building and logging in roadless areas on behalf of wildland firefighters, and make it more efficient for them, is just a big lie," Ingalsbee says. "Not in our name you don't."

Retired U.S. Forest Forest Service firefighter Timothy Ingalsbee stands by a road in the Willamette National Forest, which was once the epicenter of the so-called Timber Wars. Kirk Siegler hide caption

Ingalsbee fears that punching even more roads into these forests will lead to more human-caused wildfires, which have been rising steadily for decades. But he doesn't think any of this is really about wildfire prevention or creating more logging jobs.

"What's driving a lot of Trump's executive orders for public lands management is not real economic drivers. I don't hear industry clambering for this," he says. "It's more ideology."

For Ingalsbee, the U.S. government is just racing to open up as much of the West as possible so industry can squeeze out maximum profits on timber, oil and coal.

Everyone is tired of policies 'whipsawing' from one president to the next

One thing that almost everyone on the ground in the Northwest agrees on is that Congress has largely failed to act on either protecting or opening up federal forest lands to more development.

When President Clinton signed the Roadless Rule by executive order at the end of his second term, Republicans controlled both the Senate and the U.S. House where appetite for passing more wilderness legislation was slim. The rule was the culmination of a sweeping inventory of land conducted by the Forest Service in which the agency had marked certain forests as prime acreage for wilderness protection.

Congress never codified it, and since then, the administrative rule has become an environmental flashpoint in a polarized country.

Back at Starfire Lumber, CEO Sean Smith is tired of federal forest and timber policy whipsawing from one presidential administration to the next.

"Sometimes the cynical side of me says, 'hey, what could be better than a do-nothing Congress at least they're not screwing anything up," Smith says, with a sardonic chuckle. "But that's no way to run a country long term."

Not to mention, he adds, it makes for a hard environment to keep a business going in.

#3412. Special ed diaries: Teachers tell NPR about their most joyous, most difficult moments

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Top row (from left): Danalyn Garcia of Chicago Public Schools, Lucie Lane of Shawnee Mission School District in Kansas, Jeff Carroll of Newport News Public Schools in Virginia and Kaori Anderson-Walker of Boys' Latin of Philadelphia Charter School. Bottom row (from left): Paul Stone of Mt. Diablo Unified School District in California, Diana Swanson of Vail School District in Arizona, Kathleen Tejeda of Alvord Unified School District in California and Emily Ellis of Lexington School District Two in South Carolina. LA Johnson/NPR hide caption

One of Kathleen Tejeda's students began freshman year just trying to get by. High school can be tricky for any teenager, but she was navigating it as a blind person.

By the end of her junior year, with support from Tejeda and other school staff, the student had gone from getting by to going big: She made the cheerleading squad and the school choir. She even sang the national anthem in front of the whole school.

Tejeda, who has been a special educator for 20 years in Riverside County, Calif., said that kind of advocacy is just as much a part of her job as teaching her students. But teachers like her can be hard to find.

There is a shortage of special educators in 45 out of 50 states, according to the Learning Policy Institute, an education research organization. That shortage is growing because the number of special education students is climbing: It's now more than 8.2 million — about 1 out of 6 public school students.

While turnover worsened sharply after the COVID-19 pandemic, the shortage isn't new. It has existed since special education became embedded in federal law with the passage of the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act in 1975. Half a century later, a fundamental question remains: Why is this shortage of special education teachers so persistent?

Let's start with a common misconception: The kids are hard to work with, so teachers don't want to do the job. "That is not the case," said Liz Bettini, a former special education teacher who is now a researcher and professor at Boston University. "That has not borne out in our research at all."

What Bettini's research does show: Special educators can feel isolated from other school staff and feel they have too many student needs to manage. The volume of required paperwork can be crushing. These educators are often responsible for teaching across several subjects and grade levels. Many feel they don't get paid enough to do this essential work.

"That's a very heavy weight to carry as a teacher," Bettini said.

NPR set out to hear directly from about a dozen special educators from around the United States' public schools to learn what it takes to do their jobs day to day. They have been sharing snippets of their lives through voice memos since the start of the school year, and some will open their classroom doors to NPR in the coming months.

"No teacher does this for the paycheck," said third-year high school teacher Diana Swanson, who works in Vail, Ariz., as she sat at her desk the day before her school year began in July. "Just the students, that fulfillment, seeing them fill their cup a little bit, seeing them smile, be a member of society and part of their community. That's what fulfills me."

"I feel like I was coming into this school year more prepared this time around," fourth-year elementary school teacher Danalyn Garcia said late one school night from her living room in Chicago. "Yet, I feel the needs are even greater than the year before."

"Honestly, I had no life outside of school and outside of being a teacher," said Kaori Anderson-Walker, looking back on his first year of teaching middle school in Philadelphia. Now in his fourth year, he said he has finally learned to manage his time more effectively but said the work is still never-ending. "When my students aren't performing where I want them in the classroom, my mind is just constantly moving."

Being an educator of any kind is demanding and requires immense skill. Research shows that burnout among all kinds of public school teachers is high, but it's even more acute for special educators. These teachers work with students who have an incredibly diverse set of needs, including those with autism, learning disabilities such as dyslexia, developmental delays and physical disabilities.

Special educators also do a lot more than teach — in fact, one study found that they spent only about a third of their day on instruction. They spend the rest of their time tracking student goals, coordinating specialized services for students (say, speech therapy or extra reading help), training support staff and writing individualized education programs (IEPs), which are legal documents that track each special education student's needs at school.

In many states, that means teachers must have specialized certification and training, but they typically aren't paid more. In 32 states, teachers get no extra pay for the hardest-to-staff positions, including in special education, according to a recent study.

While all 13 special educators NPR has spoken to said building relationships with students is their favorite part of the work, they also described the realities of those students' needs being more complex than those of many general education students.

"Special educators have reported, 'I can't teach this kid and respond to the student's crisis simultaneously. So I'm abandoning instruction because the crisis has to take priority,'" Bettini said.

For example, Grace Fitzgerald, who teaches elementary school in Peoria, Ill., shared on a drive home from school that one of her students had an emotional crisis that forced her to evacuate the rest of her class for their safety. "It's been challenging mentally and emotionally to kind of go through that at the very end of the first quarter, my first year of teaching," she said. "No one gets a road map to deal with that kind of situation."

Several teachers shared that behavioral challenges with individual students can make it harder to meet the needs of their many other students.

"Our school doesn't have walls. So we have six classes per grade level in one ginormous room, and it's separated by dividers," Garcia, in Chicago, said. "So when one kid is screaming and crying and begins to escalate, it disturbs the whole pod and then other kids react to it." Getting students back on track can be difficult in these kinds of situations, she said.

Individualized education programs are a key part of special education. These written plans, required by federal law, are managed by teachers and other school staff and serve as a kind of road map for each special education student. IEPs outline the student's unique needs and their annual goals. For example, a goal of a student with autism may be to maintain eye contact for a certain period of time.

IEPs help families hold schools accountable, and they also help enforce a primary aim of special education: to teach students in the "least restrictive environment," meaning students with disabilities are taught alongside students without disabilities as much as possible. But IEPs also require regular meetings with families, an understanding of special education law and time spent away from teaching students — time that teachers often don't have.

Then there's the work of putting the IEP into practice in the classroom. Tejeda, of Riverside County, said when she's "pushing in" to other teachers' general education classrooms, she provides targeted support without singling out special education students. "We're going to walk the classroom and check in with everybody, but at the back of our minds, we know that we need to make sure we're [making] accommodations for that particular student."

For Anderson-Walker, in Philadelphia, "the biggest struggle" was feeling like he had to juggle IEP paperwork with teaching duties. Then, last year, his school, Boys' Latin of Philadelphia, hired staff members who are responsible for the paperwork part of IEPs, leaving teachers more time for the classroom. "Not having that stress is amazing," Anderson-Walker said.

In addition to the workload, at the core of the special education teacher shortage are pay and the sheer number of qualified people. Research shows that paying teachers more can help attract and retain them.

For example, in 2020, Hawaii raised special education teacher salaries by $10,000 annually. That cut the state's vacancies by 35%, but it wasn't enough to keep many special education teachers in their jobs. Seventeen states now pay teachers additional money to work in hard-to-staff subjects, including special education. That's down from four years ago, when 22 states did so.

The pipeline of qualified special educators is another big problem. According to education policy researcher Chad Aldeman's analysis of federal data a few years ago, about 46,000 special ed teachers leave public schools every year, while teacher preparation programs train fewer than 30,000 annually to replace those teachers.

Aldeman calls it a crude estimate but said that the rapid increase in demand for special education teachers is outpacing the supply of people trained to do those jobs. Many schools end up resorting to hiring teachers who aren't fully qualified for the work.

But research has found that "grow-your-own" programs that focus on recruiting people who already live in the community may be effective at filling special education jobs. Bettini and her research partners also found that supportive administrators and adequate planning time can help make the work feel more sustainable for teachers.

For example, Fitzgerald, the first-year teacher in Illinois, said she was feeling overwhelmed by her workload at the start of the year. So she worked with her school leadership to build more dedicated planning time into her day.

"I think that's going to be very beneficial and will hopefully lead to a decrease in time spent after school," Fitzgerald said. She uses that time to focus on lesson plans, logging data and writing IEP goals.

It allows her to be more present in the classroom, working with her students.

#3401. How AI is transforming hurricane forecasts

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

A satellite image provided by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) shows Hurricane Isaias approaching the Gulf Coast of Alabama and Florida on Friday, Oct. 9 2026. NOAA/NOAA hide caption

Hurricane forecasters are increasingly using artificial intelligence to make predictions — and they say the technology is rapidly transforming the field.

When the first hurricane of the Atlantic season formed this month, an AI model beat out traditional models at predicting where it would go.

In early October, Hurricane Isaias made landfall near Destin, Fla., as a Category 2 storm. Conventional forecasting models initially predicted it would head further west, toward southeastern Louisiana.

But Google DeepMind's artificial intelligence model called it right out of the gate: the hurricane was going to hit the western Florida Panhandle.

"Google DeepMind, in particular, has really proven itself the last few years as the gold standard model," says Michael Lowry, a longtime meteorologist and hurricane expert in Miami, who has previously advised both the Federal Emergency Management Agency and the National Hurricane Center (NHC).

"That model has been, by far and away, the best performing computer model that we've ever seen. Period."

The National Hurricane Center first started incorporating the Google DeepMind model into its official forecasts last year.

By the end of the 2025 Atlantic hurricane season, it was already one of the best at predicting a storm's path and strength, says Brian McNoldy, a hurricane researcher for the University of Miami.

Usually, new models take years to refine after first being introduced.

"It's something we're not used to – to have a model, the new kid on the block, very quickly becoming the best model out there," McNoldy says. "This pretty much came out of the gate in first place, and that's a pretty impressive feat."

Traditional physics-based hurricane models use real-time atmospheric data, like temperature, humidity, pressure and wind shear, as measured by satellites and aircraft. Then they use complicated equations to predict what path a storm might travel and how strong it could get.

An AI model operates very differently: it relies more on history. It identifies patterns by analyzing thousands of past storms along with atmospheric data.

"The AI models don't really know anything about the atmosphere other than the current picture," Lowry says. "Beyond that, it's not leaning on the governing equations of the atmosphere, the physics, to make predictions."

Most of the people designing and developing these models aren't even meteorologists, Lowry notes: "They're software engineers, and they're developing neural networks."

A "neural network" is a set of computer algorithms modeled on the human brain and trained to find patterns in massive amounts of data.

As a result, the models can seem like a "black box," Lowry says: "We don't know why they might get a forecast right. We also don't know why they might get a forecast wrong."

It can be unsettling for meteorologists to depend on a system they don't totally understand – especially during storms when millions of people might be relying on their forecasts.

"It makes us a little uncomfortable, because we like to know why you're getting the right answer," McNoldy says. "And I feel like we don't – or at least I don't know why."

Not all AI models have performed well, though. There are lots of unproven weather forecasting AI models out there, Lowry says.

The two that have so far proven to be the most accurate, despite some pitfalls, are the models developed by Google DeepMind and by the U.K.-based European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF).

People retrieve belongings from a collapsed boat house in the aftermath of Hurricane Isaias in Destin, Fla., Saturday, Oct. 10, 2026. An AI model from Google Deepmind beat out traditional physics-based models at predicting the path of the storm, though it didn't do as well in forecasting the storm's strength. Gerald Herbert/AP hide caption

Forecasters at the National Hurricane Center use several proven models – both AI and traditional – when putting together their official forecasts.

"Every storm has different forecast challenges," says NHC Director Michael Brennan. "That's why we take that consensus approach and use that blend of the best models."

And it's still humans who have to interpret the results of those models before deciding what to tell the public.

The NHC provides updates every six hours during active hurricanes. The models by themselves can sometimes show drastic changes from one update to the next. Forecasters have to translate those shifting predictions into clear guidance that people can act on.

#4109. A Massive Seaweed Belt Crosses the Atlantic. Scientists Are Rethinking What Feeds It

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

New studies point to ocean nutrients and recycling within the floating ecosystem.

In May of 2025, satellites captured a staggering view from space: an estimated 37.5 million metric tons of floating brown seaweed stretched across the Atlantic Ocean, from the coast of West Africa to the Gulf of Mexico. This is the Great Atlantic Sargassum Belt (GASB), a recurring mass of pelagic algae that first appeared in satellite records in 2011.

“Floating Sargassum, though intriguing in its own right, is no more than a surface outcrop of a great oceanic phenomenon,” wrote oceanographer John Ryther in 1956. At the time, sargassum was largely seen as an ecological curiosity, associated with the nutrient-poor gyre of the Sargasso Sea, adrift in a blue desert.

In a sweeping 2025 review published in Harmful Algae, researchers at Florida Atlantic University’s Harbor Branch Oceanographic Institute compiled four decades of data—including historical oceanographic observations, satellite imagery, field experiments, and chemical analyses—to understand one of the most dramatic marine transformations in recent memory: the rapid rise and explosive spread of pelagic sargassum.

“Our review takes a deep dive into the changing story of sargassum—how it’s growing, what’s fueling that growth, and why we’re seeing such a dramatic increase in biomass across the North Atlantic,” said Brian Lapointe, Ph.D., lead author and research professor at FAU Harbor Branch, in the university’s August 2025 announcement.

The GASB made its first truly dramatic appearance in 2011. It has returned nearly every year since (except in 2013), although its size varies from year to year. The belt spans thousands of kilometers, but it consists of scattered clumps and mats, with open water between them. It is not a continuous carpet.

The belt appears to have been seeded, at least in part, by an atmospheric anomaly: the negative phase of the North Atlantic Oscillation in 2009–2010. This may have nudged sargassum southward from the Sargasso Sea into tropical waters. But genetic and morphological data suggest that some sargassum, especially Sargassum natans var. wingei, was already living in the tropical Atlantic before 2011.

The consequences are visible—and costly. Coastal areas in the Caribbean, Gulf of Mexico, and West Africa have reported waves of rotting sargassum choking beaches, emitting noxious hydrogen sulfide gas, and disrupting local economies.

Sargassum’s success essentially comes down to access to nutrients, especially nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P). Experiments dating back to the 1980s showed how quickly the seaweed can grow when those nutrients are available.

Controlled experiments show that under nutrient-rich conditions, Sargassum natans and Sargassum fluitans can double their biomass in just 11 days. Growth is even faster in “neritic” waters—shallow, coastal zones—than in the open ocean.

Between the 1980s and 2020s, the nitrogen content in sargassum tissue increased by 55%. Phosphorus content dropped slightly. As a result, the nitrogen-to-phosphorus (N:P) ratio jumped by 50%.

“These changes reflect a shift away from natural oceanic nutrient sources like upwelling and vertical mixing, and toward land-based inputs such as agricultural runoff, wastewater discharge and atmospheric deposition,” Lapointe added.

His team linked some bloom variations to Amazon floods and droughts. But that interpretation is not settled, and a correlation does not establish how much of the belt’s growth river nutrients actually sustain.

A November 2025 study in Nature Geoscience offered a different explanation. Using chemical records preserved in corals, researchers connected bloom variability to phosphorus brought toward the surface by equatorial upwelling, the rise of deeper ocean water near the equator. This supply can also support microbes that turn dissolved nitrogen gas into forms living things can use. Together, the two processes could provide much of the seaweed’s nourishment.

Experiments reported in Marine Environmental Research in 2026 added another piece. During a 2024 research cruise, scientists grew sargassum with different nutrient sources. Water collected below the ocean’s surface mixed layer produced the strongest growth; Amazon-plume water and Saharan dust produced limited growth under the tested conditions.

The experiment shows that sargassum can rapidly exploit subsurface nutrients. It does not establish their contribution across the whole Atlantic, or rule out important river inputs elsewhere.

Satellite imagery and ocean circulation models reveal that the Loop Current and Gulf Stream ferry sargassum from the Gulf of Mexico into the Atlantic basin. Back in 2004 and 2005, researchers observed huge mats, called “windrows” in the western Gulf, where nutrient levels had spiked from rivers like the Mississippi and Atchafalaya.

Yet the seaweed is not increasing everywhere. A December 2025 study found a sharp decline in the north Sargasso Sea since 2015. The authors proposed that reduced delivery from the Gulf of Mexico, possibly linked to warming and marine heatwaves there, could help explain it. The Atlantic’s sargassum habitat appears to be shifting as well as expanding.

The sargassum itself can recycle nutrients. Microbial breakdown and animal excretions within the mats help maintain localized nutrient supplies, allowing sargassum to persist even in nutrient-poor areas of the ocean. This ability helps the GASB survive and expand far from shore.

An April 2026 study in Nature Communications suggests this recycling has become increasingly important. Fish, shrimp and other residents gather food from surrounding waters and release nutrients around the mats. A model combining ocean mixing with this ecological contribution reproduced much of the observed bloom variability, including in 2023 and 2024, which were excluded from its training data. Chemical measurements supported the proposed recycling mechanism, although its estimated dominance remains a model-based interpretation.

Sargassum provides an important habitat. Its floating rafts shelter and feed hundreds of marine species, including young sea turtles. NOAA designates sargassum areas as Essential Fish Habitat in several U.S. regions.

But too much of a good thing can become a marine hazard. Large coastal accumulations can smother corals and seagrasses. As the seaweed decomposes, it can deplete oxygen in surrounding water and release hydrogen sulfide, the rotten-egg-smelling gas that can irritate people’s airways.

The USF bulletin covering September 2026 illustrates the seasonal rhythm. Estimated biomass across the belt fell by roughly half from August, yet most monitored regions still held unusually large amounts for September. A seasonal decline does not mean the recurring problem has disappeared.

The 2025 review’s authors called for coordinated international monitoring systems, better forecasting models, and long-term strategies to reduce nutrient runoff.

The newer evidence makes the task more complicated. Cutting pollution can protect coastal waters, but it should not be presented as a demonstrated way to switch off the entire Atlantic bloom. Managing these influxes will also require understanding how currents, deeper-water nutrients and the organisms living among the seaweed interact.

The belt is changing the ocean. Scientists are still working out which changes keep the belt alive.

One of the biggest uses for chatbots is searching for information, so it isn’t surprising to see people using ChatGPT, Claude or another AI tool to learn about their candidates and ballot measures. But we know that generative AI tech has some serious risks, such as bias, sycophancy, hallucinations and drawing incorrect conclusions using bad information.

But if you’re going to use chatbots for your election research, make sure you take these precautions. You can also check out CNET’s guide to spotting political scams and deepfakes.

This is good advice for any conversation you have with a chatbot, but it’s especially important for election research: Verify that AI is telling you the truth.

Chatbots have come a long way since the original ChatGPT launched in 2022, but they still mess up. They work by pulling information from the web (or its database, if it isn’t web-connected) and synthesizing it. But along the way, facts can be scrambled in the AI blender.

Chatbots can also hallucinate, or make up information. This is less of a concern with national election campaigns, which have a big online footprint that you can easily check against. But if you’re looking for polling locations or what your ballot will look like, you’re better off going to your state or local government’s election website to find that information. You should also check a chatbot’s sources. For example, if you ask about a candidate’s position on data centers, see if the chatbot is citing a video clip of the candidate speaking, a news interview or a random post on social media.

Remember in 2024 when Google’s AI Overviews told people to put glue on pizza to help the cheese stick? That was based on a facetious Reddit post, and at the time, the AI couldn’t spot the joke.

A recent study from MIT Media Lab found that just as GPS can weaken our navigation skills, using AI systems to verify facts made people worse at identifying misinformation on their own.

But chatbots are not neutral platforms — no technology made by humans is. The AI models that run chatbots are coded by humans and, therefore, they’re encoded with human biases and can further harmful stereotypes.

“It’s hard to imagine what perfect political neutrality about a candidate or political issue would even look like in a personalized conversation, so while neutrality is an admirable goal, I’m skeptical of what it means in practice,” Linzer says.

But just hours later a No10 spokeswoman told reporters that no decision had yet been made because the Government was 'not at the stage where we have concrete proposals'.

Mr Burnham told ITV's Good Morning Britain he was 'not proposing any changes in this Parliament' - but refused to rule it out at the next

'The Prime Minister isn't proposing a return to freedom of movement, or any option,' she said, despite him using a weekend interview with the Sunday Times to roll the pitch for it.

'He is saying that it's time for us to discuss what kind of long-term relationship with Europe is best for Britain and he is referring to the types of areas that that agreement and long-term future could cover.'

The Prime Minister this morning ruled out making the seismic change in this parliament – which is due to end in July 2029 at the latest.

But he pointedly refused to rule it out after an election if Labour retains power, setting up a potentially blockbuster election when it happens.

Mr Burnham again dampened speculation there would be a snap election, saying he did not expect to go to the country 'any time soon'.

But when it does happen it is shaping up to be a major moment for the country, with Mr Burnham pinning a shopping list of huge changes on keeping his job.

Mr Burnham told ITV's Good Morning Britain he wants to see Britain rejoin the EU 'in [his] lifetime', but claimed he would not 'move ahead of the public'.

'We have a decade of experience now looking back at Brexit. I don't think it has played out as people said it would at the time. We got a harder Brexit. It has weakened our economy.

'I am not proposing rejoining in this Parliament. I said we wouldn't re-run the referendum. That's not what I'm doing. The British public will be the bosses here.

'I don't think things have worked out as they said they would at the time, particularly when it comes to immigration.'

But Tory Alex Burghart, the shadow Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster, said: 'The Prime Minister's latest remarks are further proof that he has completely abandoned the people of Makerfield. He is devoid of any ideas for how to take this country forward.

'Rejoining the European Union will not cut the ballooning welfare bill, reduce government borrowing, fund our armed forces or help control immigration. The Prime Minister cannot keep looking backwards.'

It came amid reports Eurosceptic ministers will be told to back rejoining, with freedom of movement, if that is what Mr Burnham decides to do, or lose their jobs.

Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy and Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood are among those who cautioned against a return to the bloc, with Ms Nandy saying in May that it was 'a world away' from what the public wants.

'Essentially that is saying to people in towns like mine in Wigan that we just need to go back to 2015 when everything was fine,' she told ITV News at the time.

'Everything was not fine then. We've got serious action that we need to take as a country to make sure that the economy, housing, public services work for people in a way that they haven't done for a really long time.'

But yesterday, Housing Secretary Angela Rayner told BBC One's Sunday with Laura Kuenssberg: 'I want to see the best deal for the people of my constituency and for Britain.

'We haven't got the best deal at the moment because we've seen exports reduced by 20 per cent, we've seen the red tape and the friction that has meant that a lot of our businesses are not able to trade in the way that they would like with our biggest trading partners.

'So, I would like us to have an honest conversation about what the trade-offs could be and then put that to the people at a general election.'

On re-adopting free movement rules, Mr Burnham told the Sunday Times that the nature of immigration into the UK had been 'different' since Brexit – with more foreigners moving permanently rather than returning to their home countries.

Reports today also suggest Mr Burnham will expect ministers previously critical of rejoining the EU to back doing so if he decides it is in the country's best interests, even if it also includes freedom of movement.

The Prime Minister wants to consider various options for a new relationship with Brussels, potentially including a return to full EU membership or joining the single market – which would require free movement.

Mr Burnham will meet EU chiefs including European Commission president Ursula von der Leyen in Brussels on November 20 as he seeks to strike a better deal with the bloc.

But he also wants a discussion about the long-term future of the UK-EU relationship, ten years after the country voted for Brexit.

The Prime Minister was asked on Monday morning if he would allow the UK to join the EU freedom of movement scheme without a referendum.

He said: 'No, I mean I wasn't saying that yesterday. I was asked about how things have changed in the 10 years since Brexit, and the truth of the matter is we've lost immigration control in the way that we once had.

'For instance, the small boats crisis has arisen in the last 10 years because we lost that returns agreement when people came here that we could work with other countries to return them.

'And when it comes to immigration more broadly, we're seeing a situation where people are coming here to settle permanently in a way that we had more revolving door migration in the EU.'
