#3384. Israel expands expulsions in occupied West Bank to cement control

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Israel escalates forced expulsions and infrastructure destruction with the goal of imposing a ‘new reality’, analysts say.

More than 3,000 Palestinians have been forced from their homes in the occupied West Bank city of Hebron as the Israeli army escalates a sweeping military operation that human rights experts describe as a calculated effort to permanently displace the Palestinian population.

Israel has imposed a lockdown across the city and detained at least 71 people over the past four days, according to the Palestinian Prisoner’s Society.

Hebron Governor Khaled Dudin said on Monday a total of 488 families comprising 3,171 people had been displaced across the wider governorate, while Mayor Yousef al-Jabari told Al Jazeera that 3,043 of those forced from their homes are from within Hebron city limits alone.

Separately, the Israeli military continues a demolition campaign that has resulted in the destruction of residential buildings, factories and roads, while also blocking roads, raiding homes and assaulting Palestinians.

The crackdown has been in place since Friday, when two soldiers were killed in a shooting near the city. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has justified it by pledging an operation “unseen” since the 1967 war.

It comes amid increased tensions across the occupied West Bank as Israeli settlers and soldiers engage in violent pogroms. Attacks on olive harvesters have been rife as aggression has been on the rise before Israel’s general election on Tuesday, in which Netanyahu is seeking re-election.

According to municipal authorities in Hebron, the majority of the forcibly displaced Palestinians are women, children and elderly individuals.

Israeli soldiers, meanwhile, have systematically converted dozens of homes of the uprooted Palestinian families into military barracks and field interrogation centres.

“The measures taken in the city of Hebron are not of a single type, but rather have surpassed any idea of temporary evacuation or deportation,” said Noman Amr, a professor of contemporary history at al-Quds Open University, Dura branch in Hebron, as he explained the multifaceted nature of the displacement. “Evacuating homes falls into three categories: evacuation for demolition, evacuation for revenge and collective punishment, and evacuation for military uses, where they have turned some homes into military barracks.”

Amr also highlighted the psychological warfare at play. “This aggressive, collective punitive, terrorist, racist behaviour aims to create a state of panic, fear and instability for the Palestinian society,” he said, quoting displaced residents who said they heard soldiers make comments such as, “You will return to your homes on Judgement Day”.

The ultimate goal, Amr added, is “to impose a new reality” that can be summarised “in one sentence: ‘more land, fewer Arabs'”.

Israeli officials explicitly linked the Hebron operation to previous campaigns in the occupied West Bank. Netanyahu and Defence Minister Israel Katz announced their intention to apply the “Jenin camp model” to Hebron, which involves forced evacuation of residents, inspecting homes, confiscating weapons and bulldozing roads.

Rights groups have compared the current attack on Hebron with the tactics used during the “Iron Wall” operation in early 2025 in the Jenin, Nur Shams and Tulkarem refugee camps, which killed at least 102 people, displaced more than 33,000 and destroyed over half of their residential buildings.

Katz previously said the aggressive tactics in Jenin represented “the first lesson from the method of repeated raids in Gaza”, where Israel’s genocidal three-year war has killed more than 74,000 people and left the vast majority of the territory in ruins.

The resulting destruction left the Jenin camp “nearly uninhabitable”, according to the United Nations agency for Palestinian refugees, UNRWA.

Last month, a UN Human Rights Office (OHCHR) report warned that the Israeli military’s actions at the three camps “appear to amount to collective punishment and raise concerns of ethnic cleansing”. Displaced Palestinians reported being told by Israeli officers that there would be no more refugee camps and that they should “all go to Jordan”.

Israeli forces killed at least 21 children during the operation, including a 10-year-old boy who was shot while waiting for his father and a two-year-old girl killed while having dinner with her family.

Analysts also pointed to the systematic destruction of vital infrastructure during the Hebron operation with the goal of making displacement permanent.

“Targeting the main infrastructure by the occupation army forces is a targeting of the life and stability of the Palestinian human being,” said Amr. He specified that targeting water wells “exceeds their direct material value, but rather relates to existence, stability and continuity”.

Amr also outlined the deep collaboration between the Israeli army and armed settler groups. “Settlers today in Hebron and other places have a militia consisting of more than 150,000 people heavily armed with modern automatic weapons and four-wheel drive vehicles, and they sometimes wear the occupation army uniform,” he said.

“The army closes the place, empties homes, demolishes them, and closes roads … while settlers continue their repeated attacks on the residents, released prisoners, and commercial interests.”

This ground-level violence is paired with administrative land grabs. The Colonization and Wall Resistance Commission (CRRC) said on Sunday the Israeli military had established 220 barriers across Hebron, severing Palestinian communities from their lands while maintaining connectivity for illegal settlements. The CRRC noted this is part of a broader network of new outposts designed to expand control over the spaces separating settlements and to besiege Palestinian expansions.

Also on Sunday, the Israeli army said in a statement that it would bar Palestinian workers from entering most areas of the West Bank through Monday and enforce a full closure of crossings into and out of the territory on Tuesday, the day of Israel’s parliamentary election.

Mayor al-Jabari accused Netanyahu and his hard-right government of exploiting the military operation to secure settler votes.

Amr echoed this sentiment. “The Palestinian pays this price as electoral propaganda for the right wing,” he said.

“They do not want Palestinians to remain in their places, but rather they propose a more advanced step, which is that they want to correct the history that allowed Palestinians to stay after the 1967 setback or even after the 1948 Nakba,” he added, using the Arabic word for “catastrophe” to refer to the ethnic cleansing of Palestine nearly 80 years ago.

#3386. Steinmeier in China: A major state visit in uncertain times

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

President Frank-Walter Steinmeier is traveling to China on a state visit in a period of political and economic uncertainty. Germany is seeking alternative partners in the region, but also wants good ties with Beijing.

Eight years have passed since Frank-Walter Steinmeier's last trip to China. Since then, the relationship between China and Europe has changed significantly. The German president plans to spend several days touring China before concluding his visit in Beijing, where he will meet with China's leaders.

His visit comes just as the Chinese Communist Party's Central Committee is convening for its plenary session, a major annual event that foreign dignitaries usually avoid when timing their visits.

The focus of the president's five-day trip will be "Germany's diverse and complex relations with China, as well as the challenges involved," his office said in a statement. In addition to sites of cultural exchange, the itinerary will also include a visit to a Volkswagen plant in Hefei.

The automotive industry, in particular, illustrates the dilemma facing Germany's China policy. China is one of the most important markets for German automotive and machinery manufacturers, as well as for the chemical industry. At the same time, state-supported overcapacity in China is putting pressure on European manufacturers.

Not only that, Germany and Europe are dependent on China for raw materials and technologies, which means they are increasingly seeking out alternative partnerships.

Steinmeier was once an advocate of the policy of "change through trade" — the idea that other countires could be persuaded to become more democratic with the leverage of global trade, now considered something of a failure.

Now he must now pursue a tougher European line in Beijing. Caught between partnership and competition, Europe leaders recently managed to reach an agreement: Imports of plug-in hybrid vehicles from China are to be scaled back.

"That was a concrete step, but it can only be the beginning," said Michael Laha, an expert on China at the German Council on Foreign Relations (DGAP) in Berlin.

Coinciding with Steinmeier's state visit to China, Chancellor Friedrich Merz is traveling to the Indo-Pacific with business representatives. His visit to Japan, Singapore, and South Korea will focus on competitiveness, technological upheavals, defense cooperation, and critical raw materials. Merz is visiting companies and attending important regional economic conferences in Tokyo and Seoul.

The dual trips are Berlin's attempt to send a twofold message: While the chancellor is "deliberately strengthening Germany's partnerships in the Indo-Pacific at a time of global upheaval," says Laha, the president is seeking dialogue with Beijing at a time when Germany and Europe are recalibrating their relationship with China.

"This joint trip sends a very well-coordinated message to the Chinese that we remain committed to the partnership but at the same time are taking steps to reduce risk," said Laha, before adding that this is a difficult balancing act that also allows China to save face.

While Merz will be accompanied by a large economic delegation, Steinmeier will be traveling to China with representatives from German culture, politics, and academia. Mikko Huotari, director of the China Institute MERICS, is also expected to be part of the delegation — even though Beijing has imposed sanctions on the institute. "The fact that he is coming along sends a clear message to the Chinese side that we do not approve of the sanctions," said Laha.

In 2021, the EU imposed sanctions against several Chinese party and regional officials, as well as an organization accused of serious human rights violations and oppressing the Uyghur minority. It was the first time such sanctions had been imposed on China since the Tiananmen Square massacre in 1989, and Beijing did not take kindly to them: In response, the Chinese government imposed entry bans and other sanctions against European politicians, scholars, and institutions, including MERICS.

Laha does not expect human rights to play a major role in the German president's trip. Steinmeier is considered a pragmatist when it comes to China policy. As foreign minister, he distanced himself from his predecessor Joschka Fischer, a critic of China, and even criticized Chancellor Angela Merkel when she hosted the Dalai Lama.

On the subject of Ukraine, Steinmeier is likely to be more direct and raise the issue of China's role in the conflict. "Beijing is clearly on Moscow's side," said Laha. In Berlin, however, officials are convinced that China can do more to persuade Russia to engage in serious negotiations and find a solution to the conflict.

Steinmeier may make this clear in his speech at Peking University on Thursday. Germany has "long viewed China's emergence with respect and goodwill," reads a statement from the president's office. Today, however, the focus is less on China's continued rise and more on how it uses its growing power.

"If the president points out that Russia used its power to attack a sovereign state, he could be alluding to another regional connection: Taiwan," says Laha. That could be seen as an appeal to Beijing to assume more responsibility for the regional and international order.

To conclude his visit, Steinmeier will meet with China's leadership in Beijing. Talks are planned with President Xi Jinping, whom he has known for nearly two decades, as well as with Premier Li Qiang and the chairman of the National People's Congress, Zhao Leji.

The trip marks the culmination of Steinmeier's focus on Asia during his second term in office. Since then, he has deliberately sought to forge close ties with partners in the region and has advocated for greater diversification. Now China – the most important and most challenging partner – will come to its own conclusion.

#3965. Fanatics teams up with OpenAI for new AI-powered shopping suggestion feature

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

At any given moment, Fanatics offers more than 1.5 million unique SKUs for sports fans to buy — from jerseys and hats to team-branded streetwear and socks. The search for those items is getting easier with a new feature called "Shop the Look," created with the help of OpenAI's Codex.

Fanatics' product assortment reflects its rise into a massive sports-industry platform, with deals with nearly every major sports league and team worldwide. Each year, Fanatics Commerce manufactures more than 45 million items and sells them to customers in more than 180 countries.

But Justin Tsai, chief product officer for Fanatics Commerce, said that growth has also created a challenge for the company.

"One problem we just kept seeing was that our customers come to our sites, and we operate hundreds of these sites … their challenges are that they're always trying to look through our very large catalog to figure out what they can buy," he said.

Tsai said customers' shopping sessions routinely ran over 30 minutes on average, and that time kept compounding as Fanatics released upwards of a thousand new products each week.

"Shop the Look" suggests coordinated outfits on each product page, matching those items with other in-stock items based on team and intended style.

Traditional shopping curation strategies, like photo shoots and stylized outfits, have lead times that don't always work with Fanatics' rapidly increasing product pool, or for quickly-released items like championship shirts and hats, Tsai said.

Random suggestions don't work for sports fans either. "Nobody who is a Los Angeles Dodgers fan wants to be matched with a San Francisco Giants item … team and player alignment is extremely important in our world," he said.

Even as Fanatics has grown into a multibillion-dollar business, team merchandise remains its biggest revenue source. Fanatics founder and CEO Michael Rubin told CNBC's "Squawk Box" earlier this month that the company's commerce business will exceed $7 billion in revenue this year, compared with roughly $5.5 billion for its collectibles business, which includes Topps trading cards.

OpenAI has been experimenting with shopping tools for ChatGPT since the chatbot's launch, including a feature last fall that let users buy merchants' products directly within ChatGPT. However, it is ending the Instant Checkout effort and now is working directly with retailers to create dedicated apps within ChatGPT, according to CNBC reporting.

The new Fanatics feature is another way OpenAI is leveraging its technology across shopping. Rosemary DeArago, head of industry GTM at OpenAI, said this personalization issue in retail "is an age-old problem." While the rise of e-commerce gave retailers new ways to "figure out the fingerprint of the customer" — whether that's past purchase history, where they are viewing the website from, or a logged-in account — those efforts still sometimes miss the mark.

"In the past we were trying to stitch together something that would be good to show a customer," DeArago said. "With AI, you can do more deterministic matching to do personalization in this next-level way."

"Shop the Look" uses OpenAI's Codex to create a tool that parses product and catalog data to find compatible items and reevaluate recommendations, ultimately creating those looks that might suggest a hat, socks and other accessories to go with a jersey a fan is considering purchasing.

Tsai said that after rolling out the new feature, there was a 10% increase in views of recommended items. He also said the suggested items help shoppers have "more conviction" about the items they're thinking of buying, seeing how it may fit with other team gear.

DeArago said that while there will always be room in the shopping experience for more curated looks and trying items on in person, the integration of AI into efforts like this — where it can help narrow down massive product selections and show what might work with a valuable purchase like an authentic team jersey — shows that there is a "really big opportunity to enhance the existing experience."

#3399. How did Lula beat Bolsonaro after losing Brazil’s first-round election?

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Brazil’s president secured a historic fourth term after overturning a first-round defeat, but faces a conservative Congress and a legal challenge from his defeated rival.

Brazilian President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva has achieved a stunning election victory, defeating far-right rival Senator Flavio Bolsonaro, despite trailing him by more than two million votes in the first round held earlier in October.

The 80-year-old leftist secured an unprecedented fourth term on Sunday, becoming the first candidate since Brazil’s return to democracy in 1985 to win a presidential run-off after finishing second in the opening round.

“I just had to tell you that I love you. Tomorrow, the fight continues,” Lula told cheering fans at a victory parade in Sao Paulo. “No one is required to like the president-elect, but he must govern for everyone.”

Lula’s victory is a significant setback for the right-wing political movement that has gained ground across Latin America, often with the backing of United States President Donald Trump.

So how did Lula turn things around in just three weeks, and what challenges does he face?

Lula, who turns 81 on Tuesday, secured 51.79 percent of valid votes, or 64.26 million ballots, compared with Bolsonaro’s 48.21 percent, or 59.83 million votes, according to the final tally.

The result marked a dramatic reversal from the October 4 first round, when Bolsonaro won 47.03 percent against Lula’s 45.16 percent, a lead of approximately 2.2 million votes.

Turnout on Sunday was 80.8 percent, nearly 2 percent higher than the previous round, when 78.92 percent of the electorate cast their votes.

Bolsonaro refused to concede and called for the run-off result to be annulled after Supreme Court Justice Cristiano Zanin ordered voting to be extended by two hours. Both candidates had requested extensions, citing transport disruptions and severe weather.

The controversy echoes the aftermath of the 2022 election, when Flavio’s father, Jair Bolsonaro, refused to accept his defeat. He was subsequently convicted of plotting a coup and sentenced to 27 years in prison, which he is serving under house arrest.

However, experts say Flavio faces significant obstacles in overturning the result.

Vinicius G. Rodrigues Vieira, an economics and international relations professor at the Armando Alvares Penteado Foundation, told Al Jazeera that Bolsonaro’s legal arguments were weak, particularly because his own campaign had requested an extension.

“I don’t think that will resonate in the courts, but certainly will help Bolsonarismo, the far-right movement here in Brazil, to remain politically strong on the streets,” he said.

Lula’s comeback was driven by a revitalised grassroots campaign, youth mobilisation and a shift towards digital campaigning, analysts said.

Al Jazeera correspondent Monica Yanakiew, reporting from Sao Paulo, said young Brazilians spontaneously organised online after Bolsonaro’s first-round victory, creating what became known as “Lula’s army”.

“It was this army that went to the streets, millions of people across Brazil, and young people just mobilised themselves, energised the campaign, which frankly was not very energised at all before the first round,” she said.

Editor-in-chief of The Brazilian Report, Gustavo Ribeiro, told Al Jazeera that Lula’s campaign had initially appeared complacent.

“Lula’s campaign also got its act together after a first round in which it acted as if this election was in the bag and essentially running an anachronistic, mostly offline campaign,” he said.

“The campaign really pivoted towards a digital mentality and a grassroots effort,” Ribeiro said, adding that Lula had used “the power of incumbency very cleverly in his favour”.

“I would say this is more the merit of Lula, even if Bolsonaro just worked to run off the clock.”

Other experts agreed, saying Bolsonaro’s own mistakes were likely to have contributed to his defeat. Academic Vieira argued that the senator had become overconfident after winning the first round.

“In the first week, he was already behaving as if he were already the president-elect. He was appointing ministers,” the economics professor pointed out.

“If there is something Brazilian people don’t like [it’s] arrogance, so Mr. Flavio is paying a very high price for this,” he added.

The Brazilian Report’s Ribeiro also questioned Bolsonaro’s political appeal.

“He does not have his father’s charisma. He was essentially running on his father’s last name,” he said.

Higher turnout was another important factor.

An estimated 33.4 million Brazilians abstained in the first round, representing about 21 percent of the electorate. Abstention fell to approximately 19 percent in the run-off.

Fernando Brancoli, an international relations professor at the Federal University of Rio de Janeiro, said previously absent voters appeared to have participated in the second round.

“We already know that a large number of people who didn’t vote [in] the first round, they now showed up,” he told Al Jazeera.

Brancoli also suggested some first-round Bolsonaro voters may have switched to Lula, although detailed voting patterns remain unclear.

According to Vieira, the election was arguably one of the most consequential in Brazil’s history.

After the 21-year dictatorship ended in 1985, he said Brazil built a “constitutional setting” which provided social rights to the population more than at any time in the South American nation’s history.

“That’s so important for the Brazilian people because we still have high levels of inequality,” he said, noting that it was crucial to “keep a very high degree of political participation”.

In contrast, the far-right Bolsonarismo movement had tried to “curtail voting rights” by employing “voting suppression tactics”, Vieira said.

Despite his victory, Lula faces a conservative-dominated Congress that could severely restrict his legislative agenda.

Bolsonaro’s Liberal Party and its allies could control more than 280 seats out of 513 in the lower house, and up to 50 out of a total of 81 Senate seats, potentially giving the opposition majorities in both chambers.

Brancoli warned that Lula would have limited room to manoeuvre.

“We can definitely expect Lula’s government [is] not going to be able to push for a broad agenda. You’ll get small wins, and you’ll have to negotiate with those groups,” he said.

Economic pressures pose another considerable challenge. Brazil’s public debt has exceeded 82 percent of gross domestic product (GDP), according to the World Bank, while the government forecasts growth of just 2.3 percent next year.

Moreover, Brancoli asserted that Lula would also need to address security and household debt, two issues that helped make the election competitive.

The president’s age raises further questions about the future of his Workers’ Party (PT), and who would replace the longtime leader, Brancoli noted.

“There’s no one that could substitute him, and he’s older,” he said.

#3796. Arkansas in play?

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Arkansas Democratic Senate nominee Hallie Shoffner speaks with voters at a campaign stop in Hope, Ark. Friday, Oct. 23, 2026. Stephen Fowler/NPR hide caption

HOPE, Ark. — For Senate candidate Hallie Shoffner, the most important political label she wears isn't that she's a Democrat. Or that she's not a Republican. Instead, it's that she is an Arkansan.

As a lunchtime crowd ate at Tailgater's Burger Company Friday, Shoffner decried what she called a historically unproductive Congress driven by extreme voices in each party and pitched herself to receptive — if skeptical — voters as a candidate who would better serve their needs.

Arkansas is one of the most conservative states in the country, with nearly two out of three voters supporting President Trump in 2024 and Republican Sen. Tom Cotton in 2020.

But in the final days of the 2026 midterm election, Shoffner and other Arkansas Democrats are crisscrossing the state with a message that seeks to capitalize on the Trump administration's unpopularity, stubbornly high prices and anti-incumbency vibes.

"We're the sleeper race," Shoffner told NPR. "I mean, we're the race that no one's noticed and that no one is going to see coming."

Republicans have taken some notice, pre-emptively pouring millions of dollars into the Senate race, the Little Rock-based 2nd Congressional District and robust get-out-the vote efforts to try and stem the rising tide of dissatisfaction with the party.

But even in a midterm cycle where Senate races in states like Kansas, Iowa, Ohio and Texas are viewed as tossups, and where the landscape of potentially competitive House races stretches deep into conservative territory, Arkansas seems a bridge too far for election forecasters.

Arkansas' 2nd Congressional District is considered "Likely Republican," according to the Cook Political Report, and its Senate race is "Solid Republican."

Hallie Shoffner, the Democratic Senate nominee in Arkansas, has emphasized her farming background and belief that both parties have ignored the people's needs in the state. Stephen Fowler/NPR hide caption

At an outdoor coffee shop in Arkadelphia, Vanilla Hannah said she was there to support Shoffner and talk about her own reelection bid to be a local justice of the peace. She said it's possible that Arkansas could elect Democrats this year because the crop of candidates seems willing to work with people to meet their needs — and there are a lot of them.

"When it comes down to basic needs or whatever, the 'D' or the 'R' after your name don't mean a thing, because we're all suffering and all having the same issues and everybody's ready for a change," she said. "And running for an election is not just running specifically on the Democratic policies or the Republican or the libertarians or whatever. It's just what you believe in, and it seems like more and more people are having a common belief now."

Later, Shoffner visited a small family farm in nearby Bierne where several members of the Hatley family cooked sorghum to make syrup and talked about why they were supporting her campaign.

Shoffner is a multigenerational farmer who decided to shut down her family's operation in 2025, citing financial headwinds from rising input costs and Trump administration tariff and trade policies. That in turn inspired her Senate run and influenced much of her campaign's focus.

Sliding behind the wheel of a side-by-side utility vehicle, Shoffner takes off across the property and recounts her life story that led her away from the farm, into nonprofit work and back home when her dad got sick.

She doesn't buy the argument that her chances are low to win.

"This race isn't about Democrats or Republicans or independents or any of that," she said. "This isn't some kind of weird political alignment, this is a values alignment. This is reorienting representation in Arkansas based on common sense, based on compassion and based on showing up."

She said national Republicans — and Democrats — have made the mistake of ignoring the needs of Arkansas residents, especially with agriculture and trade policies.

"I come from a place, a community, that died in my lifetime," she said. "I was the last one left and I felt left behind. I felt like no one was really talking to me or talking about me. I felt like folks were making decisions about me, but without me, and ultimately they were rewriting the rules."

Now, with record diesel prices, the war in Iran and tariffs, Shoffner said she believes that Arkansas voters of all stripes want something different. She's also glad that the race hasn't seen an influx of outside air support from national Democratic groups.

"I don't need them, I really don't," she said. "This is an Arkansas First campaign, and I'm an Arkansan, and I want to talk directly to Arkansans, and I want them to know that when I go to D.C., the only letters that will mean anything next to my name are the "A" and the "R."

Chris Jones, the Democratic nominee for Arkansas' 2nd Congressional District, says voters are frustrated with promises made by the Trump administration that haven't been kept. Stephen Fowler/NPR hide caption

On the flip side, Chris Jones has the backing of the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee as one of dozens of "Red to Blue" flip targets.

At an outdoor jazz night held at a brewery in Little Rock, Jones worked the crowd between visits to polling sites and a high school football homecoming game on a campaign that has seen millions of dollars in outside spending by Republicans.

"They understand that people are in pain," he said. "Folks are hurting. They can't afford gas. They can't afford groceries. And our campaign has always been about affordability and accountability for all. So prices are way too high."

Jones also ran for governor against Republican Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders in 2022 and lost, so he started off with higher name recognition as a serious challenger to Rep. French Hill.

He knows it's a tall task, even in a national environment more favorable to Democrats. Hill is a longtime incumbent whose district only became more conservative after the last Census-led redistricting in 2021.

But he also knows that Democrats, Republicans and independent voters alike are open to looking for something other than the current leadership.

"Here's what's so fascinating: a lot of folks here voted for the president and this administration because they wanted lower prices, they wanted to end foreign wars, and they wanted to end corruption, particularly with the Epstein files," he said. "None of that has happened, so they're super frustrated."

Like many states that have seen the potential for close races next week, Arkansas has a longer history of single-party control that has led to anger and frustration at the status quo and incumbency in a way that extends beyond typical partisan labels or voting history.

"One of the things that we forget, because there's so much that comes out of this administration: It wasn't but 15 years ago that the entire federal delegation of Arkansas was Democrat," he said. "So it's not that long ago. The reason they got in office was because of the conditions that existed, the economic anxiety and pain that people feel — and folks feeling like what they wanted to happen out of Washington was not happening. We're in that same place now."

In addition to the DCCC support, Jones has had campaign visits from former DNC Chair Jaime Harrison, former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg and Rep. Jasmine Crockett of Texas. Illinois Gov. JB Pritzker and Kentucky Gov. Andy Beshear have both endorsed his run. And he's heard from people who don't normally support Democrats saying they'll back his campaign.

"Folks are literally hurting, and it's to the point where they're saying, 'my life is so bad that I don't care about party anymore'," he said.

Another factor working in Democrats' favor this election cycle is a surge in Black turnout following a Supreme Court decision that weakened part of the Voting Rights Act.

Multiple states across the South rushed to redraw House maps to gain a partisan advantage, with many of the new districts crafted in a way that minimized Black voting power. Arkansas lawmakers did not redraw their maps — the state has an entirely Republican congressional delegation — but after the 2020 Census did split Little Rock up in a manner that made Hill's district more Republican.

In primaries following the ruling, and early voting this month ahead of the midterms, there has been sustained higher Black turnout in states like South Carolina, Georgia and Louisiana in ways that could affect the outcome in key races across the region.

"I think part of it is that folks realized that there was an imminent threat to their right to vote," Jones said. "And people realized across the country that if we don't show up now, and Black folks realize if we don't speak up now, then our ability to do it later might not be there."

Marcus Hatley, one of the people who met with Shoffner at his family farm, said the increased turnout seemed like a "course correction."

"People are dissatisfied with the way that that happened, and some are crossing sides and voting in a different direction," he said. "And a lot of it has to do with removing, in some of those states, the ability for people of color to have equal representation in their districts. So it looks like to me there's going to be a course correction. I could be wrong, but I believe there's going to be a change."

#3742. TRUMP: I DON'T GIVE A DAMN ABOUT GAS PRICES...

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

President Donald Trump has said he does not "give a damn" about potential criticism of his remarks boasting that the United States is making a "fortune" from high oil prices, as American motorists continue to face elevated gasoline costs.

Speaking at a rally in Georgia on Saturday, October 24, Trump said advisers had warned him against highlighting the financial gains associated with rising oil prices, suggesting the comments would invite criticism over the cost of fuel for ordinary Americans.

In footage of the rally shared online, Trump claimed the US oil and gas industry was making billions of dollars amid the conflict in the Middle East, presenting higher oil prices as a source of financial gains for the country.

President Donald Trump speaks during a midterm campaign rally at the Guardian Centers on October 24, 2026 in Perry, Georgia.

"Our country is making hundreds of billions of dollars because of what's happened in the Middle East, because they're coming to Texas and they're coming to Oklahoma and they're coming to Louisiana and they're taking that oil," Trump said.

"My people say, please don't mention that, Sir, people are paying a little higher," Trump said, adding that he expected gas prices to fall.

"I can just see the fake headlines," Trump said. "President Trump is talking about all the money they're making while people are suffering at the pump."

"But I said, 'I don't give a damn, I'm gonna say it: We're making a frickin fortune,'" he added.

Trump went on to claim that Americans were "paying lower than Biden and Obama and they don't have nuclear weapons," apparently referring to Iran's nuclear capabilities.

Under Obama, the U.S. national average gas price peaked at $3.93 per gallon in May 2011, and under Biden, it peaked at $5.06 in June 2022, according to govargus.com.

There has been widespread backlash to Trump's comments online. California Governor Gavin Newsom reposted footage of Trump's remarks at the rally commenting: "Congratulations to Big Oil on their very successful, record-profits presidency."

Newsweek contacted the White House via email Sunday for comment.

The national average price of regular gasoline stood at approximately $4.29 per gallon on October 25, according to the American Automobile Association (AAA).

That was down from approximately $4.49 a month earlier but significantly higher than the $3.06 average recorded a year ago. The latest figures represent an increase of around $1.23 per gallon over 12 months, equivalent to roughly 40 percent.

AAA's reported national averages for other fuel types were approximately $4.80 per gallon for midgrade gasoline, $5.20 for premium and $6.35 for diesel.

Prices vary considerably across the country, with motorists in some states paying substantially more than the national average. The figures also illustrate the distinction between the financial benefits Trump attributes to the energy sector and the costs facing households at the pump.

Although gas prices had eased from their September levels, the year-over-year increase meant drivers were still paying considerably more to fill their tanks than they had a year earlier.

Trump has repeatedly pointed to the potential financial benefits of higher oil prices for American energy producers and oil-producing states.

But higher crude oil prices can also put pressure on consumers by increasing the cost of gasoline, diesel and transportation. The extent to which domestic producers benefit depends on factors including production costs, market prices and the wider economic effects of more expensive energy.

There have been numerous protests across the country protesting the Iran war and its impact on gas prices.

Signs protesting the Iran war and its impact on gas prices are seen during a protest organized by VoteVets outside of the U.S. Capitol on May 20, 2026 in Washington, DC.

As previously reported by Newsweek, nearly three-quarters of likely voters disapprove of how President Trump has handled gas prices, with the midterm elections days away and his overall approval rating at the lowest point of his second term.

A New York Times/Siena poll found that 73 percent of likely voters disapprove of Trump's handling of gas prices, compared with 24 percent who approve. The poll surveyed 3,494 voters, October 12-19.

No issue in the poll scored worse for the president, and the frustration runs well past Democrats: 77 percent of independents disapprove, along with 42 percent of Republicans and 43 percent of people who voted for him in 2024.

Massachusetts Senator Elizabeth Warren posted on X on Friday: "I'd like to report a fraudster in the Oval Office. Trump promised to lower costs on Day 1. But instead he launched a war that increased the prices of everything from gas to groceries."
