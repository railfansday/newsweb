#3482. Baby orangutans found in India expose illegal wildlife trade as experts question billionaire’s zoo

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Villagers who found five baby orangutans in a casuarina forest in India in September were puzzled by their reddish hair and unfamiliar faces.

Left on a plantation in Odisha, the three males and two females—aged six to eight months—were found over 2,700 kilometers (1,700 miles) from their native rainforests in Borneo and Sumatra, evidence of an expanding illicit trade of apes into India.

The young animals are now housed at an Indian zoo, where caretakers have reportedly been playing classical music to help soothe them.

Native only to the islands of Borneo and Sumatra, wild orangutans sustain rainforest ecosystems by scattering seeds across vast areas, enabling trees to regenerate and store carbon that would otherwise contribute to global warming. Driven by severe habitat loss, the species is critically endangered, with approximately 119,500 remaining in the wild according to the World Wildlife Fund.

The global illegal wildlife trade is estimated to be worth $34 billion annually, according to a 2026 study by researchers at the National University of Singapore, with India emerging as an important destination. Experts emphasize that confiscations reflect only a fraction of a booming trade in non-native and exotic animals sold to wealthy Indian collectors.

India’s wildlife laws were largely crafted to protect native species like elephants, rhinoceroses, and tigers from being trafficked overseas. Regulatory gaps in controlling imported animals complicate efforts to trace the origins of exotic wildlife.

Interceptions have grown frequent. In 2022, two juvenile Sumatran orangutans were found in boxes at a checkpoint between Assam and Mizoram. Another was rescued in Mizoram in 2023, and a young orangutan was found in an apartment near Mumbai in November 2024. At least 23 gibbons were seized at Indian airports in 2025, according to an analysis by the nonprofit Wildlife Trust of India, while Egyptian authorities intercepted three sedated, India-bound baby chimpanzees in May 2026.

"India has become the number one destination for the live trade of primates," said Sinan Serhadli, a wildlife trafficking expert at the Orangutan Information Centre in Indonesia’s North Sumatra province.

Wealthy collectors who prize rare animals as “status symbols” are driving the trade in trafficked animals from Southeast Asia, a regional hub for trade in species from elsewhere, said Uttara Mendiratta, a wildlife trafficking researcher in India.

Expanding tourism has led to more direct flights to India through cities like Bangkok and Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia, giving traffickers routes that are faster, albeit riskier, than overland border crossings.

“The profit margins are so high that they can afford to lose a few (animals) and still make a profit,” Mendiratta said.

Questions also surround legal trade. Most commercial trade in orangutans is banned, but exceptions exist for captive-bred animals. India imported 41 orangutans between 2021 and 2024, according to CITES, the global treaty regulating trade in endangered wildlife.

Sinan Serhadli, a wildlife trafficking expert at Indonesia’s Orangutan Information Centre, questioned whether the animals were legitimately bred in captivity.

“It just shows the loopholes. And ... corruption as well,” he said.

The CITES Secretariat declined to comment, saying the matter was under review.

Concerned about diseases spreading from animals to people during the COVID-19 pandemic, India allowed people to register their exotic animals in 2020 without proving their origins.

They can report births, deaths and breeding of non-native species through an online portal. But the government lacks enough staff and expertise to adequately inspect collections and identify global species. The system also leaves room for trafficked animals to be misrepresented as registered ones, Mendiratta said.

India tightened its wildlife laws in 2022, giving authorities clearer powers to seize illegally traded exotic animals and prosecute traffickers. But by then, it already had “opened the floodgates,” she said.

India’s exotic pet trade “sits at the crossroads of biodiversity loss, unbridled community demand, and organized criminal activity,” said a 2026 report by Humane World for Animals India.

India's forest ministry didn't respond to AP's request for comment.

Indonesia’s Ministry of Forestry said it was taking “active steps” to bring home the five baby orangutans found in Odisha, sending experts to verify their origins and assess their health.

Their mothers were likely killed, traumatizing infants that normally would depend on them for up to nine years.

Conservationists have successfully returned orangutans to Indonesia from Thailand, but so far only one has been repatriated from India.

When one orangutan was transferred last year to a private wildlife facility known as Vantara, run by billionaire Mukesh Ambani’s family, India’s federal wildlife officials and India’s CITES authority were not informed, a federal wildlife official to New Delhi wrote in a report.

Officials concerned over the orangutan's care at a local public zoo ordered it transferred to Vantara, which describes itself as a wildlife rescue and rehabilitation center. The orangutan was treated for a respiratory infection and pneumonia before it was returned to Indonesia on Oct. 19 after 19 months at the private facility.

India’s CITES authority didn’t respond to AP’s request for comment.

Vantara, a 3,500-acre wildlife facility in western India, has over 150,000 animals and over 2,000 different species.

It has faced international scrutiny over its animal acquisitions. In November 2025, CITES questioned shipments to Vantara that included snow leopards from Germany, a bonobo said to have been bred in Iraq and a gorilla reportedly from Haiti. The primates are not native to those countries.

India’s Supreme Court cleared Vantara of wildlife trafficking and animal mistreatment allegations in 2025.

In September, 27 wildlife conservation and animal welfare organizations issued a letter urging the facility to end imports of wild animals, raising concerns about endangered species and animal welfare.

The groups said they had “never encountered anything comparable to the scale and diversity” of Vantara’s imports, citing more than 60,000 animals acquired in a short period.

One controversial acquisition, according to Vantara's 2024-25 inventory, is a Tapanuli orangutan, the world’s rarest great ape. Recognized as a distinct species only in 2017, it's only found in a small area of Indonesia’s Sumatra island, where 716 remain in the wild.

“The Tapanuli orangutan is hovering just above extinction,” said Serhadli, whose organization is one of the few that works with the species. The one at Vantara would be the only Tapanuli orangutan in captivity.

CITES trade data shows that the animal left Indonesia in 2024 and arrived in India via the United Arab Emirates.

“That can’t happen legally,” Serhadli said.

Indonesia’s Ministry of Forestry said it was taking “seriously” reports that a Tapanuli orangutan might be outside the country. It said it couldn't confirm the animal’s species, origins or how it got to India. An investigation would require checking permits and transfer records, along with scientific testing, the ministry said.

Neither Vantara nor Indonesia’s CITES authority responded to requests for comment from AP.

#3703. Amazon pledges 30,000 entry-level UK jobs to young people by 2030

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

The Amazon Youth Promise pays from £15.10 an hour and asks for no CV. It comes as around a million young people in the UK are out of work, education and training.

Amazon has pledged 30,000 entry-level jobs in the UK by 2030 for young people from the areas with the fewest opportunities. The roles start at £15.10 an hour, more than £31,000 a year pro rata, and need no CV or work experience, Amazon said in its announcement.

The Amazon Youth Promise, announced on 20 October, offers a mix of permanent and seasonal fixed-term roles to 18 to 24-year-olds. The jobs are in Amazon’s operations network, picking, packing and shipping orders across its more than 100 UK sites. Private medical insurance and a pension start from day one.

“When I visit our sites, I meet young people who had no idea where to start six months ago. They didn’t have a CV. They didn’t have experience.” John Boumphrey, Amazon UK country manager

Amazon has signed the government’s Youth Guarantee and will work with the Department for Work and Pensions on its programmes. Around a million young people in the UK are not in education, employment or training, known as Neets. That is the highest rate in a decade, according to Amazon.

Alongside the jobs, Amazon will offer more than 10,000 work experience places by 2030. It plans to raise the yearly number from about 900 this year to more than 3,000. Its JOBS project with Barnardo’s will support 2,000 young people from 2027. It has also committed to more than 1,000 supported internships for young people with learning disabilities and autism.

From 2027, Amazon will hold recruitment events for young people in Merseyside, Stoke-on-Trent, Greater Manchester and South Yorkshire. Every unsuccessful applicant aged 18 to 24 will get a follow-up email with guidance and a link to reapply.

“It’s great to see Amazon joining the growing number of major businesses backing our Youth Guarantee to give every young person the chance to earn or learn.” Pat McFadden, Secretary of State for Work and Pensions

Days earlier, prime minister Andy Burnham warned of an “economic catastrophe and moral failure” over the number of young people out of work and training, The Independent reported. Former health secretary Alan Milburn, quoted in Amazon’s announcement, said the problem is opportunity, not aspiration.

“The first rung has thinned, and too many young people can’t get a foot in the door.” Alan Milburn

The pledge covers warehouse roles. Amazon has also been cutting jobs: it reportedly cut roles in its retail unit this month, after earlier layoffs. In September, it sold its first sterling bonds to help fund its AI spending.

#3507. Electric shocks to the brain stop people objectifying women, study finds

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Please refresh the page or navigate to another page on the site to be automatically logged inPlease refresh your browser to be logged in

Researchers say although findings are no cure for sexism, they give an insight into how objectification works

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

Mild electric shocks to the brain have been found to remove a bias that is linked to the sexual objectification of women, a new study has found.

Research, conducted by the University of Exeter, found that applying an electrical current to the scalp for ten minutes improved how well participants recognised upright sexualised images of women, and removed a difference in the way such images of women and men were processed.

It had already been established that people are much better at recognising faces and bodies when they are are upright than when they are upside down – whereas for objects it makes far less difference.

Studies have found that in both male and female viewers, this upright advantage shrinks when it comes to sexualised images of women but not men – suggesting they are processed more like objects.

Professor Ciro Civile, who led the study, said: “Sexual objectification means treating a person as a body to be looked at or used, rather than as a whole person. It’s dehumanising and most often experienced by women.

“Our findings are initial evidence that the way our visual system processes sexualised images of women can be changed, at least temporarily, by mildly stimulating the brain. This was a lab study – we didn’t measure attitudes or behaviour, and brain stimulation is no cure for sexism – but it gives us a new way to study how objectification works.”

Sexual objectification has been linked to a greater tolerance of harassment, as well body shame, eating disorders, anxiety and depression.

The study, which has been published in the journal Social Neuroscience, involved 54 female students and 22 males students, with participants asked to memorise sexualised images of female and models in underwear, both upright and upside down.

They were then asked whether they recognised the models when these were mixed in with unseen images.

Half of the group received a mild electrical current to the scalp for 10 minutes, while the other half were given a placebo treatment of just 30 seconds of stimulation. Neither the participants or the experimenters knew who was in which group.

In the placebo group, participants recognised images of male models much easier when they were upright, compared to them being upside down. For images of women, that advantage was significantly smaller, closer to the pattern seen for objects.

In the group who received the active electric brain stimulation, recognition of upright images of women improved, and the difference between how images of men and women were processed was no longer present.

Professor Civile said: “Until now, studies had reduced this bias by changing the images or the viewer’s mindset – for example, by pixelating sexual body parts, giving humanising information, or making people feel less powerful. We acted directly on the brain of the person doing the looking instead.

“Both groups saw exactly the same pictures, so the bias seems to lie in how we process the images rather than in the images themselves, which opens new avenues for understanding how gender-based biases arise in perception.”

#3372. What to know about China's plenum as Xi seeks stronger party discipline

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

FILE - In this photo released on July 18, 2024, by Xinhua News Agency Chinese President Xi Jinping speaks at the third plenary session of the 20th Communist Party of China (CPC) Central Committee held from July 15 to 18 in Beijing. Xie Huanchi/Xinhua via AP hide caption

HONG KONG — China's political leaders and elites began a four-day meeting Monday to discuss strengthening the ruling Communist Party's discipline, as Chinese President Xi Jinping pushes for greater loyalty and reliability from officials while deepening his yearslong campaign against corruption.

This week's closed-door gathering, known as the fifth plenum of the Central Committee of the Chinese Communist Party, comes roughly a year before Xi is widely expected to secure his fourth five-year term as the party's leader and personnel changes to key leadership bodies.

Xi began the meeting by delivering a work report and speaking about a draft policy decision on advancing "full and rigorous party self-governance," according to China's official Xinhua News Agency. The policy aims to make cadres more disciplined, effective and less corrupt.

While Xi has consolidated his grip on power, analysts say the plenum will offer him an opportunity to further align what Chinese officials do with what Beijing wants.

"The plenum is a chance to ensure the new generations rising the ranks share Xi's assessment of the opportunities and risks China faces," said Ruby Osman, a senior policy adviser at London's Tony Blair Institute for Global Change.

Here's what to know about one of Beijing's most significant political meetings this year.

Xi's anti-graft drive has been a defining feature of his rule since he came to power in 2012. Purges of officials — including some viewed as his political rivals — have been carried out on a scale that led critics to argue the campaign was also used to consolidate his power.

But his focus has expanded to cracking down on underperforming cadres too. In recent years, the number of officials punished for "formalism" and "bureaucratism" — misconduct such as dereliction of duty, inaction and reckless acts that seriously hinder development — has surged.

Xi's policy proposals reflect his emphasis on the party's "self-revolution," a concept centered on the party reforming itself through efforts such as rooting out corruption and building ideological commitment, analysts say.

Osman said the disciplinary crackdowns are now aimed more at institutional longevity.

"It's less about purging elite rivals and more about building a disciplined system that can sustain Xi's political priorities across leadership cycles," she said.

Record-high disciplinary campaigns also do not necessarily mean a rebound in corruption, but rather that Xi has been steadily raising the bar for the party's expectations of officials, she added.

Mingda Qiu, senior analyst of Eurasia Group's China practice, said Beijing has been building its agenda for months.

"The message is clear: the anti-corruption campaign is not winding down. Officials are not only tested for loyalty, but also for compliance and action," he said.

Xi said in a January meeting that while decisions by the party's top leadership were smoothly implemented in general, inspections and audits found problems such as failures to comply and improper execution, according to an October article in the Qiushi Journal, a publication of the central committee.

Xi warned against "two-faced people" who might profess political loyalty while privately defying orders and doing as they please.

"During leadership transitions, we must keep our eyes sharp, elevate good cadres who are truly loyal and reliable, consistent in word and deed, and willing to take responsibility, and never allow 'two-faced people' or those driven by self-interest to slip through," Xi was quoted as saying.

The number of empty seats in the conference hall this week will help observers gauge how widespread Xi's purges have been.

Neil Thomas, senior fellow at the Asia Society Policy Institute, and his colleagues Lobsang Tsering and Shengyu Wang noted that while Xi's civilian purges are record-breaking, 42 out of 44 officials affiliated with the military elected as full central committee members in 2022 have also been purged or disappeared.

The central committee started its term in 2022 with more than 370 full and alternate members. Thomas and his colleagues estimated turnout this week could be less than 80%, potentially setting a record low at any plenum since the end of former leader Mao Zedong's rule in 1976.

Attention will also be on new demands on the officials who remain.

Thomas expects tighter scrutiny of public spending and major projects, more use of technology to uncover corruption, and tougher consequences for officials who fail to deliver on Xi's priorities.

New personnel appointments are possible, though Xi may wait until a once-in-five-years Communist Party Congress next year to anoint his picks, said Steve Tsang, director of the SOAS China Institute in London. If he does fill any openings, it will be with loyalists, he added.

The underlying theme of the fifth plenum is that it will be another opportunity for Xi to position himself for an unprecedented fourth term in office.

Xi, who removed presidential term limits in 2018, is viewed by some as the most powerful Chinese leader since Mao. He is widely expected to be confirmed as party leader for the next term in the fall of 2027.

Ahead of that, Thomas said new ideological language reinforcing Xi's authority might emerge at this week's meeting.

He said the plenum should prepare the ground for Xi's fourth term and reinforce expectations of political and policy continuity.

"After Xi has purged so many of the people that he promoted at the last congress, watch for tougher scrutiny of the officials competing to join his next leadership team," he said.

#3659. Can AI help solve the COBOL code crisis?

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Posts from this topic will be added to your daily email digest and your homepage feed.

Posts from this topic will be added to your daily email digest and your homepage feed.

Posts from this topic will be added to your daily email digest and your homepage feed.

The ancient code holding the world together may meet its match in AI. Today is not that day.

Posts from this author will be added to your daily email digest and your homepage feed.

Posts from this author will be added to your daily email digest and your homepage feed.

What do the IRS, banks, airlines, and government infrastructure around the world have in common? They’re all running on systems propped up by antique computer code that nobody dares touch, lest everything come crashing down.

OpenAI thinks it may have a solution. On October 19th, the company touted “substantial improvements” in its latest AI models’ ability to migrate aging software written in older programming languages into modern code, which it claims could help organizations with “maintaining and modernizing older systems.”

That’s a potentially massive deal. COBOL, one of the languages highlighted by OpenAI, remains stubbornly embedded in vast portions of the world’s critical infrastructure. And despite decades of efforts to banish it to history, the language has limped on, simply refusing to die.

“Nobody in their right mind would use COBOL for a new development project.”

It is hard to overstate COBOL’s ubiquity. Short for “common business-oriented language,” COBOL came into being in 1959. Nearly seven decades later, it is still everywhere. According to IBM, COBOL is the foundation for more than 40 percent of online banking systems, supports 80 percent of in-person credit card transactions, and handles 95 percent of ATM transactions. It also underpins many government systems, from Social Security to the IRS to services run by most states. One rough estimate puts the annual cost of COBOL’s inefficiencies to the US GDP at around $105 billion.

But replacing COBOL is fiendishly difficult. The same goes for other legacy programming languages, like Fortran, still in use today for everything from scientific computing and weather forecasting to atmospheric and ocean studies and codes critical to nuclear security. These systems are often too important to risk breaking, and many of the programmers who understand them have long since retired or died.

For all their impressive advances in modern software development, AI coding tools have struggled to make much headway with these legacy systems. But recent developments suggest that may be changing.

In its latest blog post, OpenAI reported promising results migrating COBOL code to more modern programming languages, including C++, Java, Kotlin, Python, and Rust, without changing what the original software does. That last bit is crucial for organizations looking to ditch code like COBOL, and OpenAI acknowledges that “a migration is considered successful only if all tests pass.”

In OpenAI’s own tests, GPT-6.1 Sol achieved 87 percent behavior preservation accuracy at low reasoning effort — how many computational steps the model spends analyzing a problem before giving an answer — climbing to 98 percent at higher reasoning. By comparison, at low reasoning effort, GPT-6 Astra scored around 86 percent, GPT-6 Sol just 21 percent, and GPT-5.6 Sol a mere 5 percent.

“Rewriting code is not the same as replacing a system.”

“These evaluation results demonstrate progress toward more accurate, affordable code migration for teams modernizing the software their businesses depend on,” OpenAI wrote.

The company also reported improvements on its models’ ability to build database software in various programming languages, including COBOL.

Anthropic also sees an opportunity in legacy systems. In February, it published a step-by-step “code modernization playbook” and pitched its coding agent, Claude Code, as a way to migrate COBOL code into modern languages like Java or Python. It offered no performance benchmarks showing how well Claude Code could perform such migrations, but declared that “the economics of COBOL modernization have shifted.” Shares in IBM, which derives part of its revenue helping companies maintain legacy systems, nosedived following Anthropic’s announcement.

Experts who spoke to The Verge agreed that AI tools have made strides in tackling legacy code in recent years. But they cautioned that advances trumpeted by OpenAI this week are a very long way from making it possible to safely replace critical legacy systems built around it.

OpenAI spokesperson Oscar Haines acknowledged in an email to The Verge that legacy code “is still far from a solved problem” and said the company is listening to feedback from developers and enterprises and exploring ways to build better tests and continue improving its models’ understanding of coding languages. Anthropic did not provide comment in time for publication.

“The evaluations shown by OpenAI don’t address the key difficulty: Breaking up a complex legacy system into smaller units that can be replaced individually.”

“Rewriting code is not the same as replacing a system,” said Vadim Zaytsev, an associate professor of software evolution at the University of Twente in the Netherlands. Systems do have a lot of code — Zaytsev said the largest system he’d seen had 250 million lines of COBOL, “definitely not the world’s largest” — but that’s only part of the problem. Code may be written in multiple languages and there’s data, potentially stored in various formats, to consider, he said. There are also technical integrations with other systems and the expectations of the people and organizations to bear in mind.

All that complexity means “AI is not playing a major role at the moment in replacing or migrating legacy COBOL systems,” Zaytsev said.

OpenAI’s evaluations don’t capture this complex reality either, Arie van Deursen, a software engineering professor at Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands, told The Verge. He described the company’s blog as “superficial,” noting it offered few details about the migration task, while its language benchmark tested models on a relatively simple and well-defined task involving systems similar to those they would have encountered in training. He also stressed the huge gap between maintaining an aging system and modernizing it. “Maintaining is of course a lot easier than modernizing,” van Deursen said, calling the tasks OpenAI used to assess its models “lightyears” away from actual modernization. “The evaluations shown by OpenAI don’t address the key difficulty: Breaking up a complex legacy system into smaller units that can be replaced individually.”

The biggest challenge isn’t necessarily rewriting code. “The main challenge is indeed not understanding how the system works,” said Alastair Donaldson, a professor of programming languages at Imperial College London. Such a lack of understanding is a “very common” problem for legacy systems using languages like COBOL, he said, adding that very few people know how to program in them anymore. And while it is everywhere, Donaldson said “nobody in their right mind would use COBOL for a new development project.”

“In many cases even minor differences in system behaviour before and after migration are unacceptable.”

That makes robust testing crucial, Donaldson said. One approach could be to run the same workflows through the old and new systems to check they behave identically. AI could also document its code translation, ideally allowing humans familiar with the legacy language to review the work. Formal verification, which uses mathematical techniques to computationally verify that code behaves correctly, is another option, said Jacopo Mauro, a mathematics and computer science professor at the University of Southern Denmark.

Such methods are already used to verify AI-generated mathematical results, but applying them to legacy code would require a “huge amount of work,” Mauro said. Using those methods would mean code could be checked automatically, Mauro said, adding that “having this will be better than having a human check that the translation is correct.” Until then, he said, “human verification remains essential.”

And when the systems involved are as critical as those running on COBOL, experts say you need to be absolutely certain the replacement works. “In many cases even minor differences in system behaviour before and after migration are unacceptable,” Zaytsev said. While AI can definitely deliver savings and speed things up, the complexity of legacy systems means partial automation isn’t enough.

“Suppose you have a magic AI wand that handles 99 percent of your code,” Zaytsev said. “What do you do with the remaining 1 percent? That 1 percent can be a million lines of code, spread all over the place and subtly failing in more ways you can imagine within your lifetime.”

#4082. Here’s the difference between a $5 and Apple’s $129 USB-C cable

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

The difference can be startling. Is the price worth it though?

In late 2022, the European Union passed a landmark law requiring all smartphones and other electronic devices sold in the bloc to use USB-C for wired charging by the end of 2024. After years of resisting, Apple finally caved and swapped to USB-C.

But of course, none of this means that their USB-C cables are cheap. In Apple fashion, they are charging as much as $129 for a cable.

To be precise, that’s Apple’s 1.8-metre Thunderbolt 4 Pro cable. And it’s not the same thing as an ordinary USB-C charging cord. Apple’s range also includes a $69 Thunderbolt 5 cable that supports substantially faster connections, although it’s shorter.

Why on Earth would Apple charge that much for a USB-C cable when most calbes are dirt cheap? It’s tempting to accuse Apple of corporate greed, and there’s a reasonable chance greed is also involved. But as it turns out, the story is more complex.

Different standards govern how USB-C cables handle charging and data transfer. That’s why two cables that look identical can perform wildly differently. To show just how deep those differences run, tech research company Lumafield used X-ray CT scanners to peer inside a range of USB-C cables—from bargain-bin cords to Apple’s high-end Thunderbolt 4 model. The X-rays show a clear difference.

Researchers peered inside the $5.59 USB-C cable by NiceTQ, with a modest 60 W charging. At first glance, it promises data transfer speeds of a whopping 10Gbps. But when Lumafield took a closer look, they discovered a different story.

The internal structure was barebones: no shielding, no PCB, just wires soldered directly to the connector pins floating in plastic. Out of the eight available pins, only four were active, meaning the cable could only reach around 480 Mbps, far below its advertised performance. And with no redundancy, if one pin fails, the whole cable is done for.

At the other end of the spectrum, Apple’s Thunderbolt 4 cable is a miniature feat of engineering. It supports blazing 40 Gbps data transfer, 100 W charging, and even daisy-chaining up to six Thunderbolt 3 devices.

Please check your inbox and confirm your subscription.

Peeling back its layers, Lumafield’s scans exposed a robust stainless steel enclosure housing 24 independent pins on a 10-layer PCBA. Ten of these wires are co-axially shielded, each meticulously soldered to a complex PCB assembly. To ensure durability, a strain-relief fitting, crimped from eight directions, encases the entire assembly.

Lumafield also examined cables from Amazon Basics and ATYFUER, finding that the latter’s $3.89 model was surprisingly more sophisticated than the pricier NiceTQ cable.

There’s an important caveat here; or rather, a couple. These X-ray scans explain why Apple’s cable is so much more complicated than a basic charging cord. But they don’t prove that Apple makes the best cable for the money. Not at all. A certified Thunderbolt cable from another manufacturer may deliver comparable performance for significantly less.

And most people don’t need all this engineering in the first place. If you’re simply charging your phone or transferring the occasional file, a decent, appropriately rated USB-C cable is usually enough. The expensive stuff becomes useful when you’re connecting high-speed external storage, multiple displays, or other demanding equipment.

It’s also worth remembering that USB-C technology has moved on. Apple’s newer Thunderbolt 5 cable supports even faster data transfers and higher charging power, yet its shorter version costs considerably less than the $129 Thunderbolt 4 cable examined here.

The takeaway? Not all USB-C cables are created equal. Two cables can look practically identical on the outside and conceal vastly different technology. But that doesn’t mean the most expensive one is necessarily the best choice.

The best USB-C cable isn’t the most sophisticated one. It’s the one that does everything you need, without making you pay for things you don’t.
