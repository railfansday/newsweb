#699. Roku Pro Series OLED TV Review (2026)

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

If you have OLED TV aspirations but an in-this-economy bank balance, good news: Roku just entered the chat with its first-ever OLED, and it starts at a reasonable $1,000—not counting inevitable future discounts, like the recent October Prime Day deal that lopped off $300. Of course, price rarely tells the full television story, like whether a budget OLED can stand up to, say, a high-end QLED or midrange mini-LED.

Here’s the story: The Roku Pro Series OLED is a very good TV, better than its price tag would suggest, though not without a few compromises. Colors dazzle, black levels go deep, and Roku’s software continues to yield the best user experience. It’s not blazingly bright, and your size options are few (55 or 65 inches), but on the whole this should please anyone making a move from an older, lesser screen.

Although Roku provides only a small printed quick-start guide, there’s nothing complicated about setting up this TV. The two tool-free feet attach with large thumbscrews and can be set at two different heights—great if you want to, say, raise the screen a bit higher so your soundbar doesn’t block it.

From there, Roku’s friendly UI walks you through the usual configuration steps, many of which you can complete on your phone (as opposed to navigating onscreen menus with the remote, always a slower process).

Four well-marked HDMI ports reside on the left side of the Roku Pro Series, set close enough to the edge that they’re easy to reach. The top one, clearly labeled “eARC,” is painted with Roku’s trademark purple for quick at-a-glance identification, while the bottom two—120-Hz ports intended for gaming—are white. (Why two? It’s a bit unusual, yes, but splendid for those who operate more than one console.) The TV also supplies the usual coaxial input for a traditional cable or antenna hookup, an optical audio input for older soundbars and an Ethernet port if you want to bypass Wi-Fi.

Speaking of which, it’s a bit disappointing that Roku opted for Wi-Fi 5 here instead of Wi-Fi 6 or later. That said, my Eero Pro router also tops out at Wi-Fi 5, and it’s likely the same situation in plenty of other households. I encountered zero issues in terms of connection reliability, stability, or performance.

The Roku operating system continues to offer the most intuitive and novice-friendly TV viewing experience, marked by its colorful, oversize app icons and simple menus. There’s a bit more UI customization available now than in Rokus past, like the option to display icons in four columns instead of three. And there’s a sense of whimsy here not found elsewhere, including the lively “Roku City” screensaver that appears when the TV is idle. You can also opt for Roku Backdrops, an art-themed screensaver that does a solid Samsung The Frame impression.

Ads do crop up in various places—nearly a third of the home screen, a banner at the bottom of the menu screen, etc.—but they’re mostly streaming-related, not obnoxiously product-oriented like in Amazon’s and Google’s UIs.

One small gripe: There are still some streaming apps—HBO Max, Paramount+, etc.—that don’t let you sign into your account via app or QR code, even though their Google TV counterparts do. So be prepared for the occasional hassle of having to manually navigate onscreen keyboards with the remote.

Thankfully, Roku’s remote still rocks, with a cheerful purple navigation pad, dedicated play/pause and shuttle buttons, a programmable shortcut button, and side-mounted volume and mute controls. It’s more comfortable and intuitive than most remotes and is backlit. Unfortunately, it’s not rechargeable, an annoying oversight for a “Pro Series” TV. And its push-to-talk voice controls are fine for the likes of basic searches and playback functions but are constrained to Roku’s UI; you don’t get the added “smarts” afforded by Alexa+ and Google Assistant. (The exception: Roku’s branded smart-home gear, such as security cameras and light bulbs, can be controlled via voice commands.)

At least if the remote goes missing in the couch cushions (or an entirely different room, which is where I tried it), a handy remote-finder button on the right side of the TV will trigger “Here I am!” beeps.

To test the Roku Pro Series, I watched a wide range of sports, movies, and TV shows and fired up my Xbox Series X. As with other budget OLEDs, the colors impress; the brightness, not so much.

I started by streaming Solo: A Star Wars Story on Disney+. The first five minutes take place in the dark (literally and figuratively) underbelly of Corellia, and I’ve found that many TVs struggle here with contrast and brightness; several of these early scenes can look muddy or just plain dim. Roku’s OLED fared well on the contrast front, but I found myself wishing for a bit more visibility when Han lays out his plan to Kira and, later, confronts Lady Proxima. Even watching in a relatively dark room, those moments revealed suboptimal brightness.

Next up, the white-hallway scene in Mythic Quest, season 3, episode 1—a great measure of both contrast and black levels. Ian Grimm’s shirt appeared dark as night and juxtaposed perfectly against the smooth white walls. And within those walls, different shades appeared in different areas as the characters walked around; the TV expertly contrasted all of them. Did I find myself wishing for slightly whiter whites? Yes. Did I enjoy the scene any less? No.

While I had the Apple TV app running, I tuned into some live MLB soccer. The action: sharp and smooth (and dull, obviously, because soccer), and the same was true when I switched over to an NFL game on DirecTV. That’s as expected with a 120-Hz refresh rate.

Speaking of which, that rate, coupled with low-latency game mode (which kicked on automatically) served my Xbox experiences well. From the 1930s palette of the cartoonish Cuphead to the candy-colored arcade racing of Burnout: Paradise, everything looked crisp and vivid, with fast response times and zero blur.

For my color investigations, I clicked into Planet Earth II on HBO Max, Pixar’s Coco on Disney+, various “8K” nature videos on YouTube, and an Inglorious Basterds 4K Blu-ray played on the Xbox. Across the board, the Roku Pro Series produced vibrant, richly detailed color—but with less of the “pop” I’ve witnessed on high-end LG and Sony OLEDs. That’s partially due to the panel’s limited brightness.

Whether that’s a bad thing is open for debate; some would argue that higher-end OLEDs oversaturate, the shiny colors more of a distraction than an enhancement. Discuss.

A notable Roku innovation is Smart Picture Max, which automatically detects the type of content you’re watching and adjusts various display settings accordingly. Though it’s hard to gauge the impact of this on things like color and brightness, there’s one clearly quantifiable benefit: motion-smoothing. Smart Picture Max disables it for most TV shows and movies, meaning you don’t get the dreaded soap-opera effect. That alone gives the Pro Series a leg up over most OLEDs, which require manual tinkering to disable this heinous feature (and again to re-enable it for sports and gaming, the only situations in which it’s not heinous).

The one catch here is that any Dolby Vision content overrides Smart Picture Max, leaving motion-smoothing switched on. Fortunately, Roku makes it refreshingly easy to modify that and other picture settings; just press the star button on the remote. I found (and toggled off) smoothing with just a few clicks. Another nice perk: Any change you make applies instantly to whatever you’re viewing, so you can immediately gauge whether it’s what you want; you don’t have to exit the menu to see the results.

On the audio front, the presence of Dolby Atmos does nothing to improve the TV’s speakers, which produce plenty of volume but get harsh and shrill the higher you go. Indeed, the opening measures of Nick Perito’s “The Green Leaves of Summer” in Inglorious Basterds were deeply unpleasant, even at a modest volume. This came as something of a surprise, as Roku’s previous Pro Series TV sounded much better overall. The speakers here are tolerable at low volumes in a small room; for everything else, you’re going to want a soundbar.

In the end, Roku’s first OLED won’t win over any die-hard fans of premium panels; it’s a budget model through and through, probably on par with LG’s similarly affordable B6. But it’s still an OLED, with all the vivid color, pitch-black black, and strong contrast that technology affords. And the Roku remote, interface, and automated image adjustments contribute to a splendid user experience. Watch for the next sale (Black Friday, anyone?) and you’ll feel like you’re getting a steal.

#697. Scientists win 2026 Nobel Prize for controlling brain cells with light

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Scientists who worked out how to control brain cells with light have won this year’s Nobel prize in physiology or medicine. Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel were honored for their research into “light-gated ion channels and optogenetics.”

Peter Hegemann, at Humboldt University of Berlin, and Georg Nagel, at the University of Würzburg, wanted to understand how a tiny, single-celled alga called Chlamydomonas senses light. Light-sensing is not unique to animals with eyes: organisms throughout the living world use light to detect their surroundings and respond to changes in their environment. Even single-cell organisms have proteins that respond to light. These proteins are used to move towards or away from light, to generate energy and to respond to the various demands of a 24-hour day.

Hegemann and Nagel discovered that Chlamydomonas has a beautifully ingenious mechanism of light detection that relies on a single protein called channelrhodopsin.

Channelrhodopsin is built into the outer membrane of the algal cell. Part of the protein faces the water outside the cell and part faces the inside. The protein also contains a small molecule called retinaldehyde, which is able to absorb light.

When light hits the retinaldehyde inside channelrhodopsin, it changes shape. This makes the channelrhodopsin protein change shape too. That change opens a tiny passage through the cell membrane. Positively charged particles, including sodium, can then flow from the water outside the alga into the cell. This movement of charged particles sends a signal inside the alga that helps it swim.

How fantastic to have solved such an interesting biological question, but why the Nobel prize in physiology or medicine? The answer lies in the way nerve cells work. Nerve cells use the movement of charged particles (called “ions”) across their membranes to control their activity. When positively charged particles flow into a nerve cell, they can make it more active.

The researchers realized that if they could put channelrhodopsin into nerve cells, they might be able to switch those cells on simply by shining a light on them. That is exactly what Hegemann, Nagel and the third Nobel-prize winner, Karl Deisseroth, a psychiatrist and bioengineer at Stanford University in California, went on to do.

The ability to control nerve cells with light is called optogenetics. It has become a powerful research tool because scientists can use it to switch specific groups of nerve cells on and off and see what happens.

First, scientists deliver the gene for channelrhodopsin to the nerve cells they want to study. The gene is usually carried into the cells by a virus that has been altered so that it cannot cause disease. The cells then make the channelrhodopsin protein, which makes them respond to light.

In animals such as mice, scientists can shine light into the brain through a thin optical fibre. When the light reaches the cells, channelrhodopsin opens and the cells become active. Nearby nerve cells that do not contain the protein are unaffected.

Light can be aimed at a very small part of the brain and switched on and off extremely quickly. This makes optogenetics a powerful way to investigate how the brain works. For example, if scientists want to know whether a particular group of nerve cells is involved in memory, sleep, sensation or movement, say, they can switch those cells on with light and see what happens.

In one experiment in 2012, a team at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology gave mice a mild electric shock in a particular cage. They used optogenetics to tag the nerve cells in the brain’s memory center that were active during that the fear-conditioning experiment. Days later, in a different cage, they switched those cells on with light. The mice froze in fear, as if they remembered the shock.

Mice that were not shocked did not freeze, which showed that the light was reactivating a specific memory. Deisseroth was one of the study’s authors.

Optogenetics has transformed scientists’ ability to investigate how the brain and nervous system work. It may also have a future as a treatment. Optogenetics is already in early-stage clinical trials for people with retinal degeneration. In these trials, researchers introduce light-sensitive proteins into nerve cells that remain in the retina after the cells that normally detect light – called rods and cones – have been lost. This can make surviving cells respond to light and restore some vision.

Researchers are also investigating whether optogenetics could eventually be used to treat neurological conditions, including Parkinson’s and epilepsy.

This year’s Nobel prize celebrates work that started with a question that may sound esoteric: how do Chlamydomonas swim toward light? This question has no obvious practical importance. It doesn’t address an urgent need in human health, nor is it a clear route to riches. The success of optogenetics is an illustration of the importance of continuing to ask questions simply to understand the world around us because it’s impossible to predict where knowledge will take us.

#694. Manchester City, the UAE and the UK's OM in a political tangle

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Nearly 20 years after arriving in England, Manchester City's UAE owners could exit amid cheating charges. That would pose a challenge for a city — and prime minister — closely tied to Abu Dhabi.

Defiance was the word that stood out as Manchester City returned home for the first time since they were found guilty of 114 financial charges by the Premier League in a case that's made headlines around the world.

City showed a measure of it on the pitch, even as they lost 4-2 to holders, Qatari-owned Paris Saint-Germain, in the Champions League group stages on Wednesday.

Among the home fanbase at the Etihad Stadium, DW found universal support for City's Abu Dhabi owners — headed by Sheikh Mansour, who is also UAE's vice president and deputy prime minister.

"I'm all in with the ownership now," said local fan Matt. "If we end up back in League Two [fourth tier], so be it. We've got 15 years of memories we wouldn't have otherwise and to me it's just an accounting line on a spreadsheet really."

The stadium name itself is a reminder of the takeover that changed this club, and its part of Manchester, in 2008. The national airline of the UAE, Etihad's inflated sponsorship of City is among the key planks of the case against them, while many buildings and businesses around the stadium bear the brand. It's a stadium that's also seen plenty of success of late.

"We've seen things other fans will never see," added fellow City fan Josh. "I think people have got to consider how many people this club has given great memories to, how many people's lives have been changed by it, myself included....you've got to be grateful for that."

Though many in sky blue shared those sentiments, most outside the club now view the trophies won in the relevant period as tarnished. On top of that, critics point to City's reticence in providing evidence in their case, the long-documented human rights abuses in UAE and the often dismissive tone with which the owners have dealt with being found guilty of charges they have now appealed.

But those City fans appear to have one powerful ally in British Prime Minister Andy Burnham. Though he has since sought to tone down his position on Mansour and co. somewhat, the former mayor of Greater Manchester said he "would be really concerned to lose them," after the verdict was announced in late September. "They've been such a huge partner in the building of modern Manchester," he added.

While Burnham has since denied being close to City's ownership, even as City were playing on Wednesday, The Guardian reported a club representative was invited on a government-backed trade delegation to India this week by Burnham's successor as Manchester mayor, Bev Craig.

Earlier the same day, Burnham was forced to confirm he had two additional dinners with the club chair, Khaldoon Al Mubarak, at the Etihad Stadium, having previously declared those only as matchday hospitality. It has become a serious domestic issue for the man who replaced Keir Starmer in July.

What is clear is that Burnham welcomed Abu Dhabi investment during his time in charge of the northwestern city. This came mainly in the form of residential and commercial buildings as well as libraries and sports centers, all popping up in previously run-down areas in and around the stadium.

Georgina Blakeley, a Professor at the University of Huddersfield who wrote a book called "The regeneration of east Manchester," told DW that UAE investment has transformed swathes of the city.

"One of the prime drivers for building this partnership with the Abu Dhabi United Group [a private equity arm of the state mostly concerned with the football club] was that at that point in time, 2014, there was no other private investor willing to invest at the scale that the council wanted. And to do so in a way that created a build-to-rent market," she said.

The UAE investment in Manchester, accelerated by the Manchester Life Partnership with the city's council signed in 2014, has been a key defense for the City owners and fans when faced with sportswashing allegations in the past. And while Blakeley believes councils in the UK are often forced to seek foreign investment, there has been some pushback on how east Manchester has changed.

"A lot of people would be critical of some of the developments in [the districts of] Ancoats and New Islington and say that it's gentrified the area and there are some tensions between newer populations coming in and living in the more expensive flats and those already there. I think there's some truth to it. But I also think there's some truth to the fact that people forget really, really quickly that if you go back to just the 1990s, Ancoats was a no-go area," she said.

While profit for both council and state have certainly been a driver of the partnership, Blakeley thinks that forging greater links to the community could be something City's owners see "as a beneficial side effect."

After 18 years in the club and city, the UAE are deeply embedded in this part of Manchester. With political ties between the UK and UAE now reportedly strained as a result of the charges, there are suggestions that City's owners will try to sell the club. They may also be forced to do so, with sanctions still to be decided pending City's appeal.

There is no such trouble at PSG, who look capable of winning a third straight Champions League powered by their own Gulf-State coffers.

What is clear is that state-owned football clubs can become successful fast in a free market, as the Premier League was when the UAE owners arrived. Deep pockets, strong contacts at the top tables of the sport and canny strategies to integrate with the local area can be a heady mix.

It's certainly one that's proved intoxicating to City's fans, who brush off secret second contracts, sham sponsorship and unreliable witnesses with a loyalty built on success, community and investment. Judging by the mood in Manchester on Wednesday, no sanctions are going to change that.
