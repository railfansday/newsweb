#1158. Manchester City Premier League charges: What do they mean?

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Since being bought by the UAE in 2008, Manchester City has become one of the world's most successful football clubs. A raft of serious financial charges threatens to undermine this and bring chaos to the Premier League.

Manchester City has won the English Premier League eight times over the last 14 years. Whether those titles will remain is one of a plethora of issues at stake after England's top division charged one of its most high-profile clubs with more than 100 breaches of its financial regulations.

On Tuesday, the Premier League announced that an independent commission had found City had drawn up "sham contracts" as well as "relying on sham agreements to artificially inflate the club's revenues and reduce its costs." These contracts, dubious sponsorships and a number of other methods were, according to the commission's report, used to inflate the club's revenue to the tune of about £900 million (€1.05 billion) over the period between the 2009-10 and 2017-18 seasons.

The case has been ongoing for eight years after initial revelations from Der Spiegel, a German magazine, and Rui Pinto of the 'Football Leaks' website. City continues to deny any wrongdoing.

"The Club is innocent of the accusations made by the Premier League and a comprehensive body of irrefutable evidence exists in support of all of its positions, relating to this case," read a club statement. "Manchester City FC will now pursue the appeal avenues open to it, on the basis that the opinion contains clear material errors, of law, principle and fact, and is unsafe."

On Friday, the club issued a further statement saying it had lodged a "comprehensive Appeal."

The 750-page report was damning, accusing City of inflating contracts and sponsorship deals, running secondary contracts off the books, making "concerted efforts to stop and frustrate the Premier League investigation" and signing off inflated image rights deals to artificially boost their earnings. The report says all this enabled them to pass both Premier League and UEFA financial spending limits.

The Premier League's chief ­executive, Richard Masters, described the case "as the most significant in Premier League history," adding that the club "systematically broke Premier League Rules for nearly a decade."

Although the Premier League, the world's most commercially successful football league, has drawn some praise for its actions, others point out that it took Pinto leaking emails and media reports to take action. The report shows that Manchester City, which had won the league once in 71 years before being taken over by the United Arab Emirates (UAE) in 2008, were consistently posting revenues way higher than any of their more established and successful rivals.

In this context, its the owners. The Abu Dhabi United Group, essentially an investment vehicle for the UAE royal family, bought the club in 2008. Mansour bin Zayed Al Nahyan, know simply as Sheikh Mansour, fronted the takeover. He is a member of the ruling Al Nahyan family and the UAE's vice president and deputy prime minister. Khaldoon Al Mubarak, who also holds several government positions in the UAE, was brought in as chairman.

Nation-state ownership of this type is controversial in European football. Manchester City's owners have long been criticized for the country's human rights record and using the club for sportswashing. Similar criticisms have been made of European champions Paris Saint-Germain, which Qatar owned and City's Premier League rivals Newcastle United, which is owned 85% by Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund.

City's CEO, Ferran Soriano, joined in 2012 as City expanded their ownership into foreign leagues, buying up clubs in the US, Japan and Australia. He replaced former head coach Roberto Mancini with fellow Spaniard Manuel Pellegrini before former Bayern Munich and Barcelona coach Pep Guardiola took over in 2016, ushering in the club's most successful period.

The club immediately started splashing out on big-money transfers after the UAE investment arrived. At the time, neither UEFA nor the Premier League had the tight financial rules they now have. UEFA introduced its Financial Fair Play (FFP) rules for the 2011-12 season, while the Premier League followed with its Profit and Sustainability Rules (PSR) in 2013-14.

City's free spending and relatively low commercial income meant they were likely to fall foul of such rules. The club was handed a conditional €60 million UEFA fine for losses in the 2011-12 and 2012-13 seasons. They would later be banned from the Champions League on similar grounds in 2020 before being reinstated on appeal.

In an attempt to try and comply with these financial rules, City set up a system they called "Project Longbow" in 2012. The commission said much of this was genuine, but one arm of it was an agreement with a third party called Fordham that was, according to the ruling, "little more than a front." Essentially, it reads, the club's UAE owners "would be used (and were used) to enable Fordham to acquire from the club (at a sizeable, artificially inflated price) the club's entitlement to benefit financially from its players' image rights."

In other words, City's owners were pumping money into their own club through vastly inflated sponsorship and image rights deals so their income looked higher and they could spend more, particularly on player transfers and wages. The report also claims that City paid three individuals, whose names are redacted, through a third party to keep them off the books.

That's the question on everyone's lips. So far, no sanctions have been imposed on City, and the appeal will likely drag proceedings out even longer. Meanwhile, the club, which is top of the Premier League, faces Liverpool on October 11, after the international break.

While there is no precedent for such a tranche of charges, clubs have previously fallen foul of the Premier League's PSR regulations. Everton were handed a 10-point penalty for a £19.5 million breach over a period ending with the 2021-22 season. This was based on a Premier League tariff of six points for the initial breach, plus an additional point for every £5 million the club overspent. Given that City's overspending is close to a billion pounds, applying that would certainly relegate them, though the Premier League does not operate England's second-tier league, the Championship.

For now, it looks like City and the league will play on under a cloud. But if the verdict remains after appeal, clubs and individuals whose careers and honors stand to be impacted will likely come forward and litigate. City themselves could be relegated, docked points over successive seasons, face transfer bans or any combination of those things. The titles won in the relevant period could also be stripped.

#1155. Building Lionesses 2.0 - Wiegman's new challenge awaits

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Sarina Wiegman has won the past three Women's European Championships - with the Netherlands in 2017 and England in 2022 and 2025

When Sarina Wiegman took the reins as England manager in September 2021, she declared she had "come to bring the next level".

Winner of the 2017 Women's Euros with her own Netherlands and runner-up at the subsequent World Cup in 2019, she certainly had the pedigree, but could she deliver on her promises?

Five years on, Wiegman has pocketed two more European Championships and acquired another silver medal on the global stage.

Not only that, but the success of her Lionesses exploded the women's game in this country, taking it not just to a new level, but to previously unthinkable heights.

And yet on the day Wiegman extends her deal until after the Euros in 2029, her newest challenge could be her hardest yet.

England manager Wiegman signs contract extension

Wiegman's successful England teams have been built on a core group of players from an outstanding generation - but a few cracks have appeared this year.

Seven of the team that started against world champions Spain in June also played in the Euro 2022 final against Germany - and their 4-0 defeat in Mallorca laid bare the gap England must bridge to the world's best.

That result also consigned England to two rounds of play-offs to reach next summer's World Cup.

Beating Greece this month navigated the first of those, with games against Ukraine, who they have beaten twice already in 2026, to come before Christmas.

Nevertheless, finding successors to those who have spearheaded the success will not be straightforward.

Defensive stalwarts Lucy Bronze and Alex Greenwood will be 37 and 36 respectively by Euro 2029, while Leah Williamson and Keira Walsh will be 32.

Other key players such as Alessia Russo and Georgia Stanway will also have turned 30, far from past their best, but still a consideration.

"We have to continue the evolution of the Lionesses," record goalscorer Ellen White told the Women's Football Weekly podcast. "We can't stay stagnant or keep trying to win in the same ways all the time."

Former England goalkeeper Rachel Brown-Finnis thinks one of Wiegman's strengths as a manager is her willingness to give young players a chance. "She's not afraid of bringing in players who have never been capped before," Brown-Finnis told BBC Radio 5 Live.

"Wiegman is looking right across the spectrum and asking who's on form, who can add to my squad, who can fill a position or a couple of different positions?"

Speaking on BBC Radio 5 Live's Monday Night Club, former Lionesses captain Steph Houghton said: "You can see Wiegman is trying to change a few things. The last camp she brought on Lexi Potter for her debut and she is now starting regularly for Chelsea. As a young player you need that to thrive in this environment.

"She is seeing the progress of young players coming through to press those who have been in the squad for a long time."

Greek takeaways: What did we learn from Lionesses' win?

It is not as though Wiegman has ignored squad regeneration.

During her time in charge, she has handed debuts to 24 players, but only Lauren James, Hannah Hampton and Lucia Kendall have become established starters.

Those games against Greece offered a clue to her next steps.

In Crete, Manchester City's Laura Blindkilde Brown, 23, showed glimpses of her ball-playing ability, while in the second leg 20-year-old Chelsea academy graduate Potter impressed in midfield.

London City's Freya Godfrey, 21, and talented 18-year-old Erica Meg Parkinson also got their first caps as Wiegman scrutinised her options.

Figures earlier this season showed the number of homegrown starters has been dropping in the Women's Super League (WSL) over the past four campaigns, with the league's growing prestige attracting more foreign stars.

The Football Association (FA) chief football officer Dan Ashworth said Wiegman has helped bring "a golden age for women's football in this country".

To prolong it, she will need to keep producing fruit from increasingly arid ground.

'Alarming' - does WSL have a homegrown problem?

Could Potter be England's next breakthrough star?

Prior to Wiegman's appointment, England women had performed well at major tournaments - finishing third at the World Cup in 2015 and fourth in 2019 - but winning Euro 2022 was something new.

WSL attendances rocketed, media coverage surged and clubs amplified their women's teams. However, four years on, there is evidence to suggest interest has plateaued.

Initial crowd figures for the 2026-27 season are down on previous campaigns, while only 18,587 fans watched England beat Greece 5-0 at King Power Stadium, nearly 10,000 fewer than qualifiers at Nottingham Forest's City Ground and Everton's Hill Dickinson Stadium this year.

With so much competition, Lionesses success is crucial for sustaining momentum in the women's game - but expectations are higher now.

A fanbase was born from success and has become accustomed to it. Will they tolerate anything less than finals and trophies?

After all, Spain, Japan and hosts Brazil are among the many nations that look poised to have a significant say at the World Cup next summer.

Only four of the top 10 nations in Fifa's world rankings have female managers (Spain, USA and Canada), with Wiegman the longest serving.

If she sees out her contract, only Hope Powell (1998-2013) and Martin Reagan (1979-90), will have been England head coach for longer.

As she signed her new contract, she said she "could not think of a better job in world football".

It is hard to think of many who have done a better job than Wiegman has already accomplished.

Committing to three more years means her legacy may now be defined by what she achieves next.

#1150. Climate leaders called for solar energy for 'everyone, everywhere' by 2030, but with three years to go, is that goal attainable?

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Prominent climate leaders have called on governments, businesses, institutions and the public to make a push for solar power before the decade is out, repowering the world with cheap, clean energy. Is that possible?

In a world "set ablaze by war and  warming," with ever-worsening climate disasters such as wildfires in Europe and collapsing glaciers in Nepal, solar energy provides a solution in the form of clean, affordable power, prominent climate leaders wrote in an open letter.

Signed by author Bill McKibben, former UN Climate Secretary Christiana Figueres and Canada's former Minister of Environment and Climate Change Catherine McKenna, the letter makes a clear call. It urges governments, businesses, institutions and the public to come together to provide clean, affordable energy to "everyone, everywhere by 2030" — from the close to one billion people who still lack access to reliable electricity, to the billions more who pay too much for dirty fossil fuels.

With just a little over three years to go until the decade is out, how realistic is this push?

"It's an ambitious target, but we've blown away in terms of where we thought we would be on solar and renewables," McKenna told DW. "So let's just keep on going."

According to global energy think tank Ember,solar power has grown at its highest rate in eight years. In 2025, it increased by a record 30% over the previous year and has risen more than tenfold since 2015.

Solar is the cheapest form of electricity in the history of the world, said McKenna, who founded the climate advisory firm Climate and Nature Solutions, and the global coalition Women Leading on Climate.

"It is scaling way faster than anyone ever imagined."

As is battery technology used to store excess renewable energy.

"The cost of batteries have as well come down as the cost of solar modules," said Eicke Weber, a physicist and former director of Germany's Fraunhofer Institute for Solar Energy Systems who went on to found the European Solar Manufacturing Council, among other things.

"We have the technologies available and we have enough money to finance it," he said, of a transition to widespread solar access.

In 2025, for the first time in 100 years, renewable energy sources overtook coal power in the global electricity mix, Ember's most recent analysis showed.

At the moment, installed solar capacity around the world stands at 3 terawatts. 1TW equals 1000 gigawatts, and a single nuclear power plant has a capacity of 1GW.

Currently, the world produces about 1TW of new photovoltaic capacity each year, most of it in China. Weber says in order to fully change global energy supply to renewables, we would need about 50TW, adding that "we have more production capacities than we are selling."

Roughly 655 million people still lack accessto electricity.

"The pace of electrification in Sub-Saharan Africa has to triple to achieve universal access by 2030," McKenna said, pointing out how quickly this gap could be closed with solar.

Areas without electricity have the opportunity to leapfrog straight into clean technologies, she says, bypassing the fossil fuels that are heating the planet. Rising global temperatures are fueling extreme weather events such as drought, storms and flooding around the world.

"And there's a misunderstanding, which is often propagated intentionally by fossil fuel companies, that it's expensive, it's hard, it's unreliable. And that just isn't the case anymore," she said. "To be honest, the most unreliable thing is if you're trying to pass fossil fuels through the Strait of Hormuz."

Hans-Josef Fell is used to the argument that certain things cannot be done. Widely known as a pioneer of renewable energy, he drafted Germany's Renewable Energy Sources Act, which established feed-in tariffs that catalyzed clean energy growth at home and beyond.

"The Renewable Energy Sources Act unleashed exponential growth that shattered every assumption about what was achievable. It overturned the scenarios and projections that policymakers and experts had considered realistic. In the end, reality outperformed all expectations," said Fell, who now heads the think tank Energy Watch Group.

Fell, who has lived off grid for five years, said achieving a global energy transition requires political and societal will that is "currently lacking around the world because public debate is dominated by fossil fuel interests."

According to calculations by his think tank, if global energy demand were met entirely by renewables, solar energy would account for roughly 60-70% of the supply. The rest would come from wind power, and other sources, such as bioenergy and hydropower.

Countries like China and Pakistan have already pushed ahead.

"It is in our hands right now in the year 2026 to say we drop everything, we concentrate on this problem. We don't need to wait for new research," Weber said.

"It is possible to achieve this, but it would require a very kind of moonshot program," he added, saying it would need serious political backing.

He says while it might be possible for Germany to pull off such as transition in two or three years, if the political will were there, it would likely take longer to achieve on a global scale. "We would need probably till 2040 to say that we have a goal of a worldwide 80%, 90% electricity from renewables."

"I would sum it up this way: If we as a global society tackle this properly, we might actually be able to make it happen," Fell said. "And even if we achieve only 90% of that goal, it would still be a remarkable accomplishment."

McKenna pointed to the need for blended finance, calling on governments, the World Bank, the African Development Bank and the private sector to put in money.

"What is irrational is continuing down the road of fossil fuels," she added. "That's irrational on the costs, the risks, the impacts. It's an old technology."

#1147. Jordan Henderson's Chelsea revival: How he's still running Premier League midfields at 36, why Xabi Alonso is so enamoured with his role as dressing-room mentor and referee whisperer, and why he hasn't given up on England dream

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

By RIATH AL-SAMARRAI, CHIEF SPORTS FEATURE WRITER

Published: 02:00 BST, 23 October 2026 | Updated: 09:22 BST, 23 October 2026

A little like the player himself, there are no frills around Jordan Henderson’s nickname. Wherever he has travelled in his long career, ‘Hendo’ follows, and it’s also how he is addressed by his manager at Chelsea.

All of which is vaguely interesting for a single reason – Xabi Alonso almost never uses soubriquets. But with Henderson, there’s an exception.

According to sources on the staff at Chelsea, if you want to see Alonso’s eyes light up, mention 'Hendo'. His responses tend to be revealing, too. Alonso doesn’t rave about the two goals in the recent 5-1 win over Bournemouth, or what the rest of us say about Henderson’s primary function at the club. His preference, instead, is to drift towards the nuances.

As a midfielder, Alonso was capable of spectacular moments but those close to him cite his deeper obsessions with tempo control, positioning and the minutiae that doesn’t make for exciting reels. In Henderson, he sees a kindred spirit and a master of the subtle arts, which has evidently survived into a 16th season as a Premier League regular.

If Henderson starts a fourth straight game against Tottenham on Saturday evening, it will come as no shock. But the surprise, beyond Chelsea, is the comfort with which he can still control a midfield in the frenzied hothouse of our top flight at 36.

An athlete excelling into their mid or late thirties is not as uncommon as it once was. But a fact lost on no one at Chelsea’s Cobham HQ is that Henderson is thriving in the physical battle.

Against Brentford, whose squad rank third in the Premier League for this metric, that was notable.

Henderson’s engine has always been one of his defining qualities. When he was taken on a free from Brentford in the summer, in a move largely driven by recruitment executives Paul Winstanley and Sam Jewell, his physical capacity was one of the pull factors.

Granit Xhaka, 34, had been their first choice on a brief to land an experienced midfielder for a young squad, but even before the Swiss rejected the move, it had been established that Henderson’s numbers offered no evidence of a declining output.

Last season, at Brentford, he ranked in the Premier League’s 95th percentile for his distance stats, but in isolation it can be a misleading data point – a player running to bad spots is no use to anyone. Keith Andrews never came close to levelling that complaint against Henderson.

There is a famous irony here – Sir Alex Ferguson once rejected the chance to sign Henderson for Manchester United in the early stages of his career on the basis of an idiosyncratic running style. He feared it would make him prone to injuries but 466 Premier League appearances in, that case has long since been put to bed.

Naturally, the conversation around Henderson these days goes to broader places, and namely his role as a mentor to a young group that was awfully ill-disciplined under Liam Rosenior and Enzo Maresca.

The initial evidence suggests that an emotionally volatile squad has benefited hugely from a strong leadership figure – it has been observed internally at Chelsea that he carries more weight with referees and team-mates.

Two examples came up in the Everton game. He was able to instantly silence his captain, Moises Caicedo, when the latter launched into a rant at Jack Grealish, and then when he led a retaliation against a rough challenge from James Tarkowski. Walking the line between aggression and a red card has often been a problem at Stamford Bridge, so an old head will add value.

Curiously, Henderson seems to bristle at any depiction of him being recruited purely as a ‘vibes’ man and has admitted the notion has fired him with a point to prove. But he presumably knows it was part of the club’s thinking. Just as it was for Thomas Tuchel during the World Cup and for Andrews at Brentford.

Henderson’s value to the dressing room ranked among the reasons why Brentford had rescued him from his post-Liverpool wilderness, which saw a poorly-judged move to Saudi Arabia followed by a fractious, uncomfortable period at Ajax. Some sources at Brentford thought he might reward the club by sticking around; that he decided to leave once Chelsea came calling will have stung.

At Chelsea, there have been predictable tributes around Henderson’s influence. One source said it is common for him to still be at the training ground at 5pm or 6pm, when most will head for home mid-afternoon, and it is known that he spent the most recent international break honing his free-kicks with set-piece coach Austin MacPhee and Cole Palmer.

Henderson celebrated his strike against Bournemouth by running to MacPhee on the touchline; it was MacPhee who impressed upon Alonso that Henderson was a good option from that 25-yard range.

For Alonso, the strike served as a measure of public vindication for a transfer that, at first glance, seemed quite strange. No doubt he would say the move for ‘Hendo’ was always about a bigger picture and smaller details. But for now, it is working well either way.

Time will tell where it leads for Henderson – he still believes he can do a job for England heading into Euro 2028. Betting against it somehow feels like the riskier choice.

Nature (2026) Cite this article

Programming biological function—designing bespoke proteins to perform specified molecular tasks—is a foundational goal of molecular engineering. However, current design models remain fundamentally limited1,2,3: they typically require either natural proteins as starting points for optimization or manual reformulation of functional goals as geometric and sequence-level constraints to guide candidate generation. Here we introduce Pinal, a 16-billion-parameter foundation model that designs candidate proteins from natural-language descriptions of desired function. Trained on 1.7 billion synthetically annotated protein–text pairs, Pinal links functional intent to protein sequence and structure. In computational evaluations, generated candidates combined high predicted foldability with functional-description alignment and sequence diversity, providing a basis for prioritizing experimentally testable designs. We applied Pinal to four distinct design tasks—a fluorescent protein, a polyethylene terephthalate hydrolase, an alcohol dehydrogenase and a metabolic H-protein—and experimentally observed the intended function in each case, including catalytic activity for both designed enzymes. Without iterative experimental optimization, a Pinal-designed H-protein increased product titre by 1.7-fold relative to the corresponding native Escherichia coli H-protein in a multi-enzyme CO2 fixation pathway. These findings support natural language as a high-level interface for candidate generation in protein design, enabling programmable exploration with reduced reliance on manually specified structural or sequence constraints.

A semantic projector maps the text representation into the same embedding space as the structural tokens. This projected text vector is prepended to the structural token embeddings to form a text-protein joint representation. This joint representation is then processed by the SaProt-T block, repeated N times, to predict the final amino acid sequence.

ProTrek score. Right panel: predicted structural plausibility, measured by ESMFold pLDDT. Both textual alignment and foldability improve as K increases from 5 to 50. Further increasing K to 100 yields only marginal gains (or a slight decrease in ProTrek score) while substantially increasing computational cost. Based on this analysis, K = 50 is used as the standard setting for all experiments, offering a robust balance between design performance and inference efficiency.

For both sequence and structure, the scores for original and paraphrased text are high and similar, while scores for random text are lower, demonstrating the metric’s discriminative power. (c, f) Scatter plots comparing the scores of original versus randomly assigned descriptions. The near-zero correlations (ρ ≈ 0) further confirm that the ProTrek score is a valid metric that reliably distinguishes between relevant and irrelevant protein-text pairings. The original ProTrek study also demonstrated that its score selectively responds to biologically relevant keywords while ignoring non-functional terms. Building on this capability, a recent benchmark paper48 adopted ProTrek as a key metric for evaluating the alignment between protein sequences and their textual descriptions.
