Jean-Philippe Mateta scored on his first start since the opening day but Crystal Palace could not capitalise on a Besiktas red card to produce a winner in their 2-2 Europa League draw in Istanbul.

Palace were looking to bounce back from Sunday’s humiliating 5-0 defeat at Brighton, and were the superior side before Wilfred Ndidi opened the scoring for the hosts in first-half stoppage time.

Yeremy Pino drew the sides level six minutes after the restart and Mateta – who had a first-half goal chalked off by VAR – fired Palace to a first-time lead four minutes later.

Junior Olaitan netted a 73rd-minute equaliser, and Palace could not complete a second comeback after Umit Akdag was sent off for a clumsy 77th-minute challenge on Jorgen Strand Larsen, who was subsequently forced off.

There were sweeping changes for the hosts from Monday’s defeat to Trabzonspor as Vincenzo Italiano introduced nine different starters.

Mateta thought he had scored his first goal since the Conference League winner in May until a VAR check deemed him to be marginally offside in the build-up.

Still, it was a promising start for Palace, who had won the ball through determined pressing.

The visitors kept the pressure on, and there was another good chance when Yeremy Pino found Adam Wharton with a neat flick, but the England midfielder’s delivery to Mateta skipped past the striker.

Palace had limited the hosts to just Oh Hyeon-gyu’s nodded attempt as the contest ticked past 20 minutes and Tyrick Mitchell dragged an effort wide, though he might have wanted to pick out the approaching Mateta.

Mateta forced Alexander Nubel into a save from Wharton’s corner just before the half hour, and nodded another one wide soon after from their third corner of the night.

Palace fell behind in added time before the break, when they let the hosts take advantage of a gap between their centre-backs and failed to spot the run of Ndidi, who latched onto Yasin Ozcan’s long ball and slotted the opener into the bottom corner with his left foot.

The Eagles squared it up after the restart, the product of some good work by Dwight McNeil and Anan Khalaili to find Pino, who swept it home on the slide.

The equaliser was originally chalked off for offside, but the call was reversed following a VAR check.

They took the lead five minutes later, this time capitalising on a Besiktas error Mateta was quick to read, inspiring him to take off running, ultimately punishing the hosts when he blasted into the roof of the net.

Mateta was replaced by Strand Larsen just after the hour in a double swap which also saw Ismaila Sarr replace McNeil.

Olaitan then drew the sides back level, making quick work of the Palace defence before firing past the diving Benitez.

The Turkish side went down to 10 men with 13 minutes remaining following Akdag’s clumsy challenge on Strand Larsen, and a Palace penalty shout moments later was dismissed.

Strand Larsen was forced off, in obvious pain off the ball, and hobbled off with his arm around a staff member, and replaced by Eddie Nketiah, who fired into the side-netting with his first chance from an awkward angle.

The hosts stood firm, and closed out the contest before the Eagles could inflict any more damage.

Nature (2026) Cite this article

Hantaviruses are rodent-borne pathogens that cause severe human disease for which no approved therapeutics are available. New World hantaviruses, including Andes virus (ANDV), use the cell-surface receptor protocadherin-1 (PCDH1) to enter host cells1. However, the structural basis of this interaction remains unclear. Here we describe the structure of PCDH1 bound to the ANDV spike at near-atomic resolution. PCDH1 asymmetrically engages a deep central pocket formed by four Gn protomers in the spike, explaining why only intact viral particles or soluble tetramers recognize it. Mutagenesis identified three Gn loops that shape the receptor-binding site and afford the transfer of PCDH1 binding from New World to Old World glycoproteins. Structural and functional analyses of four neutralizing antibodies revealed that they block receptor binding through two distinct mechanisms—occluding access to the PCDH1-binding site or sterically preventing receptor engagement. Together, these findings define the structural determinants of hantavirus–receptor recognition, reveal how a virus can mask its receptor-binding site by recessing it in a surface depression and how antibodies can counter this strategy, and provide a framework for the design of antiviral therapeutics targeting the hantavirus spike.

Hantaviruses are a large and globally distributed family of viruses associated with severe zoonotic disease in humans. The New World hantaviruses (NWHs) ANDV and Sin Nombre virus (SNV) cause hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome (HCPS) in South and North America, respectively, with high case-fatality rates that can approach 40%. Cardiogenic shock and respiratory failure are hallmarks of HCPS. Old World hantaviruses (OWHs) are endemic in Europe and Asia or globally distributed in some cases (Seoul virus (SEOV)). The OWH Hantaan virus (HTNV), Puumala virus (PUUV) and Dobrava-Belgrade virus (DOBV) are associated with severe zoonotic disease and considerable mortality. Although hantaviruses are typically transmitted to humans through inhalation of aerosolized excreta from infected rodents, ANDV appears uniquely capable of human-to-human transmission2,3, raising its global public health significance and epidemic potential. This risk was underscored by the recent ANDV outbreak on a cruise ship departing Ushuaia, Argentina4. No US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)- or European Medicines Agency (EMA)-approved medical countermeasures are available to prevent or treat hantaviral infections.

Hantavirus glycoprotein spikes are formed by two glycoproteins, Gn and Gc, which are organized into a partial lattice at the surface of the virion. Each spike consists of four Gn molecules that form a central stabilizing core surrounded by four Gc molecules at the periphery. Gc mediates the interspike contacts that stabilize the lattice. Gn consists of a head (GnH) and a base (GnB) domain. GnH is exposed at the viral surface and is thought to participate in receptor binding, whereas GnB is buried within the spike and mediates most of the intraspike interactions. Gc consists of three domains that drive membrane fusion. At neutral pH, GnH interacts with Gc, forming a pre-fusion complex that prevents premature fusion. After exposure to acidic pH, the Gn–Gc complex dissociates, allowing Gc to insert three hydrophobic loops into the host cell membrane and undergo conformational rearrangements that drive fusion of viral and cellular membranes. Structural studies have reported the structures of GnH, GnB, Gc, the GnH–Gc complex and the Gn–Gc spike from several hantaviruses5,6,7,8,9,10.

Understanding how hantaviruses recognize their cellular receptors is a critical step toward the rational development of therapies and vaccines. Previous studies have identified several cell-surface molecules as candidate entry host factors, including integrins β1–3 (refs. 11,12,13,14), decay accelerating factor (DAF, encoded by CD55)15,16, complement factors (gC1qR)17 and the cadherin-superfamily protein PCDH1 (ref. 1). However, only PCDH1 has been shown to directly recognize the ANDV Gn–Gc spike1,18 and to be required for infection of human endothelial cells—major targets of hantavirus infection1,19. Furthermore, PCDH1 is critical for ANDV infection in vivo and for disease pathogenesis in a lethal hamster model of ANDV challenge1,18. PCDH1 is a type I membrane protein expressed in human airway epithelial and endothelial cells. It contains seven extracellular cadherin-repeat (EC) domains that extend into the extracellular space. Binding studies have shown that PCDH1 does not recognize soluble monomeric glycoproteins but instead binds to the tetrameric glycoprotein spikes displayed on the viral surface1,18. Monoclonal antibodies targeting the first EC repeat (EC1) inhibited entry of a recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus (rVSV) pseudotyped with Gn–Gc from multiple NWHs1, and point mutations at the tip of EC1 prevented PCDH1’s interaction with Gn–Gc of rVSVs bearing the glycoproteins of ANDV (rVSV-ANDV)1,18. Together, these findings indicate that NWHs infect host cells by engaging the EC1 domain of PCDH1. However, the structural determinants of this interaction remain unclear. Here we sought to determine the structure of PCDH1 bound to the ANDV Gn–Gc spike.

PCDH1 binds to viral particles but not to the soluble GnH–Gc heterodimer1,18, suggesting that the receptor-binding site is presented only on viral spikes. To elucidate the structural basis for PCDH1 recognition, we designed a soluble version of the ANDV spike tetramer by adding a hetero-octamerization domain at the C-terminal end of the Gn and Gc ectodomains (Fig. 1a). The octamerization sequence was derived from HALC4_136, a de novo tetramerization domain comprising three antiparallel helices per protomer20. We split this scaffold by fusing the first two helices to Gn and the third helix to Gc, so that co-expression of Gn and Gc would reconstitute a hetero-oligomeric assembly with the stoichiometry of the native spike (Extended Data Fig. 1a). We cloned both genes into a bicistronic plasmid, purified the complex from the supernatants of stably transfected Drosophila melanogaster S2 cells using affinity and size-exclusion chromatography and confirmed that it interacts with a truncated PCDH1 construct consisting of wild-type EC domains 1 to 4 (sEC1–4WT) using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA; Fig. 1b). As a negative control, we used a construct containing the substitutions Y81A and F83A (sEC1–4MUT), which are known to abrogate virus binding18. We also confirmed that incubation of the soluble spike with an excess of soluble sEC1WT, but not sEC1MUT, prevented the binding to sEC1–4WT (Fig. 1c). Furthermore, we used biolayer interferometry (BLI) to demonstrate specific spike–EC1 binding. Specifically, sEC1–4WT or sEC1–4MUT were immobilized on sensors, which were then dipped into solutions containing increasing concentrations of soluble spike (Fig. 1d). BLI assays performed in the opposite orientation corroborated these findings (Extended Data Fig. 1b).

To verify that the soluble spike proteins fold like the spikes observed on the viral surface, we performed cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM). We observed a few two-dimensional (2D) classes compatible with Gn–Gc tetramers (Extended Data Fig. 1c). However, a low-resolution three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction obtained from them was not compatible with the known ANDV spike structure5 (Extended Data Fig. 1d), suggesting that, in the absence of lateral contacts between spikes and the transmembrane regions, the intersubunit contacts within the spike are not sufficiently stable to maintain the intact eight-subunit assembly. We next hypothesized that prolonged incubation of the two proteins might help to stabilize the spike in its receptor-binding conformation. To test this hypothesis, we co-transfected S2 cells with the plasmids encoding sEC1–4WT and the soluble spike and purified the complex using double-affinity and size-exclusion chromatography. We performed cryo-EM studies and observed many new 2D classes showing tetramers (Extended Data Fig. 2 and Extended Data Table 1). Using particles from these classes, we reconstructed a map at a resolution of 2.4 Å, and we used the map to build a model of the spike that closely resembles the tetramers observed on viral particles5. The only notable differences between the two models are a slight rotation of the GnH domains relative to GnB and the poorly resolved density for domain III in the soluble spikes (Extended Data Fig. 3).

The cryo-EM map of the complex revealed an additional density at the centre of the spike, consistent in size with a single EC domain of PCDH1. However, as the map was processed using C4 symmetry, and the symmetry axis passes through the centre of the spike, we were unable to model this residual density. We reprocessed the map by combining a masked 3D classification with a non-uniform refinement using a pseudo-C4 symmetry. This approach yielded a new map at a resolution of 2.6 Å (Fig. 1e), in which we modelled PCDH1 EC1 at the centre of the spike (Fig. 1f,g). The final model showed the EC1 domain deeply inserted into a pocket formed by four protomers of Gn, establishing contacts with two neighbouring protomers (660 and 200 Å2, respectively) and sparse contacts with the other two (80 Å2 and 35 Å2). This mode of interaction, whereby one receptor molecule interacts with four viral glycoproteins, represents a binding mode that to our knowledge has not been observed in any enveloped virus, and explains why PCDH1 binds to viral particles but not to soluble GnH–Gc heterodimers. In the model, EC1 loops BCEC1 and FGEC1 interact with the AB and CD loops of GnH and the DE loop of GnB from two neighbouring protomers (Fig. 1g and Extended Data Fig. 4a). Notably, the densities corresponding to the DE and CD loops are weaker in the protomers that do not engage EC1 than in the receptor-contacting protomers (Extended Data Fig. 3b). This suggests that these loops are flexible and that PCDH1 binding stabilizes specific conformations of these regions. Indeed, comparison of the four protomers reveals that EC1 binding induces a conformational change in the CD and DE loops of one protomer, creating asymmetry within the pocket (Extended Data Fig. 3b). Previous studies have identified PCDH1 mutations that inhibit virus binding18, and all are in the BCEC1 loop or nearby regions, probably altering the conformation of the loop. Consistent with the structural data, mutations in the tip of the FG loop had little (D142A, D142R) or no (L143A, V144A, Q145A) effect on virus binding18, probably because none of them introduce steric restrictions (Fig. 1g).

To validate the model with viral particles, we studied the interaction between PCDH1 and virus-like particles (VLPs). We did not attempt to decorate preformed VLPs with soluble receptor because flow cytometry revealed only weak binding of EC1 to ANDV glycoproteins presented on the cell surface. Instead, reasoning that increasing the local concentration of receptor near the spike could enhance binding, we fused EC1 to the N terminus of the M segment through a flexible linker (Fig. 1a). To increase particle production, we introduced an EABR tag21 and the V535K and S1096L mutations, as reported previously5,22, and transfected the construct into FreeStyle293 cells. We analysed the secreted particles using cryo-EM and obtained a reconstruction of the spike at an overall resolution of 3.9 Å (Extended Data Fig. 5). The map revealed clear densities for Gn and Gc and an additional density at the centre of the spike (Fig. 1h). This central density was compatible in size and position with the PCDH1 EC1 domain observed in the soluble spike complex. However, the quality of this density did not allow unambiguous building of the EC1 domain, preventing a detailed comparison of the VLP–receptor interface with that obtained using the soluble spike (Extended Data Fig. 5c). This work nevertheless confirmed that PCDH1 EC1 occupies the central region of the Gn–Gc tetramer in viral particles.

To begin to investigate the roles of the CD and AB loops of GnH and the DE loop of GnB in spike–PCDH1 recognition, we produced rVSV-ANDV and rVSVs bearing the glycoproteins of the PCDH1-independent1,19 OWH HTNV (rVSV-HTNV), as well as rVSV-ANDV variants in which we replaced the GnH CD loop or all three loops in contact with the receptor (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 4a) with those from HTNV. After normalizing the amount of incorporated Gn–Gc in the viral particles (Extended Data Fig. 4b), we measured their interaction with immobilized PCDH1 EC1 on ELISA plates. Consistent with the known receptor specificity of hantaviruses and our structural data, PCDH1 EC1 could capture rVSV-ANDV but not rVSV-HTNV or any of the chimeric variants (Fig. 2a).

Next, we used a deep-mutational scanning (DMS) approach to more comprehensively survey the sequence landscape of the spike–PCDH1 interaction. We generated two independent Gn libraries, each introducing all possible amino acid substitutions at 427 of 448 positions in the ectodomain (amino acids 24–471; Methods) and used these to create libraries of alphavirus-based vectors pseudotyped with ANDV Gn–Gc (Sem-Hantavirus (SHV)). We captured SHVs bound to PCDH1 EC1 immobilized on ELISA plates and sequenced the PCDH1-bound SHV fractions to identify variants enriched or depleted relative to the input population. We observed strong selective signals for the GnH CD and AB loops and nearby regions in both populations, consistent with roles for these sequences in virus–receptor recognition (Fig. 2b,c and Extended Data Fig. 6). Relatively few variants were recovered from the GnB DE loop, consistent with its higher level of conservation (Extended Data Fig. 4a) and suggesting an important structural role for this sequence in the assembly and/or stability of the spike. Notably, none of the Gn ectodomain substitutions observed in the recent Hondius outbreak isolate of ANDV (LS-CH2016/2016-12-23/Switzerland) mapped to the PCDH1-binding pocket or were selected in the DMS experiments, indicating that this virus probably resembles previous isolates of the virus in its ability to recognize and use PCDH1 (Extended Data Figs. 4 and 6).

PCDH1 is a specific receptor for NWHs, and we wondered whether the structure of the complex could reveal insights into receptor use. We analysed the sequence conservation of the PCDH1-binding pocket across hantaviruses and compared the conformation of the loops using the available crystal structures of HTNV and SNV. We identified two amino acid substitutions: one in the GnH AB loop of SEOV, DOBV and HTNV (L36F) and one in the GnB DE loop of HTNV (G418L) that are predicted to cause steric clashes with PCDH1. By contrast, both the sequence and structure of the GnH CD loop vary substantially across hantaviruses—it is an extended loop in NWHs but a compact helix in OWHs (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 4). These findings suggest that it is not only the residues directly contacting PCDH1 that are important to the spike–receptor interaction but also nearby residues that influence the overall conformation of the loops, as is also indicated by the DMS data (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 6).

To assess the functional impact of these sequence variations, we compared PCDH1-binding rVSVs displaying the glycoproteins of ANDV (rVSV-ANDVWT), SNV (rVSV-SNVWT) and HTNV (rVSV-HTNVWT), along with variants in which we swapped the PCDH1-binding loops and nearby residues (Fig. 3b,c and Extended Data Fig. 4a). After normalizing the amount of incorporated glycoprotein in the rVSVs (Extended Data Fig. 4b), we measured PCDH1 binding by ELISA, as described above (Fig. 3b). As we showed previously1,18, rVSV-ANDVWT bound strongly to PCDH1, rVSV-SNVWT bound less strongly and rVSV-HTNVWT did not bind. The phenotypic effects of exchanging the receptor-binding loops between ANDV and SNV were largely consistent with the structural and mutagenesis data. Specifically, ANDV Gn bearing the SNV GnH CD loop bound worse than rVSV-ANDVWT, whereas SNV Gn bearing GnH CD or all three receptor-binding loops from ANDV Gn bound better than rVSV-SNVWT. Notably, a chimeric rVSV-HTNV bearing all three receptor-binding loops from ANDV Gn, but not the GnH CD loop alone, could now bind to PCDH1, albeit relatively weakly. This result is consistent with the sequence differences between ANDV and HTNV in the GnH AB and GnB DE loops and corroborates the proposed roles for these loops in ANDV spike–PCDH1 recognition.

The preceding binding experiments, in which sEC1–2 coated onto ELISA plates was used to capture rVSVs bearing hantavirus spikes, reflect the avid interaction between multiple immobilized PCDH1 molecules and the multiple spikes on each virion, which probably masks differences in the intrinsic affinity of each spike for PCDH1. Accordingly, we next assessed the ability of soluble, monomeric sEC1–2 to inhibit rVSV infection of PCDH1-bearing human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) in receptor-competition experiments, as described previously18 (Fig. 3c). We established the specificity of this assay with a binding-incompetent double-mutant sEC1–2MUT as a negative control (Fig. 3c). As expected1,18, sEC1–2 inhibited infection by rVSVs bearing WT hantavirus spikes in a manner that was positively related to their capacity to recognize PCDH1: HTNVWT << SNVWT < ANDVWT. The sensitivities to PCDH1 inhibition of the ANDV-SNV chimeras (SNVWT ≈ ANDVSNV-All < ANDVSNV-CD < ANDVWT), the SNV-ANDV chimeras (SNVWT < SNVANDV-CD < SNVANDV-All < ANDVWT) and the HTNV-ANDV chimeras (HTNVWT ≈ HTNVANDV-CD < HTNVANDV-All < ANDVWT) were also concordant with our binding results. Taken together, these findings broadly support the conclusion that the sequences shaping the central cavity are the major determinants of hantavirus spike–receptor recognition and of the distinct receptor tropisms of NWHs and OWHs.

The PCDH1-binding cavity is poorly accessible to conventional antibodies. Its dimensions—approximately 2.5 × 2.5 nm at its upper opening, narrowing to around 2.2 × 2.2 nm at the bottom, and around 2.1 nm in depth—are incompatible with those of a Fab fragment (5 × 4 × 7 nm). This raises the question of how some neutralizing antibodies block receptor engagement. To examine their mechanisms of action, we analysed four well-studied human neutralizing antibodies: ANDV-5 (ref. 23) and MIB22 (refs. 23,24), which were reported to interfere with the binding of soluble PCDH1 to spike-expressing cells; ANDV-34 (ref. 23), which does not block this interaction; and ADI-65534 (ref. 10), which was reported to block the binding of rVSVs to PCDH1 EC1–2 immobilized on ELISA plates. Structures of ANDV-5, ANDV-34 and ADI-65534 are available, but no structure had been reported for MIB22. We therefore determined the crystal structure of an antibody fragment of MIB22 in a complex with ANDV GnH at a resolution of 3.1 Å (Extended Data Table 2) and identified an epitope of about 900 Å2 that includes the three previously identified escape mutations23. Notably, MIB22 contacts several residues in the CD loop and recognizes a conformation of the loop different from that adopted after PCDH1 binding (Extended Data Fig. 7a). To assess whether antibody binding occludes the PCDH1-binding pocket, we modelled the variable domains of the four neutralizing antibodies onto the spike–PCDH1 complex (Fig. 4a). In parallel, we produced the four neutralizing antibodies as both full-length antibodies (IgGs) and single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) lacking the antibody constant regions and compared their ability to block rVSV-ANDV–PCDH1 binding by ELISA (Fig. 4b). We also compared the neutralizing activity of the four full-length antibodies against rVSV-ANDV in HUVECs, which express higher levels of PCDH1 than many immortalized cell lines of non-endothelial origin1 (Fig. 4c).

We observed that ADI-65534 and ANDV-34 do not occlude access to the central pocket, suggesting that they compete with PCDH1 through a steric hindrance mechanism mediated by the antibody constant regions (Fig. 4a). Consistent with this model, ELISA-based competition binding experiments showed that scFvs did not block the interaction of rVSVs with the receptor, whereas the IgGs did (Fig. 4b). Consistent with these observations, ANDV-34 has been reported not to block binding to the soluble receptor in flow-cytometry-based assays25. By contrast, the models with the Fabs of ANDV-5 and MIB22 showed that these antibodies bind much closer to the centre of the spike, directly occluding the PCDH1-binding site. ANDV-5 may additionally make limited clashes with the neighbouring protomer, potentially perturbing the conformation of the tetrameric spike. Concordant with these findings and previous observations25, ANDV-5 and MIB22 interfere with rVSV-ANDV–PCDH1 binding as both full-length antibodies and scFvs (Fig. 4b). In our experiments, the scFv of ANDV-5 blocked receptor binding tenfold more efficiently than its MIB22 counterpart (half-maximum inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of 23 and 240 nM, respectively), which may be explained by ANDV-5’s higher intrinsic affinity for the spike23. Overall, these results revealed that neutralizing antibodies elicited during viral infection can block receptor binding either directly, by occluding the receptor-binding site, or indirectly, by sterically preventing PCDH1 from accessing the spike.

Here we determined the structure of the ANDV spike bound to human PCDH1. We found that the receptor inserts one membrane-distal EC1 domain into a deep cavity formed by four Gn molecules in the fully assembled spike tetramer.

PCDH1 is a calcium-dependent cell–cell adhesion protein. Biochemical studies with soluble ectodomain fragments showed that PCDH1 can exist in a monomer–dimer equilibrium in solution18,26; however, the oligomeric distribution of full-length PCDH1 at the plasma membrane is not known. Structural studies showed that PCDH1 can form antiparallel homodimers through an EC1–EC4 interface26—a configuration that is incompatible with the receptor-binding mode described here because EC4 would not fit into the pocket. Previous studies with recombinant PCDH1 mutants engineered to remain monomeric did not reduce their interaction with rVSV-ANDV, whereas stabilization of the dimer decreased binding18. Overall, these data suggest that the virus engages monomeric PCDH1 at the cell surface, although dynamic PCDH1 dimers may also contribute if they transiently expose the EC1 domain.

The binding mode reported here explains why PCDH1 recognizes spikes on viral particles but not soluble glycoproteins and shows that receptor binding depends on both glycoprotein sequence and spike organization. The concept that receptors can recognize quaternary surfaces formed only in assembled viral glycoprotein complexes is not unique to hantaviruses, as the receptor-binding pocket for multiple alphaviruses is accessible only in the complete spike. For example, western equine encephalitis virus (WEEV) can accommodate multiple receptors, including protocadherin-10 (PCDH10)27,28,29,30, the very-low-density lipoprotein receptor (VLDLR)29,30 and MXRA8 (refs. 27,31), using the same pocket formed by adjacent E2–E1 heterodimers. It is interesting to compare the interactions between ANDV and PCDH1 with those between WEEV and PCDH10 because both receptors are non-clustered δ-cadherins32 and use their membrane-distal EC1 domain for viral engagement. However, the two viruses use distinct binding modes and protocadherin-binding surfaces. PCDH10 EC1 engages the alphavirus spike laterally through its A, G, F and C β-strands, whereas PCDH1 EC1 inserts axially into the central cavity of the hantavirus spike using its BC and FG loops. By analogy with WEEV, we propose that the pockets analogous to the PCDH1-binding pocket of ANDV and other NWHs may serve as binding sites for the distinct receptors used by OWHs and other non-PCDH1-using hantaviruses. Consistent with this idea, we found that variations in the receptor-binding loops of HTNV Gn, which normally cannot recognize PCDH1, enable a rVSV displaying the HTNV spike to bind PCDH1. Structural, sequence and mutagenesis studies further showed that the GnH CD loop is flexible, variable and tolerant to multiple mutations, suggesting that it could adapt to recognize alternative receptors. DMS data indicated that substitutions in the GnH CD and AB loops can alter affinity for PCDH1. Furthermore, the fact that the pocket is formed by four subunits may also facilitate viral adaptation to different receptors through a limited number of amino acid substitutions.

Another interesting parallel with alphaviruses concerns fusion activation. In alphaviruses, the quaternary interactions that form the receptor-binding site must be disrupted to allow membrane fusion, and MXRA8 binding has been proposed to modulate the pH threshold for fusion activation33. By contrast, the PCDH1-binding site does not involve the hantavirus fusion protein Gc, suggesting that receptor binding and membrane fusion by hantaviruses might be independent events. However, it has been reported that Gn adopts a different tetrameric arrangement at acidic pH7, raising the possibility that PCDH1 engagement may also influence fusion activation.

The neutralizing antibody response is essential for controlling hantavirus infections, as neutralizing titres correlate with HCPS severity34 and administration of human ANDV immune plasma reduced the case fatality rate in a small trial35. Antibodies may neutralize enveloped viruses by several non-mutually exclusive mechanisms, including by blocking viral attachment, membrane fusion or egress, inducing virion aggregation and, in vivo, by recruiting Fc-dependent effector functions36,37. Several neutralizing monoclonal antibodies targeting the ANDV spike have been described23,24,38. Some block viral membrane fusion by preventing dissociation of the pre-fusion heterodimer or the formation of the post-fusion trimer. Others interfere with PCDH1 engagement, but the lack of structural information has prevented a mechanistic understanding of how they do so. The structural data presented here show that the PCDH1-binding pocket is too narrow to readily accommodate a conventional antibody. We therefore analysed a panel of four human neutralizing antibodies to understand how they block receptor binding. We observed that ADI-65534 and ANDV-34 do not directly compete with PCDH1 but instead inhibit receptor binding through a steric interference mechanism mediated by the antibody constant region. This model of neutralization, in which antibody binding to accessible spike surfaces can prevent virus attachment without directly targeting the receptor-binding sites, has been reported for other viruses37,39,40,41, and suggests that strategies to increase antibody occupancy at the virion surface, such as combining non-competing antibodies targeting accessible epitopes, or focusing the response on exposed loops in the most apical regions of the tetramer, may enhance neutralizing activity against hantaviruses.

In contrast to ADI-65534 and ANDV-34, ANDV-5 and MIB22 directly occlude the PCDH1-binding pocket by targeting epitopes adjacent to, but not within, the central cavity. This recessed receptor-binding site is reminiscent of the canyon-like clefts described in picornaviruses, which inspired Rossmann’s canyon hypothesis. In its original form, this hypothesis proposed that receptor-binding residues located deep within a narrow cleft could remain accessible to the receptor but not to conventional antibodies, thereby reducing the immune pressure on the most conserved residues42,43. However, the subsequent description43 of HRV14-specific neutralizing antibodies of which the Fabs can penetrate the receptor-binding cleft showed that virus–host arms races could indeed produce antiviral solutions to this strategy. The preceding observations with HRV14 raise an interesting alternative possibility also noted by these authors43: that the recessed receptor-binding site affords the virus a second line of defence by allowing it to escape neutralization through amino acid substitutions that disrupt a more extensive and surface-exposed virus–antibody interface while sparing a smaller and more buried virus–receptor interface. Indeed, ANDV escape mutations to MIB22 are at residues distinct from, and more peripheral to, those that are critical for ANDV–PCDH1 recognition. Our work suggests that access to conserved sequences at the base of the receptor-binding cavity in the hantavirus spike is likely to require antibodies with extended CDR loops, single-domain antibodies or biologics designed to fit into the cavity. The identification of such molecules could yield exceptionally potent and broad antiviral inhibitors. Finally, because PCDH1 binds to a pocket that is present only in the fully assembled spike and hantavirus glycoproteins are structurally dynamic44, it may also be possible to identify antibodies that recognize epitopes partially buried in the intact spike and thereby prevent formation of the receptor-binding site.

The development of the SHV system, a Semliki Forest virus (SFV) replicon expressing an adaptively mutated ANDV Gn–Gc (Chilean field strain R123, GenBank: NP_604472.1) in place of its structural proteins in the pCMV-SFV4 plasmid61 to study hantavirus entry, will be described elsewhere (L.K. et al., manuscript submitted). Transfection of a single (pSHV-ANDV) plasmid encoding the SHV genome into Huh7.5.1 cells produces self-replicating viruses, enabling the genotype–phenotype link required for deep mutagenesis scanning of Gn–Gc proteins. To generate SHV Gn mutants encompassing amino acids 24–471 of its ectodomain, the Gn sequence was divided into six sections based on the availability of unique restriction sites: lib1 (amino acids 24–92), lib2 (amino acids 101–166), lib3 (amino acids 170–230), lib4 (amino acids 235–312), lib5 (amino acids 316–386) and lib6 (amino acids 390–471). The lib1 sequence was flanked by NruI (5′ end) and AclI (3′ end); lib2 by AclI (5′ end) and PspXI (3′ end); lib3 by PspXI (5′ end) and XmnI (3′ end); lib4 by XmnI (5′ end) and BbvCI (3′ end); lib5 by BbvCI (5′ end) and XmaI (3′ end); and lib6 by XmaI (5′ end) and BlpI (3′ end). Site-saturation variant libraries of oligo pools carrying single amino acid changes, with one codon per amino acid for all 20 amino acids, including the parental sequence, were synthesized by Twist Biosciences and cloned into the pSHV vector by digesting the vector at the corresponding restriction sites and assembling the fragments using NEB HiFi DNA assembly. The parental Gn sequence was also included in each library. The selected codons for each amino acid were optimal for human cell expression: Ala, GCC; Cys, TGC; Asp, GAC; Glu, GAG; Phe, TTC; Gly, GGC; His, CAC; Ile, ATC; Lys, AAG; Leu, CTG; Met, ATG; Asn, AAC; Pro, CCC; Gln, CAG; Arg, CGC; Ser, AGC; Thr, ACC; Val, GTG; Trp, TGG; and Tyr, TAC. The sequences of the assembled plasmid libraries were verified by Illumina MiSeq amplicon sequencing (see below for details).

Individual pSHV-ANDV sublibrary (lib1–6) plasmid pools were transfected using Lipofectamine-3000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions, at 10 μg per 100 mm plate of Huh7.5.1 cells, and SHVs were collected at 72 h after transfection to generate passage 0 stocks. To eliminate trans-complementation of dysfunctional Gn–Gc variants by parental SHV or other functional variants, the library was passaged twice at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1 in Huh7.5.1 cells (a gift from C. M. Rice62 and F. V. Chisari63). Viral supernatants collected at passage 2 were clarified by centrifugation at 4,500 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min, repeated twice. Viruses in the clarified supernatant were concentrated by ultracentrifugation at 25,000 rpm at 4 °C for 1:45 h. The virus pellet was resuspended in 1× serum-free DMEM and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The resuspended pellet was then overlaid on a 10% sucrose cushion and ultracentrifuged at 25,000 rpm at 4 °C for 1:45 h. Subsequently, viral pellets were resuspended in 1× NT Buffer (10 mM Tris-Cl, 135 mM NaCl, pH 7.5) and stored at −80 °C before further downstream analysis. The SHV titres were estimated by immunostaining naive Huh7.5.1 cells infected with limiting dilutions of SHVs for expression of SFV non-structural protein 1 (nsP1) at 12 h after infection, using rabbit antisera (1:300) provided by A. To enrich PCDH1-binding SHV particles, a sEC1–2 capture ELISA was performed as described previously1,18. First, the amounts of SHV particles across the sublibraries and parental SHVs were normalized by labelling the pelleted SHV particle membranes with FSL-biotin (functional spacer di-acyl lipid probe conjugated to biotin) (Sigma-Aldrich). Equal amounts of FSL-biotin (10 μg ml−1) and pelleted SHVs were mixed and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h for labelling. Serial twofold dilutions of labelled SHVs were coated on a high-binding ELISA plate (Corning) at 37 °C for 1 h. After removing unbound virus and washing with 1× PBS three times for 5 min each, the wells were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk (Bio-Rad) prepared in 1× PBS for 1 h at 37 °C. The FSL-labelled SHVs were detected using a high-sensitivity streptavidin-HRP conjugated antibody (BioLegend) at a final dilution of 1:3,000 after incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. The ELISA signal was developed using Ultra-TMB ELISA substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and measured at 450 nm on the Cytation-5 multi-mode plate reader (BioTek, Agilent). SHV input amounts for the sEC1–2 capture ELISA for each sublibrary were calculated from half-maximum effective concentration values that were obtained by fitting the data to a sigmoidal four-parameter logistic model, with the logarithm of the virus amount on the x axis, using Prism GraphPad v.11.

For the sEC1–2 capture ELISA, high-affinity 96-well ELISA plates (Corning) were coated with sEC1-2 (100 ng per well) at 4 °C overnight. The next day, after a single wash with 1× PBS, the plates were blocked with 5% blotting-grade non-fat dry milk (Bio-Rad) in 1× PBS for 1 h at 37 °C. Normalized amounts of pelleted parental and sublibrary SHVs labelled with FSL-biotin, as described above, were applied to the sEC1–2-coated wells for 1 h at 37 °C. After three thorough washes with 1× PBS to remove unbound SHVs, the bound viruses were processed for RNA isolation and PCR with reverse transcription (RT–PCR) as described below. Based on infectious units, sufficient SHV particles were included to ensure 1,000× coverage in the input of each sublibrary for the capture ELISA, and 10–12 wells per sublibrary were pooled for RNA isolation. For each capture ELISA, a parallel set of wells was processed for streptavidin–HRP-based detection of bound SHV particles, as described above for the normalization ELISA.

PCDH1-bound SHVs from independent biological duplicates for each sublibrary were processed by adding 50 μl of Zymogen RNA lysis buffer per well, incubating at room temperature for 8 min, and then isolating RNA using the Quick RNA Viral Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Zymo Research). Input SHVs for each sublibrary were also sequenced to establish the baseline. cDNA was synthesized using the SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis System (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with a reverse primer (5′-ATTGCCTACCCTAACACTAGTTGCAG-3′) located downstream of the Gn–Gc sequence. Sufficient viral RNA was used to ensure 1,000× coverage of each sublibrary. cDNA synthesis was followed by PCR with ANDV Gn-specific primers tagged at their 5′-ends with Illumina MiSeq adapters for the forward (5′-TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAG-3′) and reverse (5′-GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAG-3′) sequences for each sublibrary. The sequences of the Gn-specific part of these primers were as follows: lib1-F, 5′-GTGCTATACTCTGACTCTCGCGATG-3′ and lib1-R, 5′- CGGTCTTTGTCTGTGCCTCAAAC-3′; lib2-F, 5′-CCACCAACGCAGCTTCCAC-3′ and lib2-R 5′-TTCTCATATATCACCTGTATCCTGTATCCTCGAGGT-3′; lib3-F, 5′-CTTGCATGGCCTCTGTGTTCAC-3′ and lib3-R, 5′-TGTGCACCCTGTCTTGGTGA-3′; lib4-F, 5′-AGCTGAAGGTGATCAAAACATTTGAAGG-3′ and lib4-R 5′-ACCCCGGCGAAAGCTGTC-3′; lib5-F, 5′-GTTCCTTCAACCTCTTCTACAGATACGC-3′ and lib5-R, 5′-TGTAGGCCTCGCAAGAAGCA-3′; and lib6-F, 5′-TGTTCTGTACTCTGGCCGGC-3′ and lib6-R, 5′-AGGGACACAGTTCGACGG-3′. Cycling conditions for the PCR were initial denaturation at 98 °C for 1 min; followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 15 s, annealing at 65 °C for 30 s, extension at 72 °C for 30 s; and then a final extension step at 72 °C for 5 min. After verification of amplified PCR products on a 1% agarose gel, the products were purified using Ampure XP beads and indexed by a second round of PCR, followed by another cleanup with Ampure XP beads. Completed libraries were assessed by gel electrophoresis using the automated Agilent TapeStation D1000 Kit (Agilent Technologies) and quantified using the Qubit dsDNA BR Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Libraries were normalized to 40 nM, pooled and diluted to 4 nM. The 4 nM pool was denatured and diluted to 12.5 pM, then spiked with 10% Phi X DNA to increase base diversity. Paired-end 2 × 250 sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeq system using the Illumina MiSeq Reagent v2 kit (Illumina). Primary data analysis, base calling and quality scoring were performed using the onboard MiSeq Control Software v.4.1.0.480 and RTA v.1.18.54.4. Samples were demultiplexed, adapter sequences were trimmed and FASTQ files were generated for downstream analysis.

The FASTQ files were aligned to the parental reference sequence using the Burrows–Wheeler Alignment Tool (BWA, v.0.7.17). Sequencing reads were processed using a custom Python-based DMS pipeline. Paired-end reads were first filtered to retain only high-quality sequences that (1) matched expected primer regions within defined mismatches; (2) had limited ambiguous base calls (N); and (3) showed sufficient overlap between R1 and R2 reads. Trimmed reads were merged, and coding regions were translated to protein sequences using a codon dictionary described above. To identify valid single amino acid variants, translated protein sequences were compared to the parental sequence. Only sequences differing by one amino acid and free of stop codons were retained. For each variant, the mutation position (adjusted by an offset to match full-length protein numbering) and substituted residue were recorded. Mutation counts were aggregated per position and normalized to compute the relative frequency of each variant, generating a position-wise matrix of single amino acid substitutions. These results were saved in a CSV file and used for downstream analysis.

For each set (input and bound replicates), CSVs containing Mut_type, Mutation_count and Position_count were parsed to extract a numeric position from the leading digits of Mut_type (for example, 24C → 24) and define Variant = Mut_type. Variant-level enrichment in each bound replicate versus input was quantified using a smoothed within-position frequency with pseudocount pc = 0.5: freq = (mutation_count + pc)/(position_count + 2 pc), and enrichment was reported as log2[FC]= log2[freq_bound/freq_input]. For each variant, a two-sided Fisher’s exact test compared the variant versus all other reads at that position between bound and input using a 2 × 2 table (mut, nonmut=position_count−mutation_count), followed by Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction per replicate. Reproducibility across bound replicates was assessed by Pearson/Spearman correlation of log2[FC] values and by calling variants reproducibly enriched/depleted if they had the same sign of log2[FC] in both replicates and FDR < 0.05 in both. Finally, an input-only filter was applied: only variants with input mutation_count ≥ 10, an arbitrary limit, were retained for the filtered master workbook (bound counts were not filtered). A custom Python code used to process the raw FASTQ files to variant counts per position is available at GitHub (https://github.com/Jwang30/DMS_Calling).

Flowchart illustrating the data processing steps used to obtain the high-resolution structure of the spike/receptor complex. Particle counts and filters used at each step are indicated. Movies were collected on a Titan Krios (POLARIS, Synchrotron Soleil) equipped with a FALCON 4i detector (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating at a calibrated pixel size of 0.7455 Å/px. After motion correction and CTF estimation, particles were picked sequentially by blob picking and template picking and extracted with a 52 px box size at 5.964 Å/px. Junk particles were removed by iterative runs of 2D classification, and the final set was reextracted with a 416 px box size at 0.7455 Å/px. An initial map was obtained after a one-class Ab-initio reconstruction followed by a Non-Uniform refinement using C4 symmetry. To improve the quality of the central density across the fourfold symmetry axis, a 3D classification (5 classes, 6 Å filter) was performed using the focus mask shown as a transparent magenta surface in the figure. All the particles with an additional density in the centre of the spike were selected and the final map was obtained after a Non-Uniform refinement with C4 symmetry using the marginalization symmetry relaxation method. For model building the map was sharpened using DeepEMhancer. All the workflow was performed using cryoSPARC v4.6.2.

#1092. Merz, Macron seek tougher EU measures against unfair trade

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Emmanuel Macron and Friedrich Merz have written to Ursula von der Leyen, calling for tougher EU mechanisms against unfair trade practices by other states. They name no names but seem to have China or the US in mind.

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz and French President Emmanuel Macron sent a joint appeal to the European Commission on Monday, calling for tougher EU measures against unfair trade practices by other states.

They said that the existing instruments at the EU's disposal were no longer sufficient.

The appeal did not name any specific country but it seemed most clearly tied to Chinese trade policy, the EU's vast deficit with China, and its dependence on certain key raw materials. The joint letter to European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen called for two new measures to be introduced in Brussels.

One would aim at ensuring diversification and preventing companies from becoming too dependent on suppliers from individual companies.

The other would allow another country to be excluded from access to the European single market on an expedited basis if exceptional circumstances made this necessary.

Merz and Macron wrote that this could be used in cases "where third countries deliberately seek to undermine the restoration of a level playing field and fair market conditions through political or economic means, leading to serious and systematic distortions of our single market."

Monday's letter is what's known in EU diplomatic circles as a "non-paper." These are nonbinding, informal documents shared between EU member states and institutions that are supposed to propose ideas and initiate discussions without officially committing to a position.

It recommends authorizing the Commission to exclude a country from single market access in extreme situations if it sees the need — currently a complicated process that would take some time to arrange and requires approval from member states.

It suggests reversing this process: allowing the Commission to take the step independently, but with member states then having the chance to block it with a qualified majority vote, which the EU currently defines as a vote by a minimum of 55% of member states that also represents a minimum of 65% of the bloc's total population.
