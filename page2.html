#3377. Biggest predictor of whether college students flourish revealed in new study - and it's not sleep or studying

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Please refresh the page or navigate to another page on the site to be automatically logged inPlease refresh your browser to be logged in

Mental health challenges among young people have become an increasing public health concern

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

Exercise, avoiding alcohol or even getting enough sleep aren’t the factors most connected to college students’ well-being.

Spending face-to-face time with friends and other people is the strongest predictor of their mental health, according to a 2026 study of nearly 80,000 students that I conducted with the help of my students.

This finding matters because young people are spending less time together in person and more time communicating through screens.

Between 2010 and 2022, the percentage of U.S. high school seniors who reported seeing friends face-to-face almost every day declined from 44% to 32%.

Against this backdrop, mental health challenges among young people have become an increasing public health concern. Suicide remains the second-leading cause of death among Americans ages 15 to 34.

But mental health is about more than the absence of mental illness. My student researchers and I wanted to ask a somewhat different question: What helps young people flourish?

To answer that question, we created a broad measure of well-being that captured more than the lack of mental health problems, such as psychological distress. It also included positive aspects of life, such as having supportive relationships, hobbies and a sense of meaning and purpose.

Our research team analyzed responses from 79,246 college students across the United States and Canada. We examined 51 aspects of students’ lives, including their substance use, the discrimination they encountered, their financial stress, any sleep problems and different relationship challenges.

We also examined how students spent their free time, whether that meant exercising, studying, socializing, meditating, playing video games or scrolling on social media.

We used statistical methods that allowed us to compare dozens of potential influences on student well-being simultaneously, helping us identify which factors were most strongly associated with flourishing.

Among the dozens of factors we examined, six stood out as the most important and influential.

The strongest factor connected to student flourishing was remarkably ordinary: spending time with other people. Students who spent more hours socializing with friends, attending student organizations and participating in cultural events tended to report feeling an overall sense of well-being.

The second most important factor surprised us. Students who spent more time on obligations, including paid work and unpaid family responsibilities, also tended to report a greater sense of well-being.

Roxanne Prichard is a Professor of Psychology at University of St. Thomas. This article is republished from The Conversation under a Creative Commons licence. Read the original article .

Our results raise an intriguing possibility: Well-being may come not only from leisure and self-care but also from contributing to something larger than oneself.

While problematic substance use, passive screen time and sleep problems tended to compromise well-being, these factors weren’t the biggest detriment. It was difficulty navigating interpersonal relationships, including problems with friends, romantic partners and roommates.

Making friends, maintaining healthy relationships and working through conflicts requires practice, and some of that practice time now competes with communication mediated by screens.

Notably, the contributors to student flourishing were similar among students diagnosed with anxiety or depression and those without these diagnoses. That suggests these everyday experiences may matter across a broad spectrum of mental health.

Supporting students’ mental and emotional health is not simply a matter of reducing screen time. It also means making it easier for young people to spend time together in person, whether through clubs, recreation programs, community events or other welcoming spaces.

Counseling and educational programs can likewise help students build friendships, communicate effectively and navigate conflict in healthy ways.

None of this means mental illness can be solved by telling a struggling young person to put down their phone, join a club or get more sleep. Mental health conditions are complex, and people experiencing them need access to effective professional care.

But our findings suggest that helping young people thrive requires more than treating symptoms. It also requires cultivating supportive relationships, healthy sleep and meaningful engagement in work, school and family life.

#3400. Apple is about to launch a product it said it never would

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Please refresh the page or navigate to another page on the site to be automatically logged inPlease refresh your browser to be logged in

Executives have long dismissed the idea of a touchscreen Mac – but it is finally coming, and Apple is rumoured to have plans to fix its central problems

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

Apple is about to release a whole new product – one that it previously explicitly derided.

The company will launch its first touchscreen Mac during an event on Wednesday, according to numerous reports. It will be a new version of the MacBook Pro, which will come with an upgraded display and a new lighter design in addition to its touch-sensitive screen, rumours suggest.

Apple’s leaders – including its new chief executive John Ternus – have repeatedly suggested that such a product was a bad idea, in large part because of the ergonomic troubles of reaching up to a computer’s screen to touch its display. That criticism goes all the way back to 2010, when co-founder and former chief executive Steve Jobs described the idea as “ergonomically terrible”,.

But Apple has planned a host of changes to address those problems, new reports suggest. Chief among them is the idea that the touchscreen Mac will still be focused around the keyboard and trackpad as its primary interface, Bloomberg reported.

It will stress that the Mac is not becoming a touch-first computer and that the new model’s software will remain optimised for the usual experience, the report said. But it will also come with a new update to its Mac operating system, Golden Gate, aimed at making it easier to use with a finger.

The software will feature what Bloomberg calls a “dynamic interface”, which will be able to switch between the usual controls and those redesigned for touch, and remember which they have done more recently. The aim is to allow people to switch between the two input methods without thinking about it, Bloomberg reported.

The report suggested however that concerns about the ergonomics of using the device remain, and that some inside Apple had resisted the idea as a result. The company has been convinced by the idea that it could bring new customers to the Mac as well as help keep momentum around its line-up of computers – which had for a long time been losing ground in favour of its other products, but have come back in recent years because of working from home and its recent hardware advances.

There have also long been concerns that a touchscreen Mac could hurt sales of the iPad. But Apple has more recently come to believe that the two could complement each other – with software, performance and versatility helping to keep them separate – and has also added Mac-style features to its recent updates to the iPad.

The event on Wednesday follows a series of Apple launches in recent weeks, many of which introduced new kinds of products or innovative versions of its existing ones. The last two weeks alone have seen the introduction of the HomeView, a smart speaker with a display for inside people’s houses, as well as the release of the iPhone Duo, Apple’s first folding device.

Chancellor Friedrich Merz will be meeting the leaders of Singapore, Japan and South Korea as Germany seeks to adjust its foreign policy to rising uncertainty and threats to global supply chains.

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz kicked off five-day tour of Asia on Monday, traveling to Singapore before his scheduled visits to Japan and South Korea. Merz is accompanied by an entourage of 30 senior businesspeople, including representatives of Germany's industry, tech and defense companies, and energy suppliers.

At the same time, Germany's President Frank-Walter Steinmeier is visiting China.

While the trips are not officially related, they both seem aimed at boosting Berlin's economic and security relationships at a time of geopolitical instability which threatens global supply chains.

In Singapore, Merz is set to deliver a major foreign policy speech and meet Prime Minister Lawrence Wong. He will then travel to Japan on Wednesday to meet with Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi and take part in the German-Japanese Business Summit.

Merz's visit "will be very much welcomed in Tokyo," said Ben Ascione, an assistant professor of politics and international relations at Tokyo's Waseda University.

"For more than a decade, Japan has been looking to generate new security allies and closer trade partnerships," he told DW.

Joseph Nixon, 43, abused guests attending the festivities at Liverpool's historic Adelphi Hotel in the early hours of September 15 last year, magistrates in the city heard today.

The chef confronted Jack Surpree, who had been best man at the wedding, after spotting him with his teenage sister in the reception area at about 12.45am, Louise Coe, prosecuting, told the court.

Nixon asked him whether he supported Palestine and then, after Mr Surpree ignored him, whether he supported Israel.

The court heard Mr Surpree then 'clearly' heard him say: 'You Jewish bastards.'

In a statement, Mr Surpree said he went to take his phone out of his jacket pocket and saw the man's facial expression change, which made him 'fear he was going to become violent' towards him and his sister.

He said Nixon and another man who was with him then entered the function room where he could hear 'aggressive' shouts of 'Jews are murderers' and 'you guys are killing babies'.

The incident was said to have lasted seven to eight minutes before Nixon was arrested.

Joseph Nixon, 43, abused guests at a Jewish wedding held in a function room at Liverpool's historic Adelphi Hotel in the early hours of September 15, 2025, a court heard today

'That is not who he is and he has full empathy for Mr Surpree for the distress he has caused him.'

Nixon was found guilty after a trial of racially or religiously aggravated intentional harassment.

Jamie Oliver’s wife Jools Oliver has revealed that she has been diagnosed with autism and ADHD, saying that not understanding her neurodivergence “ruined” her life for 45 years.

Clothing brand owner Jools shared the news during a podcast interview, stating: “I just think it’s quite draining. I think it’s tiring. I think, actually, it’s ruined, so far, 45 years of my life thinking it’s something else. It’s no superpower. It’s just a drain and it’s just really hard work.”

She revealed that she decided to announce her diagnosis to her friends and family via an invitation to her 50th birthday party last year.

Jools, who suffers from social anxiety, said on Lessons From Our Mothers podcast: “My 50th birthday invite, I decided to come out.” She told her chef husband: “Jamie, I'm going to tell everyone that I’m not normal because I put on such a great act of being really like, ‘Woo,’ and so social. In fact, it’s like my worst night. I’m such an introvert.’”

She said that “a lot of people didn’t come and were turned off”, but she thought to herself: “Well, this is me. I’m afraid this is the new 50. This is it.’”

The 51-year-old went on to say that she is “scared” to take prescribed medication due to her health anxiety. “I’ve got the medication, but I don't want to take it because I’m scared due to health anxiety that something will happen,” she explained, calling it “one ongoing situation”.

Jools said that, following her attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) diagnosis, she decided to get tested for autism after realising that she shares behavioural habits with her children, who were diagnosed with autism.

She said that she “just feels things so deeply”, adding: “I get so dramatic over things and I can be really up and down. And the empathy sometimes is painful.

“I don’t know whether that’s just a personality choice. Sometimes you don’t even know, or is it just the menopause? Am I just going through the menopause and I’m really going bonkers? I think when you do have neurodiversity, menopause is a double nightmare.”

Jamie has previously discussed his wife’s neurodivergence, without revealing she had ADHD or autism, during a 2025 appearance on Davina McCall’s Begin Again podcast.

“Of course, there’s positives, but there’s loads of things that, you know – she makes us the way that we are. And I’m very grateful for Jools.”

The pair, who have four children, met as teenagers in 1993, and have been in a relationship for 33 years, and married since 2000.

NHS England figures shared in 2025 estimate that nearly 2.5 million people in England have ADHD, including those who are undiagnosed. The figures mark the first publicly available estimate of how common the condition is among the population.

Research also found earlier this year that a significant majority of autistic individuals over 40 in the UK are living without an official diagnosis. Researchers at King’s College London’s Institute of Psychiatry, Psychology & Neuroscience (IoPPN) reanalysed 2018 UK healthcare data, estimating that a staggering 91.45 per cent of autistic men and 79.48 per cent of autistic women aged 40 to 59 remain undiagnosed.

It found that 96.29 per cent of men and 97.19 per cent of women with autism over 60 are thought to be undiagnosed, with an overall rate of 96.51 per cent. Symptoms can include having specific and obsessive interests, a reliance on structure and difficulty in understanding social cues.
