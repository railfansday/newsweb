#3968. Brazil’s economic problems, in a nutshell

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

It would take true Milei-like ardour to slice through Brazil’s economic troubles. These are caused by a gnarly mix of high public spending, stubborn inflation and low saving. The result is a crippling cost of capital. Some analysts put Brazil’s real interest rate, the BCB’s policy rate adjusted for next year’s expected inflation, at 9% or more. That is higher than in Russia and four times that in peaceful peers like Chile, the Philippines or South Africa.

It all starts with the public finances. Brazil’s 65,000-word doorstop of a constitution bars cuts to nominal public-sector wages and links pension, disability and unemployment benefits to the minimum wage, which is adjusted annually based on recent inflation and GDP growth. This ratchet has lifted mandatory spending to 90% of the federal budget.

It also perpetuates inflationary pressure. This plus a generous welfare state in turn make Brazilians unwilling to save. At 14% of GDP, the national savings rate is 12 percentage points below the world average. If price rises have regularly eroded your bank balance in the past and the state pays for your health care, school and pension, you may as well spend all the money you make right away—especially if, like most people in unequal Brazil, you do not make much to begin with.

#4353. How will pedestrianisation of UK’s most famous shopping street work in practice?

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

A stretch of London’s Oxford Street is to be vehicle-free as part of £150m government-backed scheme

Much of Britain’s most famous shopping street will be closed to traffic from Monday as the long-awaited pedestrianisation of London’s Oxford Street begins.

About half of the 1.2 mile street will initially be vehicle-free, after decades of planning and dispute over how to best use the street, an important transport thoroughfare and the home for flagship stores for big shopping chains.

Private cars have been barred for years but now all motorised traffic, including buses and black cabs, as well as bicycles, will be kept away.

For the London mayor, Sadiq Khan, who has targeted the switch as part of clean air plans throughout his tenure, it is a political victory, aided by the backing of central government to override local council objections.

While towns and cities around the UK have long pedestrianised their centres, the location, scale and notoriety of Oxford Street, attracting millions of foreign visitors each year, will make the £150m scheme’s outcome of interest to city planners around the world.

London has studied the pedestrianisation of central Sydney, and discussed the scheme with Barcelona, whose La Rambla is similarly viewed as shabbier than its renown deserves.

The big retailers, Transport for London and those who just want a pleasanter, green city centre will be hoping the changes live up to their promise. Here we unpack what is changing:

From 7am on 26 October, a stretch of nearly 0.6 miles (almost 1km) of Oxford Street, containing the most famous department stores including John Lewis and Selfridges, will be vehicle-free.

The junction with Orchard Street, near Marble Arch, to Great Portland Street, east of Oxford Circus Ikea, will be closed to all traffic between 7am and midnight on every day of the week.

Businesses will be able to make deliveries outside those hours via certain access routes, but vehicles will not be able to travel the length of Oxford Street.

Private vehicles, such as cars, are already forbidden to drive on the street between 7am and 7pm and this ban will now extended to 24 hours a day seven days a week and cover buses, black cabs and bikes too.

Vehicles will still be able to cross the pedestrianised stretch of street at several points including the major junction of Regent Street and Oxford Circus, and others including New Bond Street.

Bus routes including the numbers 7, 98 and 390 will now run along Wigmore Street, which runs parallel to Oxford Street.

There will be taxi ranks within 100m of the traffic-free area on Oxford Street and surrounding roads.

In contrast to some other traffic-free areas of London, cycles, e-bikes and e-scooters will not be allowed on the pedestrianised strip at any time.

Mobility scooters will still be allowed. Access for disabled people may prove easier than many other parts of London, with Bond Street and Tottenham Court Road stations, on the Underground and Elizabeth line, both step-free.

Emergency services will be able to access the entire area whenever required via the cross streets. Cargo e-bikes will be introduced to collect litter and enable street cleaning during pedestrianised hours.

Khan has talked about making Oxford Street a public plaza with outdoor dining, and improved street furniture and lighting is coming.

Early next year, more trees and seating are due to be installed, while the road surface will be raised level with the pavements in places to create public squares and event spaces.

The pedestrianisation of this stretch of the landmark shopping street has repeatedly been described as the “first phase”, although plans for the rest of the thoroughfare are likely to depend on the success – or otherwise – of the scheme.

#4271. Circles – The Secrets of the Stones: 5 Real Places to Visit

A Cambridge professor vanishes in 1843. Generations later, three teenagers find his coded notebook and a glowing crystal deep in England’s New Forest. Then the trail leads them to ancient stone circles. That is the setup of Circles – The Secrets of the Stones, the debut novel of David Horsup. It comes out on 27 October 2026.

So why does a travel blog care about a novel? First, the places in the story are real, and you can visit every one of them. Second, the author’s proceeds support a literacy charity that brings books to children in Mindanao.

This guide starts with what Circles – The Secrets of the Stones is about. After that, it walks through the five real places behind it: the New Forest, Stonehenge, Framlingham Castle, Brú na Bóinne in Ireland and the Ring of Brodgar in Orkney. For each one, you get what it is and how to reach it. You also get what a Philippine passport holder needs to sort out first, such as the UK visa fee in pesos.

Early reader Vron Earp of Manchester called it “a wonderful C.S. Lewis feel, a journey filled with discovery, self-reflection, and wonder.” Alistair Madle in Seville wrote that it is “a story that stays with you long after you’ve finished.” So if your teenager finished Narnia and wants the next thing, this book is for them.

Every place in this guide is in the UK except one. Brú na Bóinne is in the Republic of Ireland, and that matters because the two countries run separate visa systems.

UK. Philippine passport holders need a UK Standard Visitor visa. As of October 2026, it costs £135 for a stay of up to 6 months. That is around PHP 11,400 at about PHP 84 to the pound. You can apply up to 3 months before you travel. After that, gov.uk says you will usually get a decision within 3 weeks. You give your biometrics at the VFS Global UK visa centres in Manila and Cebu. Ireland. A UK visa on its own does not cover Ireland, and Filipinos are on Ireland’s visa-required list. The standard Irish short stay visa costs €60 for a single journey or €100 for multiple journeys. That is roughly PHP 4,250 or PHP 7,100. But there is a useful shortcut: the Philippines is on Ireland’s Short Stay Visa Waiver Programme. With a valid UK short-stay “C” visa, you can travel on to Ireland from the UK without a separate Irish visa. However, you must enter the UK first. Then you can stay 90 days, or until your UK permission runs out if that comes sooner. Fly to Ireland without first entering the UK and the waiver does not apply.

In the book, the teenagers find the weathered satchel, the glowing crystal and the professor’s coded notebook here. In real life, the New Forest is a national park in southern England. It became a National Park in 2005, and its most famous residents are the ponies. However, they are not wild in the strict sense. Local “commoners” own the ponies, cattle and pigs that roam freely, and they have the right to graze them there.

Getting there is easy without a car. The fastest trains from London Waterloo to Brockenhurst take about 1 hour 30 minutes. From there, the forest starts right around the village. The animals graze freely, so give them space.

It is one of the cheaper stops in this guide, as the forest itself is free to explore.

Stonehenge is the best known of the book’s stone circles. English Heritage dates the first monument here, an early henge, to about 5,000 years ago. The stone circle itself dates to about 2500 BC. Together with Avebury it forms the heart of a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

What surprises first-time visitors is the logistics. Salisbury is the nearest train station, about 19 km away. From there Salisbury Reds runs a hop-on hop-off tour bus to the site. At Stonehenge, you arrive at the visitor centre first. Then shuttle buses run every few minutes from there to the stones. Tickets are timed, and English Heritage says booking early saves up to 15 percent. If you buy on the day, you pay full price.

If you would rather not juggle trains and buses from London, a guided day trip handles all of it. Compare Stonehenge day trips from London on GetYourGuide.

Want to stay nearby and see the stones early? Salisbury is the natural base.

Suffolk is where David Horsup grew up, and Framlingham Castle is one of the real Suffolk places in the book. English Heritage describes it as a late 12th-century castle. Roger Bigod II built its huge stone curtain walls. Its most famous moment came in July 1553, when Mary Tudor fled here to gather her troops. She arrived on 12 July, and thousands of her supporters soon flocked to the castle.

The highlight is the wall-top walk around the defences. English Heritage promises “800 years of history from above” there. However, it also says some sections close for maintenance from 24 September, so check the castle page before you go.

From London, take the train to Ipswich, then on to Wickham Market, about 26 minutes further. Wickham Market station is 6.5 miles from the castle. Then an electric minibus service called Katch takes you on to Framlingham. Booking advance tickets online saves 15 percent.

The Irish stop in the book is Brú na Bóinne in County Meath. It is home to the passage tombs of Newgrange and Knowth. Ireland’s heritage service dates them to around 3200 BC. In fact, Meath County Council says they are older than the main pyramids at Giza by about 400 years. They also predate the main trilithon phase of Stonehenge by about 500 to 700 years.

On winter solstice mornings, a shaft of sunlight enters the Newgrange chamber through a special opening above the doorway. So the visitor centre opens early from 19 to 23 December, and it stops regular tours on those days.

The practical rule to remember: you cannot walk up to the monuments. All access is through the Brú na Bóinne Visitor Centre. From there, a shuttle bus takes visitors to the tombs, and you must book ahead. Turn up directly at Newgrange and you will not get in. The site is about 40 to 50 km north of Dublin. Historic Environment Scotland says 36 of the original 60 stones survive, and the circle is 104 metres across. It is part of the Heart of Neolithic Orkney World Heritage Site.

Better news for the budget: the Ring of Brodgar is free to visit and open year-round. The hard part is getting to Orkney. Loganair flies direct to Kirkwall from Edinburgh, Glasgow and Inverness. #4084. Private equity pours billions into defense tech as investors debate a bubble

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Private equity firms are pouring billions into defense technology as rising military spending and geopolitical tensions fuel an investment boom.

Traditional buyout investors are increasingly backing younger, high-growth firms in the sector, according to PitchBook data, with private equity growth investments in defense reaching $4 billion in the first nine months of 2026, up from $500 million in 2024.

As defense tech companies mature, analysts say private equity, debt and other forms of financing are expected to become more important in bridging the gap between innovation and industrial-scale production, as venture capital alone may not be enough to finance companies' expansion.

It comes as geopolitical tensions, wars, and countries' efforts to strengthen military capabilities are creating opportunities for companies developing everything from autonomous drones to missile defense systems and satellite technology.

A survey of 250 senior decision-makers at U.K. defense companies by law firm White & Case found that 97% expected their businesses to seek significant external funding over the next 12 months.

"Private capital is now playing a critical role in the financing of defence technology companies," Daniel Turgel, a partner at White & Case, told CNBC.

Private equity and venture capital firms, sovereign wealth funds and family offices were all looking to invest more in the sector, marking a significant change from just a few years ago, Turgel said, adding that many investors were previously reluctant or restricted from doing so.

The growth of private equity allocations to defense technology also comes amid a surge in venture capital investment in defense technology. Startups across NATO and allied countries raised $27.1 billion through September, nearly double the total for all of 2025, according to an October report from Dealroom and Resilience Media.

While venture capital traditionally backs young, high-growth startups, and private equity focuses on more established businesses, the distinction is increasingly blurring as some PE firms move into growth investments.

"Private equity firms are putting more money into defense, and they are changing how they do it," Jim Corridore, lead industrials analyst at PitchBook, told CNBC.

The number of defense PE growth deals increased from 15 in 2024 to 31 in 2025 and 48 through September 2026, according to PitchBook.

Those investments accounted for 41% of defense PE deal value in 2026, up from just 3% in 2024.

"It is clear to me that PE is moving into territory VC used to own," Corridore said.

Private equity firm Advent International announced a commitment of up to $1 billion to next-generation defense technology in March, including investments in autonomous systems developers Shield AI and Saronic.

Shonnel Malani, Advent's managing partner and global head of aerospace and defense, told CNBC the firm has already deployed roughly three-quarters of that commitment.

Malani said Advent's expansion into younger defense technology companies was a natural evolution of its existing investments in more mature defense businesses.

Advent is backing businesses it knows well and prioritizing proven technologies that address specific military needs and have been validated by customers, rather than focusing primarily on financial metrics, Malani said.

While the influx of private capital is helping accelerate innovation in defense technology, it is also fueling concerns about inflated valuations and speculative investments.

Malani said defense technology companies are increasingly valued on their technological promise rather than revenue, profit, or order books, leaving considerable room for interpretation over what they are worth.

He added that a correction may be inevitable as some companies struggle to turn technological promise into commercial success.

"I think there is a very big risk of that," he added when asked whether an investment bubble could emerge.

There is "a lot of froth in the system," Malani said, adding that Advent had walked away from several potential investments because valuations had become "silly."

PitchBook's Corridore pushed back against the notion that the sector is overheated, but he expects investment interest to keep growing.

"Budgets are growing, the military has used up a lot of ammunition, and new drone and counter-drone technology is arriving," he said. "All of that gives investors the steady revenue PE needs to make long-term bets."

Other private equity investors are sticking more closely to traditional buyout strategies.

Lionel Mestre, partner at French private equity firm Weinberg Capital Partners, told CNBC his firm still focuses on profitable defense businesses with established products, customers and positive cash flow, rather than early-stage startups.

He said valuations for the smaller, established defense suppliers Weinberg targets had not increased significantly, despite growing investor interest in the sector.

Mestre sees opportunities to finance manufacturing expansion and consolidate Europe's fragmented defense supply chain, and considers the rapidly evolving drone market too immature for Weinberg's traditional buyout strategy.

The smaller defense businesses Weinberg is targeting need investment in production capacity, employees, working capital and commercial operations to meet rising demand.

"This sector will have to consolidate anyway," Mestre said, arguing that smaller suppliers need to become larger and more competitive.

He also said European institutional investors' attitudes toward defense had shifted significantly, particularly since President Donald Trump's return to the White House and renewed pressure on European countries to increase military spending.

Malani and Mestre both see opportunities for PE firms to help defense companies scale production, providing not only capital but also industry connections and access to government customers.

Malani said some startups were rushing toward initial public offerings before developing the operational maturity needed to become sustainable public companies.

And, despite the increase in investment activity, exits remain a potential challenge.

According to PitchBook, there have been 504 defense PE deals but just 101 exits since 2021, although the comparison includes add-on acquisitions and therefore does not directly measure the proportion of investments awaiting an exit.

IPOs accounted for 70% of defense PE exit value through September 2026, leaving investors exposed to changing stock market conditions.

Carl Meyer has been reporting on environmental and climate policy for a decade and is a member of the Parliamentary press gallery in Ottawa.

On Canada Day, parts of Ottawa were hit with a one-in-200-year storm. Nearly four months later, it’s still having an impact as the polls open for Ontario’s municipal elections.

As much as 167 millimetres of rain, more than an average month’s worth, fell in a matter of hours on the southwest corner of the city on July 1. Combined with high winds, the storm closed roads and downed power lines. City staff admitted the sudden deluge overwhelmed neighbourhood drainage systems, and more than 7,000 people reported their basements had flooded. It was a big contributor to what became Ottawa’s wettest summer on record.

But it was all heavily concentrated in one part of the city — so localized, in fact, that Environment and Climate Change Canada didn’t even clock it in its system for monitoring climate-driven extreme weather, a spokesperson confirmed to The Narwhal. That system only looks at rainfall across the broader region, which didn’t record the same degree of rainfall levels that day.

The lopsided impact demonstrates how climate change doesn’t affect everyone equally.

Some residents are more exposed to climate risks, like those who live along shoreline, or in neighbourhoods that don’t have robust enough sewer systems to handle huge amounts of stormwater.

That’s a big issue for Ottawa, as both heavy rains and flooding are expected to be significant risks across the city in the future, as the climate keeps heating up from fossil fuel use.

A 2020 report commissioned by the City of Ottawa and the National Capital Commission predicted that climate change will bring “more intense precipitation.” This will increase the risk of flooding and stormwater overflows, and “municipal planning must account for climate impacts, including future flood risks,” the report noted.

“The real surprise isn’t the storm. The surprise is that we are still treating these events as unexpected rather than preparing for the climate reality we know is already here,” Community Action for Environmental Sustainability Ottawa said in a statement following the storm.

“Every flood, every heat wave and every major storm is another reminder. Climate action isn’t only about preventing a dire, far-off future, it’s about protecting our communities today.”

Many Ottawans have spent months dealing with the mess the storm caused, removing ruined furniture and mould, while also dealing with the complexities of insurance that left some people out of pocket. Meanwhile, the city has been investigating flooding impacts and studying what can be done to fix the situation.

The issue has spilled over into the Oct. 26 municipal elections. All four leading candidates in Ottawa’s mayoral race have promised to do something to protect residents from future flooding.

According to Jeff Leiper, a three-term city councillor who is running for mayor, the Canada Day storm was another example of the extreme weather, energized by climate change, that has pummelled the city in recent years. There have been multiple tornadoes, heat waves, flooding, toxic wildfire smoke and a derecho storm.

“On the whole, we have a good idea that we need to make our infrastructure considerably more resilient to more extreme storms,” he told The Narwhal in an interview. “But we are not at the point where we have funded making our infrastructure appropriately resilient.”

Leiper has announced a $50-million plan to fix sewers and other infrastructure in the three Ottawa wards hit hardest by the Canada Day storm. His plan would also increase grants to those impacted by flooding, create a new team to help residents with insurance claims and relief programs, waive permit fees for flood repairs and increase municipal staff responding to 3-1-1 calls during major storm events.

Ottawa Mayor Mark Sutcliffe, who has held the title since 2022, is running for a second term. He plans to prioritize $500 million in funding for “infrastructure that make[s] our communities stronger in the face of extreme weather,” including heavy rains which cause flooding. He said he would “expand the application of a climate lens for infrastructure investments.” A spokesperson for his campaign told The Narwhal he will make “all necessary investments” to fix infrastructure “in the areas directly impacted by the July 1 storm.”

Sutcliffe’s plan includes upgraded storm sewers and ditches, with priority “given to addressing infrastructure gaps in flood-prone communities as guided by expert recommendations.”

The campaign spokesperson said they disagreed with Leiper’s assertion that climate infrastructure hasn’t been funded. Ottawa’s 2026 budget includes $94 million in capital contributions “that apply a climate lens to projects across the city.”

Another mayoral candidate, Neil Saravanamuttoo, an economist, plans to replace sewers, prioritize infrastructure investments in existing neighbourhoods and carry out flood mitigation measures like creating stormwater ponds and planting trees. He is also promising “city-run neighbourhood retrofits,” where the city arranges the bulk of the buying and financing, and also helps residents apply for federal rebates.

“The Canada Day flooding was a wake up call for Ottawa,” Saravanamuttoo’s platform states. “Weather events that we thought were once-in-a-century events are now happening every few years, and we are seeing the significant cost that flooding can impose on Ottawa households.”

Alex Lawson, a business owner who is another contender for mayor, said he would review the city’s process for responding to disasters and produce a report that examines maintenance records for sewers, street by street.

A spokesperson for the Lawson campaign, Laura Kurkimaki, also said Lawson has experience navigating disaster response from his time on the board of West Carleton Disaster Relief, and he believes the city’s emergency response plan puts “too much pressure on volunteer organizations.”

Sutcliffe’s campaign disputes both the minister’s timeline and Leiper’s comment about the city’s response.

He then spoke with Dunlop, Ontario Premier Doug Ford and Municipal Affairs Minister Rob Flack on July 2 and 3, the spokesperson added.

#4043. A simple liquid can stop cavities without drilling, major study confirms

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

A cavity doesn't always have to mean drilling, injections, or an uncomfortable visit to the dentist. A major clinical trial has confirmed that a simple, inexpensive liquid can stop tooth decay in young children, potentially preventing painful infections and the need for dental surgery.

The treatment, called silver diamine fluoride (SDF), takes only seconds to apply to each affected tooth. Rather than removing damaged tooth material and placing a filling, a dentist uses a tiny sponge tipped applicator to paint the liquid directly onto the cavity. The process requires no drilling, injections, or sedation.

The findings could have enormous implications for childhood dental care. Every year, thousands of young children in the United States visit emergency departments because of untreated cavities. Emergency physicians generally cannot provide the dental procedures needed to address the underlying problem, leaving some children with ongoing pain and infections. Others ultimately need dental surgery under general anesthesia, which makes them completely unconscious during the procedure.

Now, researchers led by the University of Michigan have produced compelling evidence that a much simpler approach can stop many cavities from getting worse.

Major Clinical Trial Confirms Silver Diamine Fluoride Works

Published in JAMA Pediatrics, the Phase III clinical trial included 830 children younger than 6. Participants were recruited through dental clinics, pediatric medical practices, and Head Start and Early Head Start programs in Michigan, New York, and Iowa.

Head Start and Early Head Start are federally supported programs that provide early childhood education and related services to eligible families.

The results showed that a solution containing 38% silver diamine fluoride successfully stopped decay in more than half of the affected baby teeth when treatment was provided at six month intervals.

Dentists refer to this as arresting tooth decay, meaning the damaged area stops deteriorating. Unlike a traditional filling, the treatment does not replace lost tooth structure. Instead, it helps prevent existing decay from progressing.

SDF works through the combined effects of silver and fluoride. Silver helps combat bacteria associated with tooth decay, while fluoride helps strengthen the mineral structure of teeth and resist further damage.

"This is a very effective and safe treatment -- even in children as young as 1," said Margherita Fontana, professor of dentistry at the University of Michigan School of Dentistry and the study's lead investigator.

A Simple Treatment Could Help Millions Avoid Dental Pain

Tooth decay is the most common chronic disease among children, affecting more than 40% of American children. Although cavities may initially cause few noticeable symptoms, untreated decay can eventually lead to severe toothaches, infections, problems eating and sleeping, missed school days, and repeated medical appointments.

For young children, conventional dental treatment can also be particularly challenging. Some are too young to remain still throughout a procedure, while others experience significant fear or anxiety.

SDF offers an alternative for many of these patients because it can be applied quickly without the equipment and procedures required for a traditional filling.

Fontana said the benefits could extend well beyond young children. Older adults, people with developmental or physical disabilities, individuals with severe dental anxiety, and patients who have difficulty obtaining conventional dental care could also benefit.

There is, however, one important tradeoff. SDF permanently turns the decayed portion of a treated tooth dark, which can be especially noticeable on front teeth. The discoloration is primarily cosmetic, but it may influence whether patients and families choose the treatment.

For many people, avoiding pain, drilling, or more invasive procedures may outweigh that drawback.

Why This Decades-Old Treatment Could Soon Reach More Patients

Silver diamine fluoride is not a newly invented substance. Dentists have successfully used it to manage cavities in numerous countries for decades.

In the United States, the FDA approved SDF as a medical device for treating tooth sensitivity in 2014. Dentists have also used it off-label to stop cavities, meaning they apply an authorized product for a purpose beyond its original approved indication.

What has been missing is large-scale clinical evidence from U.S. populations establishing its safety and effectiveness for treating tooth decay, a key step toward obtaining FDA approval for that specific use.

The University of Michigan trial was designed to help fill that gap.

"If we want more children and families to benefit from this treatment, we need rigorous evidence showing both that it works and that it's safe. From a public health perspective, if we want broader implementation across the United States, including in medical settings, we need carefully collected data in U.S. populations, and we now have that," Fontana said.

The investigation began in 2018 and continued despite the major disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic.

The researchers believe stronger clinical evidence could make SDF more widely available, including in pediatric medical settings where children often receive care long before their first dental appointment.

"In medicine, clinicians want high-quality evidence before changing practice," Fontana said. "It is important to have data they can refer to because young children often see pediatricians years before they ever visit a dentist, broader acceptance could allow many more cavities to be treated while a referral to a dental home is successful, and before they become painful, infected or require surgery."

A dental home refers to an ongoing relationship with a dental care provider who can deliver preventive care, treatment, and follow-up services.

Expanding access to SDF could allow more children to receive early intervention before relatively manageable cavities develop into serious dental problems.

FDA Approval Could Expand Access to Cavity Treatment

The trial brought together researchers from the University of Michigan, New York University, the University of Iowa, and Indiana University. The National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research, part of the National Institutes of Health, provided more than $12 million in funding.

The product tested was Advantage Arrest 38% SDF, supplied by Elevate Oral Care.

The findings provide clinical trial evidence that the manufacturer can use in an application seeking FDA approval of SDF as a drug for arresting dental caries, the medical term for tooth decay.

Amr Moursi, a professor of pediatric dentistry at New York University College of Dentistry and a co-principal investigator, emphasized the potential importance of that regulatory change.

"Our results support FDA approval of SDF for managing arrest of tooth decay in young children. Removing SDF from off-label status would be an important innovation which could lead to increased utilization by providers, enhanced payments by insurers and more consistent product quality," said Moursi, a co-principal investigator on the study.

For some children, receiving another application of SDF every few months could be sufficient to keep a cavity under control until the affected baby tooth naturally falls out.

Adults could also benefit, either by using SDF to manage decay over a longer period or as a temporary solution until a filling or another restorative procedure becomes affordable or practical.

The treatment is not suitable for every cavity, and stopping decay does not necessarily eliminate the need for future dental work. Nevertheless, the findings suggest that a procedure requiring only seconds to perform could help many patients avoid more complicated treatment.

"For almost anyone, this can arrest the decay and stop the infection and the pain it causes," Fontana said. "This could benefit many people."
