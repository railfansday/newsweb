What constitutes a human right in prison? Not Vegemite, apparently.

An Australian convicted murderer has lost his appeal to bring Vegemite back to prison in Victoria. Andre McKechnie, 54, who is serving a life sentence for stabbing a property developer to death in Queensland in the 1990s, argued that the ban of Vegemite has denied him the right to "enjoy his culture as an Australian".

“Without being able to consume Vegemite, I feel deprived of being able to practise my Australian culture, sad and disconnected from my family and community,” he told the state’s Supreme Court.

For those who haven't yet had the (dubious?) pleasure, Vegemite is a strong-flavoured yeast-based food spread that is typically eaten on toast, biscuits, and in sandwiches. It's similar to Marmite in its malty, umami flavour, and because it pairs beautifully with hard cheeses - even if Marmite is less dense in texture compared to its Australian cousin. (Thereby making it superior.)

The polarising salty condiment was first developed in Melbourne in 1919, following the disruption of British Marmite imports after World War I. It was first sold in 1923.

Vegemite has been banned in prisons since 2006, with Corrections Victoria arguing that the strong smell can distract sniffer dogs doing drug searches.

Some prisons have ruled Vegemite as contraband over rumours that it was being used to brew alcoholic drinks. This has been debunked by many as an urban legend, as the yeast used to make the spread is not alive and therefore cannot be used in the fermenting process.

However, McKechnie's petition was still met with rejection by a Supreme Court judge and a response by authorities in Victoria, who stated that the thick paste could be used to disguise contraband substances.

Justice Harris said McKechnie failed to show that the Vegemite ban breached the state's Corrections Act, which requires prisons to provide food to maintain the health and wellbeing of inmates. She said it was "difficult to accept" McKechnie's sadness and isolation was caused by a lack of Vegemite and that the laws did "not extend to an obligation to provide to prisoners foods of cultural significance to enhance their wellbeing".

Justice Harris added there were other ways for prisoners to enjoy Australian culture, including by listening to local music, reading books written by Australian authors, and eating food that was not prohibited.

The Department of Justice and Community Safety welcomed the court decision. A spokesperson said: "Ensuring the safety of staff and prisoners is our top priority. We will continue to manage approved and prohibited items in accordance with operational and security requirements."

The Portuguese multinational energy corporation Galp issued a statement on Monday confirming that, after several months of negotiations, "it was not possible to reach an agreement" on the proposed combination of its downstream, refining and distribution activities with those of the Spanish company Moeve, whose shareholders are Mubadala Investment Company (Abu Dhabi) and the US group Carlyle.

The Portuguese oil and gas company stressed, however, that this outcome has no impact on its operations or on confidence in the company.

"Galp holds an exceptionally strong position, with one of the most attractive upstream growth profiles in the sector, robust industrial and commercial businesses and a diversified portfolio that includes renewables and significant trading activities," wrote Paula Amorim, chair of Galp’s Board of Directors.

"This business mix, combined with a solid balance sheet and strong cash-flow generation capacity, gives Galp a firm foundation to continue growing, investing and creating value," the statement added.

Maarten Wetselaar, CEO of Moeve, also wrote that the combination with Galp "represented an opportunity to accelerate value creation", but that its strategy was never dependent on that deal going ahead.

"After extensive discussions, it was not possible to reach a commercial agreement with Galp. We remain focused on executing our transformation with financial discipline and on generating sustainable long-term value," the statement concluded.

The Portuguese online newspaper ECO reported on Sunday night that the deal had fallen through because Paula Amorim had not agreed to the Spanish company’s demands, citing sources familiar with the talks.

Galp’s downstream business is underpinned by "a leading retail network" in Portugal and by "a significant portfolio of energy transition projects in Sines", the statement adds.

These include one of Europe’s largest green hydrogen production projects using electrolysis and a biofuels production unit.

The company also revealed on Monday, in its quarterly results and financial disclosure, that its net profit jumped 68% year on year in the third quarter, to €682 million.

"The increase in oil and natural gas production in Brazil to a new record, the strong performance of exports and oil and gas trading activities in international markets," the report read.

Israelis head to the polls on Tuesday in the country’s first national election since Hamas’s 7 October 2023 attack, a vote that is shaping up as a verdict on Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu.

Yet for a country that has spent three years fighting on several fronts, the campaign has been strikingly quiet on how those wars were fought.

Israel’s conduct in Gaza, which led to an international arrest warrant for Netanyahu, has barely been debated. Nor have its occupation of much of southern Lebanon and parts of Syria, or the two wars it launched against Iran. All enjoy broad support among Jewish Israelis.

The main contenders have instead argued over Netanyahu himself: his character, his responsibility for the security failures of 7 October, military exemptions for ultra-Orthodox men and his 2023 attempt to overhaul the judiciary.

There is also little to separate the two main blocs on the Palestinian question. Both are dominated by parties that back Jewish settlement in the occupied West Bank and oppose a Palestinian state, and no leading candidate is calling for a revival of the peace process.

Reminders of the war are everywhere. Stickers bearing the faces of fallen soldiers line train stations, alongside fading yellow ribbons for the hostages, the last of whom came home under a ceasefire deal last year.

According to the Israel Democracy Institute (IDI), a Jerusalem-based think tank, around 68% of Jewish Israelis say their views on 7 October are central to how they will vote. Among Arab citizens, the figure is 32%, with most naming crime as their main concern.

Netanyahu, Israel’s longest-serving prime minister, has pointed to military gains against Hamas, Hezbollah and Iran while placing blame for the 7 October failures on the security establishment.

His critics say he has dodged accountability by blocking an official state inquiry. They also accuse him of delaying a hostage deal and of failing to secure a decisive victory on any front.

The issue that has stirred the most anger is conscription. Military service is compulsory for most Jewish Israelis, but Netanyahu’s coalitions have long depended on ultra-Orthodox parties that insist yeshiva students be exempt.

Resentment has grown as reservists have been called up repeatedly and the army struggles with a manpower shortage. Around 13,000 ultra-Orthodox men turn 18 each year, but fewer than one in ten enlist.

About a quarter of Jewish voters told the IDI the issue would weigh on their choice. IDI president Yohanan Plesner said it was “probably the number one issue” for the anti-Netanyahu camp.

The IDI’s Tamar Hermann said many Israelis see military service as a “kind of permit to citizenship”.

Netanyahu’s main challenger, former army chief Gadi Eisenkot, has pledged to end the de facto exemptions. Eisenkot, a centrist, lost his son and two nephews in the fighting in Gaza.

The judicial overhaul brought hundreds of thousands onto the streets in 2023 and left Israel deeply divided when Hamas attacked. Netanyahu cast it as a check on unelected judges. Opponents saw a power grab by a leader under indictment on corruption charges, which he denies.

After 7 October, much of that protest movement turned its energy towards demanding the hostages’ release. Today, only 18% of Jewish Israelis say the overhaul will shape their vote, a sign of what Hermann described as “public fatigue”. It matters more on the left, where 22.5% cite it.

A law passed last year giving the government more say in judicial appointments will only take effect once the next Knesset is sworn in. The opposition bloc has promised to scrap it.

Israelis vote for party lists rather than individual candidates, and no party is expected to come close to the 61 seats needed for a majority in the 120-seat Knesset. The next prime minister will be whoever can assemble a governing coalition. If no one can, Netanyahu will stay on as caretaker until yet another election.

More than a quarter of Jewish voters say the party leader is a decisive factor. Netanyahu’s backers see a seasoned statesman who has stood up to US presidents and refused concessions they believe would endanger Israel. His detractors see him as corrupt.

Eisenkot’s supporters cite his decades in uniform and his personal losses. The son of Moroccan Jewish immigrants, he would become Israel’s first prime minister of non-European heritage, though he has not campaigned on it.

Living costs are a major concern but have barely featured. Research by Sarit Menahem-Carmi of Reichman University’s Aaron Institute for Economic Policy found that Israel is now more expensive than wealthy European countries such as Austria, Denmark and Sweden.

The IDI found 38% of Jewish Israelis rank the economy as their first or second priority, rising to 46% among Arab citizens. Netanyahu credits his policies with fuelling growth and a thriving tech sector, while opponents say many have been left behind.

None of the leading candidates has put the cost of the wars at the centre of their campaign. Joseph Zeira, an economics professor at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, warned that heavy military spending cannot be ignored. “The cost of the conflict is tremendous,” he said.

November can sometimes be an awkward month for travel. The days are shorter, the weather is less predictable, and much of Europe is firmly between autumn and winter.

However, that all tends to equal fewer crowds, plenty of cultural events and, by the end of the month, the first signs of Christmas appearing across European cities.

From early festive markets in Vienna to food and wine in Portugal, a major marathon in Athens and winter sun in Fuerteventura, there are still plenty of reasons to get away.

Here’s Euronews Travel’s guide for where to go, what to see and where to stay this November.

If you want to get into the Christmas spirit without waiting until December, Vienna is one of the best places to start.

The Austrian capital's Christmas markets begin opening early in the month, with both the Schönbrunn Palace and Stephansplatz markets opening on 6 November. The most famous market, Rathausplatz, follows on 13 November.

When you’ve had your fill of mulled wine and Christmas lights, November is a great time to explore Vienna's museums, grand coffee houses and palaces without the summer crowds.

Make sure to check out both the Belvedere and Hofburg palaces and head to the Kunsthistorisches Museum, which is filled with European old masters and Habsburg art treasures.

For those who would rather avoid the cold altogether, the Canary Islands remain one of Europe's safest bets for some late-autumn sunshine.

Fuerteventura is particularly appealing in November, when its beaches, volcanic landscapes and enormous dunes can be explored without the intensity of the summer heat – and it’s one of the most affordable islands in the Canaries, too.

For those who suffer from seasonal depression, the sun sets in the Canary Islands later than in most places in mainland Europe, so it’s ideal for staying cheerful as the winter sets in.

There's also a new reason to stay here. The Elba Corralejo Resort & Spa is opening on 1 November, bringing with it 478 rooms, a staggering 20 swimming pools, restaurants and extensive wellness and family facilities.

Hilton's new Outset Collection is making its European debut in Madrid with Casa Verbena Madrid.

The 45-room hotel is set on Calle de Preciados, a pedestrianised shopping street in the heart of the Spanish capital, within walking distance of Puerta del Sol, Plaza Mayor and the Royal Palace.

It is designed by Spanish interior designer Lorenzo Castillo, with boutique rooms drawing on Spanish architecture and culture.

Maastricht is getting a new four-star-plus hotel this November, with the Van der Valk Exclusief Hotel Maastricht-Maas celebrating its grand opening on the banks of the River Maas.

The 113-room property has a contemporary design with South American influences, alongside a restaurant, bar, meeting spaces and a fifth-floor panoramic wellness area.

It is also within easy reach of Maastricht's historic centre, making it a useful base for exploring the buzzy Dutch city.

Germany’s capital gets particularly cerebral in early November, when Berlin Science Week takes place from 1-10 November.

This year's theme is “In Touch”, exploring how science affects everyday life through exhibitions, workshops, talks, performances and events across the city.

A public weekend from 6-8 November is one of the highlights, making this a good option for anyone who wants something a little different from the usual museum-and-gallery city break.

November is a particularly good month to visit Athens if you like your city breaks with a bit of sporting drama.

The 43rd Athens Marathon, the Authentic, takes place on 7 and 8 November, following the historic route from Marathon to the Panathenaic Stadium.

Even if you're not running, it's a chance to see one of the world's most famous marathon routes and enjoy Athens at a time when summer's heat has disappeared.

The Alentejo region of Portugal is set to gain tonnes of popularity next year, thanks to its appearance in countless travel experts’ trend predictions.

To experience it for yourself before it blows up as a destination, why not visit the International Hot Air Balloon Festival taking place there from 7-12 November.

The festival, which has been running since 1997, will feature balloon flights, public displays and opportunities to see the colourful aircraft filling the skies over the Alentejo landscape.

While you’re there, you could head to Borba, which will host its annual Vine and Wine Festival from 11-15 November, bringing together wine tastings, regional food, entertainment, crafts and local producers.

November is also the month when Christmas travel starts becoming a much more immediate prospect.

If you want to visit some of Europe's biggest markets without the crowds of December, the final week of November is worth considering. Vienna starts early, while cities including Strasbourg, Nuremberg and Prague move into full festive mode later in the month.

It's also worth booking winter-sun trips now, with the Canary Islands and Madeira remaining as popular choices as temperatures fall across northern Europe.

Travelling over Christmas and New Year is notoriously tricky and sometimes pricey, so it’s best to book ahead to secure the best possible deals.

Finally, if you're thinking about skiing, November is the time to start looking ahead. A number of new Alpine hotels and lodges are opening for the 2026-2027 season, including The First Cortina in Italy, The Ski Lodge in Verbier and new properties in Morzine and Les Menuires.

Oil prices tumbled on Monday morning as diplomatic efforts to calm the Iran war gathered pace and US President Donald Trump said he was weighing a deal with Tehran.

Brent, the global oil benchmark, fell more than 6% from about $117.4 per barrel at Friday’s close to around $110 at the time of writing, while US benchmark West Texas Intermediate dropped about 2% to $101.5 per barrel.

Friday's close was Brent’s highest since 19 March and only about 2% below the $119.5 peak hit in early March, at the start of the Iran war. Before the conflict, Brent traded at around $72 per barrel.

Prices had surged last week after Yemen's Houthi rebels, an Iran-aligned armed group, attacked airports and energy facilities in Saudi Arabia, the world’s largest oil exporter.

However, the mood shifted over the weekend.

Qatari mediators reportedly travelled to Tehran on Sunday in coordination with Washington, while Saudi Arabia's defence minister, Pakistan's army chief and Turkey's intelligence chief were due in the Iranian capital on Monday for regional security talks.

In an interview with Fox News on Sunday, Trump framed it as a choice asking, "do I make a deal with them, or do I finish them off?"

Supply risks have not gone away as the Houthis said they struck Saudi airports and energy sites again over the weekend, while Riyadh reported shooting down further missiles and drones.

At a campaign event in Port Huron, Michigan, ahead of the US midterm elections, Trump defended his administration's reasons for starting the conflict.

Citing the Wall Street Journal, he also claimed that "oil coming out of the Strait of Hormuz is now topping what it was before the war [...] our navy has been incredible."

The Strait of Hormuz carried about a fifth of the world’s oil before the war.

Gulf crude exports, excluding Iran, returned to pre-war levels in September, according to ship-tracking firm Kpler, but around 40% of them now bypass the strait through pipelines, compared with 17% before the conflict.

#4078. A Bizarre 170-Million-Year-Old Predator Is Rewriting What We Know About Ancient Crocodiles

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

A Moroccan crocodile relative’s teeth resemble those of prehistoric filter feeders, suggesting that different reptile groups independently evolved similar tools for catching tiny aquatic prey.

A crocodile relative that lived around 170 million years ago had a long, narrow snout lined with exceptionally thin, needle-like teeth. No known close relative has teeth quite like them. Their delicacy suggests a diet of very small fish, crustaceans such as fairy shrimp, and other small aquatic creatures rather than large prey.

Scientists at the University of Portsmouth and Hassan II University of Casablanca, Morocco, identified the new species, named Ctenosuchus tenuidens. It belonged to the thalattosuchians, a group of marine and freshwater reptiles that lived during the age of the dinosaurs. Its fossils came from rocks formed in ancient rivers and lakes in what is now the Atlas Mountains of southeastern Morocco.

The researchers examined the fossil teeth under powerful microscopes and studied their internal structure, finding specialized ridges and a closely spaced arrangement. For lead author Hamzah Imran, from the University of Portsmouth’s School of the Environment and Life Sciences, the teeth’s fragility offered a clue to what the animal could safely catch.

He said: “The teeth are so fine that we don’t think it was catching normal prey. If it caught a big fish, and the fish wriggled, those delicate teeth would get smashed. We think the animal was probably eating soft prey like shrimp, worms and tiny fish, so nothing that would break its teeth.

“These feeding habits make this particular species very different to other long-snouted crocodiles, so it really is quite an exciting discovery!”

Recognizing the teeth in the first place required a microscope and careful work with a scalpel. Imran recalled: “We noticed these little black circles on either side of the long bone in the middle of one of the fossils, so we put it under a microscope.

“We carefully scratched around one of the black circles with a scalpel, and as we uncovered more of it, it became longer and longer. That’s when we realized these little black circles were actually incredibly thin teeth.

“These comb-like teeth inspired the name Ctenosuchus tenuidens as ‘cteno’ means ‘comb,’ ‘suchus’ means ‘crocodile,’ and ‘tenuidens’ means ‘thin teeth.’”

Although unusual among its close relatives, Ctenosuchus had teeth resembling those of other prehistoric animals. Similar teeth occur in ancient aquatic reptiles called mesosaurs and in certain long-snouted pterosaurs that lived millions of years later. Its jaws and teeth share features with those of the Jurassic pterosaurs Gnathosaurus and Ctenochasma, which scientists believe were filter feeders, using their teeth to separate small food items from water. These similarities indicate that different groups independently evolved comparable feeding adaptations.

The Moroccan fossils broaden scientists’ understanding of the habitats and feeding roles occupied by Jurassic crocodile relatives in Gondwana, the southern supercontinent that included modern-day Africa. They also add to the picture of the varied prehistoric ecosystems that once existed in Morocco.

Imran added: “It just shows there is much more out there that needs to be explored. Morocco has an incredibly rich fossil history, but there is still so much waiting to be discovered.”

Smith, Samir Zouhri, James Gunn and Nizar Ibrahim, 8 October 2026, Journal of Vertebrate Paleontology. DOI: 10.1080/02724634.2026.2728670

#3973. ‘Ticking time bomb’: The world's pension funds face an ageing crisis

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

The world's pension systems are confronting a demographic crisis as longer lifespans and dwindling birth rates threaten to stretch retirement finances, raising questions about their ability to sustain payouts for future generations.

Pension offerings generally fall into two categories: defined-benefit plans, which promise retirees a predetermined income, and defined-contribution plans, where payouts depend on accumulated savings and investment returns.

Ageing poses different challenges for the two systems: defined-benefit schemes face mounting liabilities as retirees live longer, while defined-contribution members risk outliving their savings, placing greater pressure on funds' investment performance.

The problem is "huge, and it's gotten more pronounced over time,” said Sebastien Betermier, executive director at the International Centre for Pension Management, noting that some pension funds have members who have been retired for nearly half a century, aged aged 107 or 108.

"The higher the longevity, the more years the pension funds have to cover, and so that immediately comes in as an additional liability," he said. "Think about the cost that it imposes actually on the next generation … it is a ticking time bomb," Betermier told CNBC.

The scale of the challenge is laid bare in the 2026 Marsh CFA Institute Global Pension Index, or MCGPI, report published Tuesday last week. Of the 55 pension systems covered by the index, 43 have fertility rates below the replacement level of 2.1 children per woman, while 23 have rates below 1.5.

Meanwhile, life expectancy at age 65 in more developed regions has increased by 50.9% since 1950 to about 20 additional years, meaning retirement benefits must now last considerably longer. The number of potential workers supporting older people is also set to decline sharply.

“The funding of future pensions and aged care both become more critical as the ratio of workers to retirees continues to decline,” the MCGPI report stated. “Very few systems, as they currently operate, are secure.”

The problem is particularly acute for pay-as-you-go pension systems in which contributions from today's workers largely fund benefits for current retirees, leaving them especially vulnerable to a shrinking workforce.

The pressures are already evident in the U.S., where the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance Trust Fund, which finances retirement and survivor benefits for tens of millions of Americans, is projected to exhaust its reserves in the fourth quarter of 2032, according to its latest trustees' report.

Fully funded pension schemes, which invest contributions from workers and employers to finance future retirement benefits are not immune either.

As their members retire and begin drawing down benefits, some funds could find themselves paying out more than they receive in fresh contributions, forcing them to rethink their investment strategies and liquidity needs, MCGPI cautioned.

“Future benefit outflows will exceed the level of contributions or premiums ... this financial strain will inevitably grow in the future,” the report highlighted.

The demographic crunch is reshaping how pension funds invest, with some taking on greater risks to generate the returns needed to meet growing retirement obligations.

Many mature funds are already paying out more in benefits than they receive in contributions, forcing them to rely increasingly on investment returns and accumulated assets.

In Australia, about a third of superannuation funds recorded negative natural cash flows from assets over a three-year period, meaning benefit payments exceeded contributions, according to a 2025 report by the Australian Prudential Regulation Authority.

Industry watchers say the pressure to generate higher returns has pushed some funds toward riskier and more complex investments, including private equity, infrastructure and private credit.

"You can try to earn higher returns on your investments, but that's going to require taking on greater levels of risk," said Betermier.

Jean-Pierre Aubry, associate director of state and local research at the Center for Retirement Research at Boston College, said pension funds were increasingly shifting toward riskier investments, although the extent varied considerably across markets.

"I think everybody is shifting a little more risk where they can. The costs are going up with this demographic shift," Aubry said.

He pointed to U.S. state and local government pension funds, which hold only about 20% of their assets in bonds, with much of the remainder invested in equities and private assets.

Such allocations make U.S. public pension funds considerably more exposed to investment risk than their counterparts in Europe and Canada, he said.

Not every pension manager sees ageing as a reason to take bigger bets.

David Elia, CEO of Australian pension fund Hostplus said demographic changes were only one of many factors shaping investment decisions.

He stressed the importance of maintaining a diversified portfolio across asset classes, geographies and industries, while ensuring that potential returns adequately compensate for the risks involved.

Elia also said that governments could find ways to mitigate the pressures of ageing populations, including through immigration policies and technological advances, while demographic shifts themselves could create new investment opportunities.

"I don't underestimate governments being incredibly creative in terms of dealing with these types of issues," he said. "You know, but they're going to have to address it, no doubt about that."

#4083. Medieval Children Were Exposed to Shocking Levels of Lead, 1,000 Years Before the Industrial Revolution

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Children in medieval English mining communities faced lead exposure matching or exceeding that in Industrial Revolution cities, with social standing apparently shaping who bore the greatest burden.

Between the 16th and 19th centuries, lead producers in the Derbyshire Peak District of central England repeatedly remelted waste left by Roman and medieval workings. Reusing that material erased many of the industrial traces archaeologists would normally examine to establish when mining and smelting resumed after Roman Britain. A record survived, however, in the teeth of the people who lived there.

Lead preserved in tooth enamel has now provided the first definitive evidence that production was established by the middle of the seventh century AD. It also reveals the extent of childhood exposure. Even in the seventh century, lead concentrations in the mining communities matched or exceeded those found in people from industrial cities around 1750 to 1850.

Mining and smelting exposed communities to lead through contaminated dust, fumes, and materials. Tooth enamel forms during childhood and does not remodel later in life, preserving a record of the lead absorbed while a person was growing up. Measuring the metal locked within enamel allowed researchers to reconstruct childhood exposure from about 650 to 1500 and compare its extent across individuals and communities.

The research was led by the University of Nottingham in collaboration with the British Geological Survey, the University of Sheffield, York Archaeology, and Castleton Historical Society. Combining archaeology, history, chemistry, and environmental science, it is the first study to analyze lead contamination in people who mined and smelted the metal in western Europe’s largest medieval lead-producing region.

The samples came from three cemetery populations spanning the industry’s revival and expansion. Wigber Low, in the Derbyshire White Peak, dates to the seventh century. At Castleton, Joule Yard dates from the seventh to 11th centuries, while Spital Field dates from the 12th to 15th centuries. The research builds on decades of archaeological investigation, including earlier work by Colin Merrony at the University of Sheffield, who identified and excavated key medieval burial sites in the region.

To place the measurements in context, the researchers compared these communities with populations elsewhere in Britain, ranging from the Neolithic period through the Industrial Revolution. This comparison revealed how high childhood exposure already was during the early medieval period, long before the industry reached its greatest scale.

Lead author Christopher Loveluck, Professor of Medieval European Archaeology at the University of Nottingham’s Department of Classics and Archaeology, said, “The team is delighted to have finally confirmed definitively the date by which the early medieval lead industry was established in the Peak District, by the mid-7th century AD, through evidence provided by the people themselves, providing the foundations of western Europe’s largest lead-producing region between the late 12th and 16th centuries.”

By the middle of the eighth century, large-scale production had contaminated the wider environment around mining centers such as Castleton. Childhood lead exposure remained high throughout the medieval period, reaching its greatest levels in the late 12th and early 13th centuries. Historical records place the medieval peak of Peak District lead production in the same period.

That timing also connects the people’s teeth with evidence collected far beyond their communities. Previous studies of Alpine ice cores and sediment cores from river channels, lakes, and peat bogs across western Europe identified a rise in atmospheric lead pollution around 1200, attributed especially to British production. The enamel findings provide a direct link between those environmental records and the people living and working in one of Europe’s major lead-producing landscapes.

Within the mining communities, exposure varied with apparent social standing. People buried with high-status grave goods, including weapons, generally had much lower lead concentrations than those whose burials were less richly furnished. The pattern suggests that elites may have controlled or benefited from lead production while lower-status households worked more directly with ore processing and smelting. Children growing up in those households would consequently have faced greater exposure.

Isotopic analysis indicated that most of the individuals studied had grown up locally. This suggests that mining and smelting knowledge passed through generations within relatively stable communities, rather than the industry depending on large numbers of incoming workers.

Merrony said, “Our recent excavations in Castleton have provided a lot of new information about the people living in the Hope Valley during the medieval period. We now understand more about the size of the population in the valley, and we can see from the archaeological evidence that these working communities were involved in industries that required hard physical labor. However, this study has enabled us, for the first time, to see beyond this broader understanding and to look more deeply into the profound impact lead production had on individuals and the communities they lived in.”

Lead can damage the nervous system, kidneys, and reproductive system, making the childhood exposures a concern even for people who survived for many years afterward. Three women analyzed in the study had elevated childhood lead exposure and died between approximately 17 and 25 years of age. The relationship between childhood exposure and premature death is complex, so these findings suggest a possible association with increased health risks without establishing that lead caused their deaths. Pregnancy may have been a particularly vulnerable time because lead stored in the body can be released back into the bloodstream.

Dr. Anna Bloxam, co-director of the Castleton excavations, said, “We know from modern studies that lead can have profoundly harmful effects on the health and development of children: there is no safe level of exposure. All the individuals included in our study survived into adulthood, but they were exposed to lead while they were young and growing. We can tell from their skeletons that most of the men we studied lived until middle or old age, whereas the women primarily died much earlier, in young adulthood.

“Our results indicate that in these mining communities, girls and boys alike were raised in dangerously polluted home environments. This exposure will likely have caused negative health effects in life, and perhaps contributed to higher rates of premature death among women of childbearing age.”

The findings concern medieval mining and smelting between the seventh and 15th centuries and do not describe modern environmental conditions. The legacy of historic mining in the Peak District is now well understood and carefully monitored, with land management, conservation, and environmental regulation helping to minimize risks associated with former mining activity.

The results will contribute to the developing medieval archaeological research agenda for the Peak District National Park. A temporary display in the region during fall and winter 2026–27 will present the research alongside archaeological discoveries from the Castleton excavations.

Reference: “Social dynamics of toxic metal industry in medieval Europe: Peak District lead production, AD 600–1500” by Christopher P. Loveluck, Hannah J. O’Regan, Anna Bloxam, Catrin Fear, Jane Evans, Simon Chenery, Carolyn Chenery, Colin Merrony, Fiona E. Moore and Angela Darlington, 13 October 2026, Antiquity. DOI: 10.15184/aqy.2026.10416

The research was funded and supported by the University of Nottingham, the Peak District National Park Authority, Castleton Historical Society, the University of Sheffield, Sheffield Museums and Historic England.

#4087. Eating 30% Less Food Has an Unexpected Effect on DNA, Study Finds

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Eating fewer calories may do more than slow certain aspects of aging. Scientists have found that mice consuming 30 percent less food accumulated fewer DNA mutations across several tissues, suggesting that diet can influence how genetic damage builds up over time.

The study, published in Cell, examined mutations across a large portion of the genome rather than focusing on individual genes. The results showed that calorie restriction affected different organs and regions of DNA in surprisingly different ways.

DNA mutations develop throughout life as cells experience genetic damage or make mistakes while copying and repairing their DNA. Most mutations are harmless, but some interfere with normal gene function and can contribute to cancer and other diseases. Although animal studies have established that calorie restriction can extend lifespan and slow certain effects of aging, its broader influence on mutation accumulation has remained unclear.

To investigate, researchers led by NYU Langone Health and University Hospitals Cleveland worked with scientists at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center and the National Institutes of Health. Using highly accurate DNA sequencing technology, they examined genetic changes in mice fed 30 percent fewer calories than animals allowed to eat freely.

The restricted diet reduced several types of mutations, including substitutions, which occur when one DNA letter is replaced by another, and insertions and deletions, which add or remove DNA letters. However, the reductions differed across tissues, with liver cells showing a stronger response than kidney or brain cells.

“While links between diet and lifespan have been established in animals, our results show that diet and mutations across the genome are also linked,” said co-corresponding study author Gilad D. Evrony, MD, PhD, a researcher at NYU Langone Health and associate professor at NYU Grossman School of Medicine. “Mice on a calorie-restricted diet are healthier for longer, but this diet is too challenging for humans. Nevertheless, considering the role mutations play in cancer and many other diseases, it’s exciting to have found a way to decrease mutations, which we can now work to understand.”

The researchers also discovered that calorie restriction did not affect all regions of DNA equally. In liver and kidney cells, the greatest reductions in mutations occurred in the least active portions of the genome. These regions contain either no genes or genes that are not being used by the cells.

One possible explanation involves differences in DNA repair activity. Regions containing actively used genes already undergo frequent DNA repair, which may limit how much additional protection they receive if calorie restriction reduces DNA damage. Less active regions, where repair occurs less frequently, could experience a larger reduction in accumulated mutations. The researchers have not yet established whether this mechanism explains the pattern.

“We didn’t expect to find this asymmetry across the genome,” said co-corresponding author Jonathan Shoag, MD, an associate professor in the Department of Urology at University Hospitals Cleveland and Case Western Reserve University School of Medicine. “By profiling more dietary interventions and mutations in additional tissues and cell types, we hope to learn what drives the connection between diet and mutations.”

The findings establish a connection between calorie restriction and lower mutation levels in mice, but they do not demonstrate that people would experience the same benefits. Sustained, substantial calorie restriction is also difficult to maintain and can carry health risks.

Researchers now hope to identify the biological processes responsible for the reduction in mutations. Understanding those processes could help scientists investigate approaches that protect DNA without requiring people to follow severely restricted diets.

“Understanding these mechanisms could eventually reveal new ways to reduce mutations – without the challenges and risks of an extreme diet—to counter diseases of aging,” said first study author Marta Grońska-Pęski, PhD, a postdoctoral fellow in Evrony’s laboratory.

The research relied on a specially established group of mice used to study the biological effects of calorie restriction. “We were fortunate to have collaborated with Dr. Victoria Acosta-Rodríguez, Dr. Joseph Takahashi, and Dr. Carla Green, who established this mouse caloric restriction cohort,” said Dr. Evrony.
