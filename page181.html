As SpaceX throttles Ukraine’s Starshield, experts warn that Elon Musk’s de facto monopoly over launch and satellite tech poses a major threat to global security.

Posts from this author will be added to your daily email digest and your homepage feed.

Last week, Starshield — the version of SpaceX’s Starlink used by the Ukrainian military — was throttled to block terminals traveling above certain speeds from accessing the service. The Ukrainian army’s interceptors, which counter Russian drones carrying bombs, suddenly stopped working at high speeds, making it easier for Russia to attack the country’s energy infrastructure and leading to a reported two deaths and at least 17 injuries.

The Ukrainian military reported receiving no advance warning about the Starshield speed restriction. But Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said it was a direct message from the US amid rising tensions between himself and President Donald Trump. The abrupt nature of the cutoff and its potentially dangerous military and humanitarian ramifications led to widespread public outcry focused both on Trump for pulling the rug out from under Ukraine and on SpaceX CEO Elon Musk for enabling him.

Since then Musk and Trump have traded denials back and forth. Musk first said that SpaceX has no control over Starshield and decisions are made by the US military, while the White House denied taking action. Then reports emerged that Musk was the one who suggested the slowdown tactic to Trump, who then accepted it, followed by a further report that Musk himself unilaterally ordered the restrictions.

The finger-pointing is of little interest to Ukrainians, who are focused on trying to defend their country from a brutal ongoing Russian invasion. Zelenskyy is well aware that he is being used as a scapegoat as Trump flails around attempting to blame Ukraine for the rising oil prices caused by his unpopular war with Iran.

But Ukraine needs to play nice to some extent, given how dependent it is on US technologies like Starlink/Starshield that enable it to mount a defense against Russian attacks. Starlink access has been deliberately restricted there before, including in 2022, when coverage was not extended to cover a counterattack operation in Crimea. If Musk decided to pull the plug completely, the effect on Ukraine could be devastating.

This amount of geopolitical power being wielded by an unelected tech oligarch is the direct result of consolidating so much of the space industry into just one company, and one man’s hands. And the scope of Musk’s control goes well beyond Ukraine. From the defense of nations to the ability of NASA to launch its astronauts into space, SpaceX has been irreplaceable — and that means Musk has the space industry in his grip.

SpaceX’s recent IPO positions it as one of the most valuable companies in the world. And while the company has generated most of its recent revenue from its Starlink service, it made its name as a company that launches rockets into space.

SpaceX’s Falcon 9 is the most successful rocket in history, and its “rideshare” program, which lets small satellite operators share space on a rocket, has dropped the price of launches to historic lows. This has helped organizations from small startups to university research departments launch their own satellites and has been hailed as a democratizing force in the space industry.

However, that success has come at a cost, which is that SpaceX now holds all the cards when it comes to access to space.

“For all intents and purposes, SpaceX has a monopoly on launch right now,” said Wendy Whitman Cobb of the School of Advanced Air and Space Studies at Maxwell Air Force Base, Alabama. “There are competitors out there,” she said, such as United Launch Alliance, Blue Origin, and Rocket Lab, “but nobody comes even close to SpaceX in terms of frequency, mass, scale, cost. I mean, they’ve got everything in the bag.”

Space economist Oğuz Karasu of the Oxford Space Initiative concurred. SpaceX doesn’t have a monopoly on launch in the legal sense, he said, but in practical terms it does. And that gives the company tremendous pull over the industry as a whole.

“The rocket launch industry is around 8 percent of the entire [space] industry,” Karasu said, “but without that 8 percent, you can’t have the rest of it.”

SpaceX, which didn’t respond to a request for comment, has a tremendous advantage in providing space-based services like satellite internet. The company has been able to build out its network of over 10,000 Starlink satellites currently in low Earth orbit by launching them cheaply and efficiently using its Falcon 9 rockets.

“This is almost like an antitrust issue,” said defense expert Andrew Reddie of the University of California, Berkeley. “The issue is that SpaceX launches support Starlink constellations that are owned by SpaceX. There is no incentive for a SpaceX launch to be used to provide constellations for other countries that would compete.”

Interestingly, SpaceX has previously launched Amazon Kuiper (now Leo) satellites on a handful of missions — arguably as a preemptive defense against antitrust complaints. Or perhaps SpaceX is simply so confident in its position that it can afford to be magnanimous (or to flex a little on its rival) by launching a few competitor satellites.

The downside of a monopoly, at least for the customer, is that eventually prices rise.

SpaceX doesn’t share prices for its launches publicly, but both Whitman Cobb and Karasu say they’ve heard complaints that Falcon 9 launches are getting more expensive, particularly for low Earth orbit deployments. And the Starlink service has seen considerable increases in price since its beta launch in 2020.

Of course, there are plenty of reasons for a company to increase its prices, from inflation to rising costs, but when a company has enormous market dominance (and now shareholders to please) there isn’t much incentive against raising prices to increase profits.

If this business model sounds familiar, it’s because it’s one of Silicon Valley’s favorite and most destructive tactics. Uber perfected the art of predatory pricing, whereby investor cash is used to artificially lower prices so that a company can corner a market and push out competition. Then, with the monopoly safely in place, the company can jack up its prices and lower the quality of its product or service, secure in the knowledge that customers no longer have any other viable alternatives.

This might sound like an obscure problem, something that might make life more difficult for a few high-tech operations but has little relevance to the day-to-day lives of most people. But space infrastructure is now embedded in the mechanisms of our most essential technologies, in the form of satellite communications and GPS data.

“If GPS goes down, you’re not going to be able to get gas at the gas pump, you’re not going to be able to get money out of the ATM,” said Whitman Cobb, “let alone not be able to do financial transactions or get an ambulance to the right place on time.”

It’s not that space experts are realistically worried that Elon Musk is going to lose his mind and cut off Starlink access worldwide, or cause a collision that could take down GPS. It’s that the space industry is woven, unseen, into so many parts of our everyday lives that allowing one company or one person to control access to space hands them enormous power.

“All of our current economic activities are based on space technologies right now,” said Karasu. “Giving this much control, or this much power, to one person is not good for society’s future.”

Even huge agencies like NASA and the Space Force face risks from being too dependent on SpaceX. With the company deciding to retire its workhorse and extremely reliable Falcon 9 rocket, reportedly not taking any new missions from 2028, the Space Force is struggling to find launch options for its military satellites, and NASA now faces a potential future in which it doesn’t have access to the Crew Dragon capsule for carrying its astronauts to and from the International Space Station.

History has shown the dangers of having only one launch option. After the Space Shuttle Columbia disaster in 2003, which cost the lives of seven astronauts, the entire program was canceled and no humans were launched into space from the US from 2011 until 2020.

That’s why NASA is continuing to support beleaguered Boeing in its slow and troubled development of the Starliner capsule, because dependence on a single launch provider means putting all of your very expensive and delicate eggs into one precarious basket. That was demonstrated in February this year, when issues with the usually rock-solid reliability of the Falcon 9 temporarily grounded it, leading to concerns that NASA’s launch of the Crew-12 mission to the space station could be delayed.

This was a timely reminder that even the most impressive and reliable of technologies — and, to be clear, the Falcon 9 is both an incredible feat of engineering and outstandingly successful — can have failures or outages. NASA continues to contract with smaller companies like Rocket Lab and Blue Origin, and has repeatedly stressed its desire to have multiple commercial providers for services like transporting astronauts to the space station.

“Even NASA is realizing, okay, we are putting all our bets on a single player,” said space business expert Michele Scataglini of the Oxford Space Initiative and University of Oxford. “The market needs to have more competition, otherwise it’s not a market.”

Whether any of these companies will be able to catch up and provide a meaningful, affordable alternative to SpaceX remains to be seen. It will be hard for any company to keep pace with SpaceX’s sister companies developing complementary technologies as well.

“The complementarity of the assets that SpaceX has built — artificial intelligence, the batteries, the launch capabilities — these are all self-reinforcing capabilities for the firm, that I would say none of the other players alone possesses,” said Scataglini.

For now, with Starlink access restored in Ukraine, the country will continue to walk the tightrope of trying to defend itself using technology it does not ultimately control. Unlike previous restrictions of Starlink usage there, which were arguably more about limiting offensive capabilities, “what seems to have been impacted in this most recent episode is Ukrainian defensive capability,” said Reddie, the defense expert. “And then you end up with all the harrowing images of apartment blocks on fire and people getting pulled out of the buildings.

“And so that’s the major security challenge, that you’ve effectively got a non-state actor making decisions as if they’re a government.”

Scataglini concurred, describing the events in Ukraine as a global wake-up call. “There’s a single entrepreneur that has more power than governments in deciding how strategic defense capabilities can be used,” he said.

In times of stress and uncertainty — and between ever-climbing costs of living, a decimated social safety net, fears about AI coming for our jobs, and rising global populism and extremism, there’s plenty to be stressed and uncertain about — it is tempting to turn our gazes inward, to look out for our own interests and prioritize the needs of our own nations.

But space does not respect national boundaries, and what happens in orbit around our planet affects us all. The risk, Scataglini said, is that “we are moving into one firm dominating the launch market and deciding who goes up, and who doesn’t. Which country has access to space, and which one does not, because not all nations around the world, especially if you think about the Global South, will have easy access to these capabilities.”

Looking to the future, experts hoped that out of the brutal events in Ukraine could come more public awareness of the dangers of too much power held in too few hands.

“I’m cautiously optimistic that, at least in this case in particular, it is highlighting the dangers of being reliant on a single company, a single individual,” said Whitman Cobb. “It took something bad happening — people dying — for people to realize, oh, wait, maybe there’s a problem here.”

#57. Alberta welcomed migrant workers. Now, it wants to restrict their rights

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

‘Scapegoating non-permanent residents is not going to lower the cost of living,’ Vanesa Ortiz says

When Vanesa Ortiz came to Calgary from Mexico, she found herself in a city gripped by winter. Outside the airport, it was -15C (5F).

“I would come to understand that this was not an extreme cold,” she said. “But in the moment it was terrifying.”

But she also found in those first moments an immense warmth. She was welcomed by elderly volunteers in white cowboy hats, and Ortiz soon felt that Calgary was a place she could one day raise a family. And yet when the teacher reflects on that moment a decade ago, she worries that the opportunities she had to put down roots could be soon closed to others.

This week, Alberta held a referendum, asking residents to weigh in on the province’s relationship with Canada – and the extent to which the province should restrict some newcomers’ access to healthcare, education and social support. It also asked about the need for constitutional reforms.

While unofficial results show both the separation and constitutional questions will fail, the ones on immigration are likely to succeed.

“I love Calgary and I love Alberta,” said Ortiz. “But when I hear people asking whether people should get access to these services – I mean, that was my daughter’s education. That was my family’s healthcare.”

The referendum amplified the grievances of fringe separatists, but it also exposed a central tension in Alberta’s approach to immigration. Even as the oil-rich province seeks newcomers to fill shortages in the job market, the provincial government wants to limit services available to some of those same workers.

The governing United Conservative party blames the rapid population growth for overcrowded and underfunded hospitals and schools. But critics say the proposed solution – restricting access to healthcare, education and social supports – could leave workers and their families more vulnerable without fully addressing the underlying causes of the crisis.

The referendum asked if the government should introduce a law ensuring only Canadian citizens, permanent residents and “individuals with an Alberta-approved immigration status” can access healthcare and education. Another asked if newcomers should have lived in Alberta for 12 months before qualifying for certain social support programmes and allowing the province to charge some temporary residents fees for healthcare and education.

Earlier in the year, the Alberta premier, Danielle Smith, blamed the immigration policy of Justin Trudeau’s Liberal government for the resource strains in classrooms, emergency rooms and social support systems. And in a social media post, Smith said: “For every $1 of taxes a guest worker generates, taxpayers are covering $10 in services. This is not sustainable.”

Canada’s federal government has since trimmed immigration targets and placed restrictions on its temporary foreign worker program amid criticisms it was being abused by large companies. But the western province’s economy relies on workers from abroad to fill jobs in sectors, like agriculture, food services and tourism, where employers struggle to recruit or retain enough staff.

Ortiz, who works with the non-profit Asociación de Mexicanos en Calgary, worries mounting skepticism towards migrant workers will push workers into the shadows and make them more vulnerable to labor exploitation.

“I can’t imagine the scale of the harm that a policy like this might create,” she said, noting that thousands of farm workers in the province don’t receive healthcare.

Ortiz has seen views shift on immigration in recent years and says the results of the referendum reflect a population that is “squeezed” economically.

“Scapegoating non-permanent residents is not going to lower the cost of living. That’s not going to fix our classrooms. They keep essential services open. In many cases, they are frontline workers. And now they’re being treated as disposable.”

For some residents, the proposals represent a sharp reversal from Alberta’s earlier efforts to attract newcomers. The UCP government invested in recruitment and expanded its provincial immigration nomination targets to address labour shortages. But as a growing number of Albertans sour on immigration, the government position has shifted.

Ximena González, a Calgary-based writer, said when the provincial government began its campaign to bring in more people, it “wanted more workers to exploit”, noting that most workers come from abroad on temporary permits.

“Now, the message feels more out in the open: ‘We wanted you to come, to exploit you. And now, we don’t need you any more – so we will punish you.”

The provincial government has claimed that temporary residents, who make up almost 6% of Alberta’s total population, contribute roughly $150m in tax revenues but cost the province more than $1bn annually in health, education and other social services.

But González argues that there is no data to back that claim.

Economist Gillian Petit notes that temporary residents also remit taxes to the federal government, which helps fund Alberta’s healthcare, meaning those residents probably contribute far more than the province acknowledges. If they contribute less in total tax dollars than other Albertans, it is because they earn less than the median resident. They are also less likely to use healthcare and education resources than the average Albertan.

“In all honesty, if I were a temporary resident and had to choose whether or not to stay, I wouldn’t,” said González, who came to Canada 16 years ago as an international student. “I would not feel wanted, and I don’t want to be treated like a third-class citizen.”

She says that media fixation on Alberta’s fringe independence movement meant the other questions in the referendum received little scrutiny and left voters uninformed.

“The media failed to help people to make an educated vote – instead of just voting based on racism.”

Economist Jim Stanford said hundreds of thousands of residents skipped over those questions on immigration, choosing to only answer the one on separation.

“This cannot remotely be interpreted as a mandate for the government to crack down on immigration,” he said, adding the province didn’t have the power to “unilaterally” act on these proposals. “This was a symbolic ideological exercise and gives a cast of legitimacy to some of these very rightwing nativist sentiments that are bubbling in the far right of Alberta politics.”

During a series of town halls over the years led by the government, more extreme views on immigration were clear. One resident received cheers after suggesting a policy of mass deportations and another said immigrants “harm” children and suggested she might seek asylum in the US.

For Ortiz, the government’s about-face reflects a “betrayal” of the workers who have helped build the province.

“We’re asking people if we should remove the right to access healthcare and education from taxpayers who are, quite literally, building our communities,” she said. “But I also worry that what gets lost in this debate is these people. They are not just labour. They work, study and raise their children here. They need to know they deserve the chance to build a life with dignity and with respect to feel safe and to feel welcome.”

#56. Russia, Ukraine agree to US-brokered, 1 city ceasefire

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

The Ukrainian city of Oleshky, occupied by Russia and on the frontline, faces a humanitarian crisis. The localized truce comes as Ukrainian President Zelenskyy said negotiators would meet for wider talks next month.

US first lady Melania Trump helped broker a ceasefire between Ukraine and Russia in the city of Oleshky, the White House announced Friday. The localized truce, according to the White House, is slated to begin on October 29.

The Ukrainian city occupied by Russia on the frontline of the war faces a humanitarian crisis and only around 2,000 people still live there. Prior to the war, the city had a population of around 24,000.

Oleshky has been under blockade since 2025 and was first occupied when Russia launched its full-scale invasion in February 2022.

The city is located in southern Ukraine, just 9 kilometers (around 5.6 miles) from the major city of Kherson.

"Oleshky's remaining population is comprised primarily of elderly residents who urgently need medical attention, medicine, food and clean water," Melania Trump said in a statement. "The people of Oleshky have limited electricity, live amid mined roads, and endure regular military attacks."

Dmytro Lubinets, Ukraine's human rights ombudsman, thanked the first lady for securing the deal.

"Under our agreement: first a ceasefire will be put in place; second, humanitarian aid will be delivered to Oleshky; and third, civilians will be allowed to move freely out of Oleshky," Lubinets said in a video message posted on Telegram. "The operation will be carried out gradually and is extremely challenging to implement."

Russia also confirmed the agreement and said it will halt fire earlier than what the White House announced at 0400 local time (0100 GMT) on October 27.

According to the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe Office for Democratic Institutions and Human Rights, residents in Oleshky say Russian occupying forces prevented them from leaving the city. Oleshky residents also say Russia has used civilians to shield military positions in the city — a war crime under international humanitarian law.

The ceasefire deal in Oleshky comes as US, Ukrainian and Russian negotiators are expected to meet in Abu Dhabi next month to discuss ending the war.

"The plan is to meet in the first half of November, and as early as possible, of course," Zelenskyy told the US conservative Hudson Institute think tank.

#52. Argentina drops investigation into President Milei over crypto fraud

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Javier Milei had denied crypto fraud allegations

A criminal investigation into Argentine President Javier Milei and his sister's links to a cryptocurrency scandal has been dropped.

The case against Milei and his sister Karina Milei was dismissed by federal judge Marcelo Martinez de Giorgi at the request of prosecutors, court records show.

The Argentinian president had denied promoting the newly launched cryptocoin $Libra , which collapsed shortly after he mentioned it in a social media post in February 2025.

He said he had acted "in good faith" when the cryptocurrency rose in value before crashing and causing huge losses for the majority of its investors. Milei is yet to respond to news of the case being dropped.

Dismissing the case, the judge said "the reported events do not fall under a legal category", and concluded that no "criminally relevant deception" had been proven.

Accusations of fraud arose when Milei posted on X, formerly Twitter, about the $Libra coin, which he said at the time would help fund small businesses and start-ups.

The cryptocurrency's price subsequently shot up when the Argentine president shared a link to buy it.

But, within a few hours, he deleted his post and the crypto nosedived in value, losing investors most of their money.

It led opposition members of Congress to plan impeachment proceedings, while some online accused Milei of a "rug pull" - where promoters of a cryptocurrency draw in buyers, only to stop trading activity and make off with the money raised from sales.

The Argentinian president, though, insisted: "I didn't promote it, I merely shared it" in an interview with local media Todo Noticias'.

The presidential office insisted that Milei was in no way involved in the development of $Libra and announced that the Anti-Corruption Office would determine if the president had acted improperly.

Milei himself defended deleting his post, saying that at the time he did not know "the details of the project and after learning about it, I decided not to continue promoting it".

And, in a TV interview, he insisted that those who invested in $Libra had done so "voluntarily" and knew of the risks.

A shooting at a Greek Orthodox church in southern Jordan happened during a funeral, according to authorities. Two unidentified men opened fire on the procession, with Jordan's king vowing "the harshest retaliation."

A shooting at a church in the southern Jordanian governorate of Karak left seven people dead and another 34 wounded on Friday, Jordan's public security director Obaidallah Maaytah said.

Twenty-seven of the injured are receiving hospital treatment.

The shooting occurred at the Hammoud Church and reportedly took place during a funeral.

The attackers' identities and motives remain unclear. Two men opened fire on the funeral procession.

A witness told Jordanian state media there were 150 to 200 people present at the church at the time of the shooting.  Videos circulating online showed wounded people on the floor of the church covered in blood, but this footage cannot be verified by DW.

Jordan's Interior Minister Mazen Al-Faraya said a group of "extremists" carried out the massacre. According to Jordanian outlet Roya News, Al-Faraya said authorities already possess the names of suspects behind the attacks but would not publicly disclose their identities as of now.

Jordanian government spokesperson Mohammad al-Momani said security forces have launched a probe into the attack.

Jordan King Abdullah II said "the terrorist attack against Karak will be met with the harshest retaliation."

"Those who delude themselves into thinking that they can divide Jordanians with their bullets have no chance in the face of our unity," Abdullah II said, adding, "We will strike with an iron fist against those who dare to threaten our nation."

Jordan's Royal Court later posted footage and photos of the king visiting the hospital where the wounded are being treated.

Lebanese President Joseph Aoun, a Christian, condemned the "heinous terrorist attack" in Jordan and expressed condolences to the victims.

US Ambassador to the UN Mike Waltz said the news regarding the attack is "devastating."

"Praying for the victims, their families and all those affected by this depraved and brutal attack on a house of worship," Waltz said. "Attacks on Christians continue to escalate worldwide and must be stopped."

UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres' spokesperson said the attack at a place of worship during a funeral procession is "especially heinous."

Gun attacks like the one on the church in Karak are rare in Jordan.

Although Jordan is largely a Muslim-majority country of around 11.5 million people, there is a small Christian minority of a couple hundred thousand. The Karak region, about 180 kilometers (110 miles) south of Amman, has a significant Christian population.

Commonwealth Fusion Systems (CFS) announced on Thursday that it would receive $85 million from the U.S. government to develop advanced chips for the fusion power industry.

Fusion power startups have been racing to complete their first demonstration devices, with CFS aiming to turn on its Sparc reactor sometime next year. But building one reactor is just a proof point. If fusion startups want to build dozens of commercial power plants, they’ll need more durable power electronics and a robust supply chain.

The new CHIPS Act award from the Department of Commerce, which also grants the government a stake in CFS, will help boost R&D and manufacturing capacity for semiconductors in the U.S., the company said.

“Ultimately, that means a more capable, economical power plant and a stronger U.S. supply chain to build it,” Brandon Sorbom, co-founder and chief science officer at CFS, wrote in a blog post.

CFS’s award will go toward supporting five different initiatives.

The first is the advancement of silicon carbide power electronics. The semiconductors are widely used to manage high voltages, and they’re found in everything from LEDs and EVs to cell phone base stations and data centers. It’s also used in fusion machines, where intense bursts of electricity are used to drive the superconducting magnets that confine plasma inside a reactor.

CFS will also use the award to develop next-generation radio-frequency power generators, which the company uses to heat fusion fuel, and advanced electronics that will enable faster control of the reactor and be hardened against radiation. The latter could help extend the lifespan of electronics within a fusion power plant, reducing operating costs.

Lastly, CFS plans to use part of the CHIPS award to adapt AI hardware like GPUs and TPUs for power plants.

“The funding would help improve the U.S. chip and power electronics industries at the same time it accelerates fusion energy’s progress toward the electrical grid,” Sorbom said.

#42. White House says Melania Trump brokered truce for Ukraine city 'on brink of disaster'

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

These residents were able to get out of Oleshky in spring this year

US First Lady Melania Trump has "successfully secured" a ceasefire between Russia and Ukraine in the Russian-occupied city of Oleshky, the White House has said.

It said the truce in the "front-line war city on the brink of disaster" without medicine, food and clean water would begin on 29 October.

Ukraine and Russia confirmed the deal was agreed to evacuate residents and deliver aid. They both said their forces would stop firing at 02:00 GMT on 27 October.

Oleshky was seized by Russia during the first days of its full-scale invasion in February 2022. Trapped between Ukrainian positions across the Dnipro River and Russian forces to the east, residents have recently resorted to eating garden weeds to survive.

Trump: First lady's 'initiative' led to truce for Ukraine city

The White House statement said Melania Trump and one of her representatives had worked "directly with Russian and Ukrainian leadership to define the terms of the localised ceasefire".

"Non-military, civilian leaders from both countries will work together to visit Oleshky residents in their homes and determine the level of assistance they require," she is quoted as saying.

"Residents who wish to evacuate will receive co-ordinated support and transportation to their preferred destination. I anticipate some will want to go to Russia, and some to Ukraine."

Melania Trump added that Oleshky residents "have limited electricity, live amid mined roads, and endure regular military attacks" and that "without my intervention, the possibility to bring humanitarian aid into the city and to evacuate the elderly would be impossible".

The statement did not say how long the ceasefire would be in place for.

Davyd Arakhamia, a Ukrainian MP who heads President Volodymyr Zelensky's party in parliament, later said "an operation to unblock the settlement of Oleshky and its surroundings will begin in the near future".

"To achieve this goal, a ceasefire will be achieved in the near future, as announced by the First Lady of the United States, Melania Trump," he said.

In Russia, President Vladimir Putin's spokesman confirmed that, "those who wish to do so will be able to leave this settlement".

Oleshky has been without gas or electricity since 2023, so residents have had to find other solutions

Last month, some of the remaining Oleshky residents told the BBC harrowing accounts of how they were surviving in the city that was being constantly shelled and cut off from food and water.

They said that children remaining in wider Oleshky were suffering from malnutrition, a lack of medical services and were "degrading" without access to education.

Many also told the BBC there was only one hospital partially operational, opening a few days a week to perform critical amputations.

The local population was living under a "full blockade" by Russian forces, according to Ukraine's Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha.

Ukraine says it has attempted to deliver supplies to the area but has not had success in recent months.

The Kremlin in September acknowledged that Oleshky was facing "a critical situation" and "problems with supplies of food and medicine", but added that "Russia's military was working to resolve them" and blamed Kyiv.

Oleshky was home to 24,000 people before Russia's full-scale invasion. Now Ukraine estimates that some 6,000 people remain in the city and its surrounding areas, including 200 children.

It is also one of a number of settlements where thousands of people lost their homes to a massive flooding caused by the collapse of Nova Khakovka Dam in 2023. Ukraine and its allies accused Russia of blowing up the dam, while Russia blamed Ukraine.

Melania Trump worked directly with Russian and Ukrainian leadership to agree the ceasefire, the White House said.

While the US first lady is relatively lower profile than some of her predecessors - and only occasionally seen at White House events - she has become involved in addressing humanitarian issues stemming from the Russia-Ukraine war.

In August 2025, Melania Trump penned a letter to Putin, telling him it was "time to protect children and future generations worldwide".

Although she did not mention Ukraine by name, just days later at the White House Zelensky handed over a letter from his wife Olena, thanking her for her efforts on children.

In a rare public appearance in October the same year, the US first lady said that Putin had answered directly in writing and that her representatives were working with the Russian president's team to repatriate Ukrainian children from Russia and vice versa.

Some 34 children have reportedly been returned since 2025, including five this July.

According to US President Donald Trump, his wife has also privately expressed scepticism about Putin and his desire to achieve peace.

"I'd get home, I'd say, 'First lady, I had the most wonderful talk with Vladimir. I think we're finished'," Trump said in July last year.

"And then I'll turn on the television, or she'll say to me one time, 'Wow, that's strange because they just bombed a nursing home.'"
