#4103. Scientists Develop a Clever New Way To Kill Cancer Cells From the Inside

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Scientists have developed an experimental strategy that exploits a chemical byproduct of cancer cells to produce anticancer drugs directly inside them.

Scientists in Japan have developed an experimental method that uses a chemical produced by cancer cells to manufacture anticancer drugs directly inside them. The approach relies on carbon monoxide, a toxic gas that certain tumors produce in unusually high amounts, to activate a chemical reaction designed to destroy cancer cells while limiting damage to healthy tissue.

In a proof-of-concept study published in Nature Communications, researchers at the RIKEN Pioneering Research Institute (PRI) demonstrated that their system could generate drug compounds selectively in cultured cancer cells. The findings suggest a possible alternative to conventional chemotherapy, although the technique has not yet been tested in animals or humans.

Carbon monoxide is best known as a poisonous gas released during incomplete combustion, including in vehicle exhaust. However, the human body also produces small amounts of it naturally. Research has shown that many types of cancer cells generate elevated levels, creating a chemical difference that could potentially be exploited for targeted treatment.

A team led by Katsunori Tanaka investigated whether this excess carbon monoxide could serve as a raw material for producing anticancer compounds inside tumors. Their strategy uses palladacycles, organometallic compounds containing palladium, to convert carbon monoxide into a molecule called phenanthridinone. Certain phenanthridinone derivatives are toxic to cells and have been used in anticancer drugs.

The researchers faced a significant obstacle: palladium catalysts are rapidly deactivated by substances naturally present inside cells, including glutathione. Without protection, the catalyst would lose its ability to carry out the drug-producing reaction.

To solve this problem, the team enclosed a palladacycle within albumin, an abundant protein found in blood. Albumin acts as a protective carrier, shielding the palladium catalyst from molecules that would otherwise disable it. The resulting albumin complex can circulate through the bloodstream and remain chemically active after entering cells.

After screening several palladacycles to identify the most effective candidate, the researchers tested the protected complex against human liver, cervical, breast, and lung cancer cell lines, along with a noncancerous cell line.

Following 48 hours of incubation, the researchers detected the intended phenanthridinone product in all four cancer cell lines but not in the noncancerous cells. The results indicated that the protected catalyst remained functional and that the reaction depended on chemical conditions associated with the cancer cells.

The team then refined the system to allow the newly synthesized compound to separate from its carrier and move freely within the cell. Further modifications enabled the production of a highly toxic phenanthridinone derivative used in anticancer drugs. When tested in liver cancer cells, the modified system generated the toxic compound internally, causing harmful effects in those cells.

Another concern was whether carbon monoxide already present in blood might activate drug production before the complex reached a tumor. Blood tests showed no detectable formation of the toxic compound. The researchers suggested that carbon monoxide bound to hemoglobin, the oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells, was unavailable to participate in the reaction.

Rather than administering a finished anticancer drug, Tanaka’s approach aims to deliver relatively harmless chemical components that become toxic only after encountering suitable conditions inside diseased cells.

“This work represents an important advance in therapeutic in vivo synthetic chemistry,” says Tanaka. “In our therapeutic strategy, molecules naturally present or generated within the body—including disease-associated metabolites—are used as endogenous chemical feedstocks to synthesize therapeutic molecules directly at the site of disease.”

The researchers’ next step is to test the method in animal models, where they can investigate whether the catalyst reaches tumors, produces sufficient amounts of the drug, and avoids damaging healthy tissues.

Reference: “Harnessing endogenous carbon monoxide for cancer-selective drug synthesis” by Masayuki Kawai, Tsung-Che Chang, Ambara R. Pradipta and Katsunori Tanaka, 17 September 2026, Nature Communications. DOI: 10.1038/s41467-026-77616-8

AI hasn’t benefited the bottom line of any big corporations outside the tech industry, according to a note recently published by the chief economist at Apollo Global Management (the private equity giant that used to own TechCrunch).

Apollo’s Torsten Slok focuses on what he calls the S&P 493 — the S&P 500, minus Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia and Tesla — finding that profit margins and revenue per worker appear to be flat, with no evidence that AI is improving productivity.

“Despite massive investments and widespread hype around artificial intelligence, the technology has delivered zero financial payoff to corporate America outside of tech,” Slok said.

#3461. Protein shakes for teenagers: Good or bad idea?

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Protein products are marketed as a way of building muscle and losing weight — and they're very popular with young people. They also claim to promote healthy growth in children. Is this true? How much protein is healthy?

You can take it as a powdered drink, or in a snack bar. Puddings and certain breads are also promoted as "sources of protein," and protein-enriched products like Kindershake and Teen Balance are specifically designed to appeal to a young people — and it appears to be working.

In a representative survey of almost 1,000 parents by the University of Michigan in 2024, just under half said their children, who were aged 13 to 17, had consumed protein products over the previous year. The survey found that boys were more likely to do so than girls, especially in connection with sport, or to build muscle. Girls took them to balance their diet, and to lose weight.

According to the statistics portal Statista, between March 2024 and March 2025 the fitness protein product market in Germany recorded a huge jump of around 25% in retail revenue.

Proteins are key building blocks for our metabolism. They play an important role in building up and repairing tissue in our muscles, skin and organs. Proteins are the basis for the formation of enzymes that regulate chemical reactions in the body, and of antibodies that are vital for our immune system.

According to the German Nutrition Society (DGE), healthy adults should consume 0.8 grams of protein daily per kilogram of body weight. A woman weighing 65 kilos should eat 52 grams of protein per day, for example.

But they can do this without special protein products. A portion of oat flakes with an almond drink for breakfast, a baked potato with curd cheese for lunch, and two slices of wholemeal bread with peanut butter in the evening — objective achieved.

Young people should consume a little more, as they are still growing. For them, the formula is 0.9 grams of protein per kilo of body weight. A teenager weighing 45 kilos should therefore eat 40.5 grams of protein per day. People over 65 and children aged one to four should have a little more: one gram of protein per kilo of body weight.

Older people tend to eat too little protein, often because their appetite decreases. But a study done in Germany between 2015 and 2017 showed that children and young people there often consume too much. Around half of six- to 17-year-olds ate more than double the recommended daily amount of protein.

Too much protein can be harmful in the long term, according to the DGE. Various studies show that children who eat too much protein, particularly animal protein, often become overweight. Some enter puberty early.

Above all, too much protein can put excessive strain on the kidneys, causing damage. The kidneys have to convert the amino acids that constitute the building blocks of proteins into urea before they are excreted in urine. Damage is particularly likely if a person doesn't drink enough fluids, or has pre-existing kidney damage — but adolescents are also at greater risk.

A person's protein requirement is greater if they are very physically active. Among other things, according to a DGE position paper, proteins can help maintain or build up muscular mass and strength, and support recovery after strain.

The paper states that, depending on physical condition and what they are training for, sportspeople should consume between 1.2 and 2 grams of protein per kilo of body weight, divided into several portions throughout the day. These recommendations only apply to people who train for more than five hours a day.

Proteins are of interest to people who want to lose weight because they are good at satisfying hunger. People usually lose more weight by following a protein-rich diet rather than another. Long-term, though, the most important thing to look at is: How many calories am I consuming, and how many am I burning?

Overall, plant proteins usually contain somewhat fewer essential amino acids than animal protein. "Essential" because the body cannot make them by itself – it has to take them in as food.

However, wholegrain products, pulses, or nuts are also higher in fiber, vitamins, and unsaturated fats – all of which are healthy. The DGE therefore advises that protein intake should be increased primarily with sources of plant protein.

Protein powders and bars are often enriched not only with vitamins and minerals but also with unhelpful ingredients like sweeteners, sugar, additives and flavorings. The German Sport University in Cologne even found doping substances in protein products that were praised for building muscle.

Adolescents in particular might experience an unpleasant side-effect of consuming protein powders. These usually consist of milk proteins. There are various ways in which milk proteins can increase the skin's production of sebum, and this can trigger or exacerbate acne, although a direct link between milk protein products and acne has not been definitively proven.

Specialists are also skeptical about protein shakes, fearing that they could lead to young people eating fewer normal meals, and thus consuming less fiber, micronutrients, and healthy fats.

Protein-enriched foods are usually expensive. In a 2022 study, consumer organizations also found that foods that are naturally high in protein, such as cheese, curds, or soya drinks, often cost more if the protein content has been highlighted by the manufacturer.

Not even sportspeople with a high protein requirement need to supplement their diet with special protein products. The DGE advises that they should aim to satisfy their requirements by optimizing their diet, and says there is no scientific evidence that our bodies require protein-enriched food supplements when put under strain.

However, the experts do say that it may make sense for people to use protein preparations if they have allergies, problems with digestion, chewing, or swallowing, or if their appetite is very low.

U.S. federal safety regulators continue to tackle the sticky problem of autonomous vehicles, this time with a proposal that could signal to the public, quite literally, when a vehicle is operating autonomously.

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration has proposed new lighting requirements for autonomous vehicles that include an option (dare I say a strong suggestion?) to use cyan-colored lights to signal when a vehicle is operating autonomously without a human occupant monitoring the road.

The agency is not proposing a mandate for blue-green lights. Importantly, under the proposal, the lights would be used when an AV is operating in autonomous mode.

Still, federal regulators are clearly seeking some uniform way of identifying autonomous vehicles.

The proposal establishes equipment specifications, including where the lights are mounted, the color they emit, and how they’re activated. It would also remove requirements for manually operated controls for turn signals, high beams, and hazard lights.

Under the proposal, the NHTSA suggests the lights be cyan (blue-green), remain steadily illuminated rather than flash, and be visible from the front, rear, and both sides.

This latest proposal is one piece of a bigger effort to update regulations that help speed up the adoption of AVs while maintaining safety, according to NHTSA administrator Jonathan Morrison.

The agency is already working on a federal automated vehicle framework and is trying to update federal motor vehicle safety standards, which already govern automakers today. #4014. Audi defends plans for a $4 billion Georgia plant amid VW restructuring, tariffs

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

DETROIT — Audi's CEO defended the carmaker's plans for a new $4 billion plant in Georgia on Sunday, calling it a business decision aimed at growing sales and reducing exposure to U.S. tariffs.

The plant, set to be Audi's first in the U.S., was announced Saturday by President Donald Trump during a rally in Georgia, near where the Volkswagen-owned luxury brand plans to build the new SUV-producing facility.

"We have significantly greater ambitions for Audi in America," Audi CEO Gernot Dollner said during a media roundtable Sunday. "The United States is the second largest premium market in the world, and today we are not fully exploiting this potential. We are changing that."

The announcement comes amid an intense restructuring of its VW parent company, including plans to cut 50,000 jobs and close four German plants, including an Audi facility, between 2031 and ​2034.

Dollner, who joined Trump at the rally, said the decision for a plant in the U.S. was years in the making, with more than 70 locations evaluated over "many months" and "intense" negotiations with public officials.

"This is not a decision against Germany. Germany remains the backbone of Audi," Dollner said. "We develop our cars here. Our technologies are created here, and Georgia does not take any task away from us in Germany."

Dollner said localized production is the best decision for the brand, which has struggled in sales this decade amid rising operational costs and Trump's tariffs on vehicles from Germany and other countries into the U.S. Current tariffs on vehicles from Germany are 15%, down from 25%.

Last year, U.S. tariffs cost Audi 1.2 billion euros ($1.4 billion), as it imports all its vehicles for the U.S. market from Europe or Mexico.

Dollner said the Trump administration asked Audi to announce the new plant at the Georgia rally promoting Republicans ahead of Nov. 3 midterm elections.

"It was … a three-year project that, at the end, by coincidence, led to that moment yesterday," he said Sunday. "With respect of the office and country, we joined the invitation."

Dollner noted Audi is the last major German luxury brand to establish U.S. production after Mercedes-Benz and BMW.

The company said the Georgia plant is expected to begin production of Audi's Q7, Q8 and Q9 in the early 2030s. The plant would have the capacity to produce up to 200,000 vehicles annually, roughly half of which the automaker said will be exported outside the U.S.

"This is to make Audi obviously a significant player here in the U.S. market," said Audi of America President Vito Paladino during the roundtable. He also said additional details on U.S. investments and production are forthcoming.

Audi's U.S. deliveries were roughly flat through the third quarter of this year, following a 16% decline from 2024 to 2025.

#3618. Trump immigration win could strengthen admin's hand in looming Supreme Court fight

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Supreme Court justices will begin hearing oral arguments Monday on a wide range of cases this term involving gun rights, religious freedom, immigration detention and the limits of government power.

The Trump administration scored a major full-panel victory at the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals Friday, which ruled that detained illegal immigrants who were never formally admitted into the U. S. are not constitutionally entitled to bond hearings while awaiting deportation.

The ruling comes as the Supreme Court prepares to hear Rhoney v. Barbosa da Cunha this term, a closely related fight over whether the federal mandatory-detention statute applies at all to longtime immigrants arrested inside the country — setting up the possibility that the justices could either undercut Friday’s ruling or strengthen the government's hand.

The Fifth Circuit answered the constitutional question in Ignacio Sosnava Rodriguez v. Sylvester M. Ortega, finding that three immigrant men detained under the mandatory-detention statute are not entitled to bond hearings under the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. The court held that their years or even decades of residence in the United States, along with ties to the country and their communities, do not create a constitutional right to a bond hearing.

"The Constitution does not entitle them to a bond hearing," the court wrote.

FEDERAL APPEALS COURT COMPARES TRUMP MIGRANT DETENTION POLICY TO JAPANESE AMERICAN INTERNMENT

Dozens of immigrants are detained by US Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) agents inside the Federal Plaza courthouse in New York City on Thursday, June 26, 2025, following their legal proceedings.  (Lokman Vural Elibol/Anadolu via Getty Images)

Meanwhile, the Supreme Court will consider Rhoney, a case out of the Second Circuit, to determine whether the mandatory-detention law applies to longtime immigrants arrested inside the country in the first place.

At the center of the issue is a 9-2 circuit split over which federal detention law applies to immigrants who entered the country illegally, were never formally admitted and were later arrested by immigration authorities after living in the U.S. for years — sometimes decades after crossing the border.

The Fifth and Eighth Circuits have held that those immigrants remain "applicants for admission" under Section 1225 of the Immigration and Nationality Act, subjecting them to mandatory detention without an ordinary bond hearing while their removal proceedings play out. Nine other federal appeals courts have ruled that Section 1226 applies instead, which provides a path for them to seek release on bond.

The Supreme Court will essentially decide whether immigrants who entered illegally and have lived in the U.S. for years can be subjected to the same mandatory-detention provision that applies to certain immigrants seeking admission into the country.

If the Supreme Court sides with the Second Circuit, Barbosa da Cunha — a Brazilian national who entered the country illegally roughly two decades ago and was detained in 2025 — would have a path to seek release on bond. Such a ruling would likely apply to the three longtime immigrants at the center of Rodriguez as well, substantially undercutting the significance of Friday’s ruling by eliminating the need to answer the constitutional question of whether due process requires a bond hearing.

SUPREME COURT TAKES UP FIGHT OVER TRUMP’S THIRD-COUNTRY ILLEGAL ALIEN DEPORTATION POLICY

The US Supreme Court in Washington, DC, US, on Tuesday, Nov. 4, 2025. (Pete Kiehart/Bloomberg via Getty Images)

Judge Leslie Southwick pointed directly to the looming Supreme Court fight in her dissent.

Should the Supreme Court eventually determine that this court was wrong as to the statute, then the constitutional issue we address here will have far less significance. It certainly has significance at the moment," Southwick wrote.

But conservative legal experts say Rodriguez could also strengthen the administration’s hand in Rhoney by undercutting one argument against the government’s interpretation of Section 1225: that applying the mandatory-detention provision to longtime residents would raise serious constitutional concerns.

Michael Morley, a constitutional law professor at Florida State University, said Friday’s ruling could influence how the justices approach that question because courts often consider whether a particular reading of federal law would create constitutional problems.

FEDERAL APPEALS COURT DEALS BLOW TO TRUMP DEPORTATION AGENDA

U.S. federal agents working for Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) detain immigrants and asylum seekers reporting for immigration court proceedings at the Jacob K. Javits Federal Building's U.S. Immigration Court in New York, New York, Thursday, July 24, 2025. (Getty Images)

"The Fifth Circuit’s en banc ruling in Rodriguez today takes the next step by holding that such detention would not violate the U.S. Constitution’s Due Process Clause," Morley said. "This holding may impact the Supreme Court’s reasoning, because an important consideration in disputes over federal laws is whether a particular interpretation would be unconstitutional."

"If the Court agrees with the Fifth Circuit’s analysis that the Due Process Clause allows such detention, it may be more likely to conclude that federal law permits it, as well, Morley continued.

Hans von Spakovsky, a senior legal fellow at Advancing American Freedom, argued that the decision could bolster the administration as the justices prepare to resolve the circuit split.

"I actually think this ruling will help the government in the Rhoney case," von Spakovsky said, pointing to the Fifth Circuit’s position that the applicable immigration statute does not require bond hearings for immigrants who were never admitted to the United States.

"There was already a split in the circuit courts on this issue but the Fifth Circuit has consistently held that the applicable immigration statute does not require bond hearings for aliens who were never admitted to the United States," he said. "I will be surprised if a majority of the Supreme Court disagrees when they ultimately issue a decision in the Rhoney case."

The Supreme Court has not yet scheduled oral arguments in Rhoney, which it agreed to hear earlier this month.

#4107. A Single Enzyme Could Hold the Key to Treating a Devastating Brain Disorder

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Blocking an enzyme’s production restored brain growth and healthy behaviors in mice engineered to develop Angelman syndrome, pointing to a possible treatment target.

Children with Angelman syndrome begin to show difficulties with thinking and movement at around 6 months of age. The rare condition occurs in approximately one in 20,000 births and is caused by the absence of a gene normally inherited from the mother. Researchers know which gene is missing, but they have struggled to explain why its absence causes these problems, making treatments elusive.

Research in mice now points to a chain of events that connects the missing gene to a stress response that interferes with brain growth. A team led by Jason Yi, PhD, an assistant professor of neuroscience at Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis, traced that response to a buildup of a normally harmless enzyme. The study was published in Nature.

The missing gene, UBE3A, produces an enzyme called ubiquitin ligase, which helps keep cells orderly by marking unneeded proteins for disposal. It is active throughout the brain and participates in 60 different cellular processes. Identifying which of those processes contribute to Angelman syndrome required narrowing that broad field of possibilities.

Yi and his colleagues developed modified cells in the laboratory with UBE3A deactivated, then looked for proteins that accumulated without it. They identified 23 candidate proteins whose levels increased. One stood out for its strong connection to ubiquitin ligase’s normal housekeeping function: asparagine synthetase, an enzyme that helps cells produce essential proteins.

In mice genetically modified to develop Angelman syndrome, elevated levels of asparagine synthetase activated the brain’s stress response. Yet the enzyme does not interact with the usual triggers for that response, and its overabundance does not otherwise damage the cell. The consequences instead arise indirectly, as the stress response sharply reduces protein production and growth.

“The neurons are tricked into thinking that they’re in stress when they’re not — that’s bad because they will then not grow normally,” Yi said, noting that most children with Angelman syndrome have smaller brains than their peers of the same age. “It’s a reaction from the brain trying to conserve resources to protect itself. It does suggest that there is a dysregulation in the brain we might be able to correct.”

Blocking the production of asparagine synthetase restored normal brain growth and healthy behaviors in the mice. The team had corrected a later step in the biological sequence set off by the missing gene, allowing healthy development to resume.

The study is the first to link an increase in asparagine synthetase to a neurological condition. It adds to evidence that an imbalance in proteins can contribute to neurodevelopmental disorders by triggering stress responses, even when the proteins themselves are not directly damaging cells.

Yi said medications could potentially target the stress response triggered by asparagine synthetase, but much remains to be learned before work on a possible treatment can begin. His team is investigating precisely why the enzyme activates that response, where in the brain the response is most active, and when during development medical interventions would be most effective.

“We still don’t know a whole lot about the disease,” said Yi. “We haven’t been able to identify all of the things that the missing gene is doing and rescue the things that it is not doing in Angelman’s syndrome. But I think our data are the most complete so far to begin answering those questions.”

Nemera, Travis Law, Huma Naz, Guoyan Zhao, Harrison W. Gabel, Gary J. Patti, Erik S. Musiek, Tae-Hyuk Ahn and Jason J. Yi, 21 October 2026, Nature. DOI: 10.1038/s41586-026-11165-4

#4115. Just Two Days on a Vegan Diet Triggered Unexpected Changes in the Liver

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Switching from a vegetarian to a vegan diet altered liver metabolism in mice within just two days, making glucose production and toxic waste removal less efficient.

Millions of people follow a vegan diet, excluding all animal-derived products. Plant-based eating is recommended in part for its environmental benefits, but the health consequences of getting all dietary protein from plants are not yet fully understood. For researchers, that raises a question about how the body responds when the source of its protein changes.

Scientists at the University of Geneva (UNIGE) investigated that question in mice, focusing on the liver, which helps maintain blood sugar and processes the components of dietary protein. Their study, published in Molecular Metabolism, compared a lacto-vegetarian diet containing both animal and plant proteins with a vegan diet.

“In just two days, the mice fed an exclusively vegan diet showed less efficient glucose production,” explains Pierre Maechler, full professor in the Department of Cell Physiology and Metabolism and at the Diabetes Center of the UNIGE Faculty of Medicine. “Furthermore, their livers were less effective at eliminating ammonia, a toxic substance released during the breakdown of amino acids. A significant reduction in physical activity also suggests effects on the body as a whole.”

Proteins from plants and animals consist of amino acids, small molecules joined together like links in a chain. Their types and proportions differ depending on the food source, so changing protein sources gives the body a different mixture to process. Plant proteins naturally contain fewer branched-chain amino acids than animal proteins. Along with helping build the body’s proteins, these amino acids supply some of the raw material the liver uses to produce glucose and maintain sufficient blood sugar during fasting.

“Overall, a change in diet alters liver metabolism very quickly, but this effect is just as rapidly reversible,” emphasizes Maechler. “The body adapts within one to two days to even subtle changes in dietary composition and returns to normal upon the reintroduction of animal proteins.”

The combination of less efficient glucose production and ammonia removal pointed the team toward a liver enzyme called glutamate dehydrogenase, or GDH. Located in mitochondria, the structures inside cells that help process fuel, GDH connects protein metabolism with sugar metabolism. To investigate its role, the researchers compared two groups of mice lacking the enzyme in their livers. One group ate a lacto-vegetarian diet, while the other ate a strict vegan diet.

Without GDH, the mice eating only plant proteins developed a severe buildup of ammonia and impaired blood glucose regulation compared with mice whose diet also included animal proteins. This confirmed the enzyme’s essential role in the metabolic response to an exclusively plant-based diet.

The experiments were conducted in mice, so they do not establish that people would experience the same changes. They do, however, provide a way to investigate the physiological questions that accompany a change in protein source.

“In addition to the ethical and environmental considerations that often motivate a switch to a plant-based diet, there are also scientific factors relating to the physiological functioning of our metabolism that are worth being aware of,” notes Maechler.

For people following a strict vegan diet, the researchers emphasize choosing protein sources rich in branched-chain amino acids, such as nuts, tofu, and lentils, to support the liver’s metabolic functions. They place that consideration alongside the need to ensure an adequate intake of specific vitamins.

Reference: “Transition to dietary plant-based proteins requires functional liver GDH for proper control of ammonia and energy homeostasis” by Yan Zhou, Angela M. Ramos-Lobo, Tingting Fu, Hector Gallart-Ayala, Julijana Ivanisevic and Pierre Maechler, 4 September 2026, Molecular Metabolism. DOI: 10.1016/j.molmet.2026.102435

The work was funded by the Swiss National Science Foundation (#310030_192486 to P.M.) and the canton of Geneva.
