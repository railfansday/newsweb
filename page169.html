#295. Bulgaria: Concerns about security in the Black Sea dominate presidential election campaign

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Bulgaria's presidential election campaign has been shaped by recent drone attacks on ships in the Black Sea and the government's reduced support for Ukraine, making foreign policy a key factor in the first round.

Six months after Bulgaria's parliamentary election, the country is gearing up to choose its next president.

Although the role of president in Bulgaria is largely ceremonial, the election on October 25 — with a potential runoff on November 1 — will serve as a test for the government of former President Rumen Radev, whose party won a landslide victory in April, securing an unprecedented majority — the first in Bulgaria in almost three decades.

Although there are 27 candidates on the ballot, the race has narrowed to a duel between the incumbent president, Iliana Yotova, who served two terms as Radev's vice president and is backed by the prime minister, and former caretaker Prime Minister Andrey Gyurov, who is supported by opposition parties.

According to the Bulgarian Constitution, the Bulgarian president embodies national unity. He or she represents the country at international level (with a few exceptions), is the commander-in-chief of its armed forces, appoints members of the Supreme Judicial Council and various regulatory bodies, including caretaker governments, and has the power to veto legislation.

Hristo Panchugov, a political scientist at the New Bulgarian University in Sofia, argued that although the president's powers are formally limited, the office is particularly significant in the current context.

"For the first time in Bulgaria's modern democratic history, we have an absolutely uncontested majority that is not held together by any coalition, but rather by a single political party that faces an opposition that is completely falling apart," he told DW.

Panchugov went on to say that it would have been better if the campaign had focused more on "whether the president could serve as a counterbalance."

Opposition candidate Andrey Gyurov reiterated this point in an interview with DW: "We are seeing a concentration of power that always bears the marks of a slide toward autocracy... A president should know how to say no."

Iliana Yotova declined DW's request for an interview.

Initially, the presidential race was characterized by small rallies, a lack of televised debates and numerous personal attacks on the two leading candidates.

In recent weeks, however, following drone attacks on ships in the Black Sea, the two frontrunners' diverging positions on support for Ukraine have come to the fore.

Bulgaria's government initially declined invitations to join the Coalition of the Willing and NATO's anti-drone initiative but changed its position on the latter after the recent drone attacks.

Yotova has advocated for the establishment of a "Coalition of Peace," in which she believes Bulgaria would have a stronger diplomatic voice in the process to endRussia's war in Ukraine.

"There are many warmongers in our country; I am not one of them," she said at a campaign rally this month. "As long as I am president — and if you place your trust in me — not a single Bulgarian soldier will go to Ukraine."

Yotova has sought to reassure voters that no Bulgarian troops will be sent to Ukraine, even though this has not featured in public debate, and has been ruled out by a 2025 parliamentary declaration that reaffirmed that the Bulgarian Armed Forces would not participate in hostilities there.

Nevertheless, the recent drone attacks on commercial ships in the Black Sea have put new pressure on both the president and Rumen Radev's Progressive Bulgaria party.

"The drone strikes in the Black Sea brought the otherwise abstract geopolitical situation right into the daily lives of every Bulgarian," Boriana Dimitrova, managing partner of the Alpha Research market and social research agency, told DW.

Many in Europe, including Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, have laid the blame for the attacks squarely at Russia's door. The fact that Bulgaria's leaders did not clearly attribute blame for the attacks fueled frustration across the country.

After the first drone attack, President Yotova convened the country's Consultative Council on National Security. Its subsequent declaration did not name the source of the threat in the Black Sea.

A week later, after the second attack, Prime Minister Radev said, "We lack solid, clear evidence to level accusations against any specific party; however logic suggests the outcome works to the advantage of Russia." He then summoned the Russian ambassador for talks.

The stance of the government and president is having an impact on the race.

"We're seeing a slight decline in support for Iliana Yotova, which is not threatening her first-place position by any means in the first round, but there has been a decline given the ambiguity of some of the positions she has taken," said Boriana Dimitrova.

As the issue of security entered the public debate, some Bulgarians took to the streets in Sofia to protest against what they saw as the government's ambiguous foreign policy stance on this matter.

"I think the drone attacks in the Black Sea and the utterly spineless stance of the president, the prime minister, and everyone acting in unison were the last straw, and I believe that, on the eve of the presidential election, this should serve as a catalyst," Vlada Tzenova, one of the protesters, told DW.

All this is reflected in the preliminary results of the latest opinion poll conducted by Alpha Research. While expected voter turnout is up, the gap between the two frontrunners has narrowed: Yotova is still ahead, leading Gyurov by about 12 percentage points in the first round, but the margin is smaller than it was.

Nevertheless, these recent dynamics do not necessarily mean a shift in the balance of power.

Panchugov said that Gyurov's campaign has been hampered by his hesitancy to declare his candidacy, a lack of "passion" in his campaign, and the fact that he made few comments on current affairs before he joined the race.

Moreover, supporters of the candidate of the pro-Russian nationalist Vazrazhdane (Revival) party, which ranked third in the Alpha Research poll, would be most likely to support Yotova in a second round.

What could decide the outcome of the election is the fact that both GERB and DPS, two of the established parties that attracted the support of more than 660,000 voters in April, did not field a presidential candidate.

According to Alpha Research, half of GERB's supporters do not intend to vote in the first round. The party dominated Bulgaria's political landscape for 17 years but lost power in December following mass anti-corruption protests backed by opposition parties supporting Andrey Gyurov.

Most of the remaining GERB supporters are expected to back Gyurov, as the party has historically opposed Iliana Yotova's political home, the Bulgarian Socialist Party.

At the same time, both Gyurov's campaign and nationalist parties are seeing increased mobilization, with the latter drawing support from voters disappointed by the government's cautious response to the drone attacks.

"We can view these elections as the first test of confidence in the party that recently won the election," Boriana Dimitrova said. "In peaceful times, the center attracts the most attention; in turbulent times, those candidates who take a clearer stance — regardless of the direction — gain greater visibility."

#294. Indian court orders release of two leaders of Cockroach movement

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Saurav Das and Ashutosh Ranka spent a week in jail after defying restrictions on protests in Delhi amid government crackdown

An Indian court has ordered the release of two leaders of India’s Cockroach movement who were jailed last week amid a mounting state crackdown on youth-led protests.

The two leaders of the Cockroach Janta party (CJP) movement, Saurav Das and Ashutosh Ranka, had spent a week in Delhi’s Tihar jail after being arrested by Delhi police last week.

On Wednesday, a special magistrate ordered that Das and Ranka be released, despite efforts by police to keep them in prison. They were made to sign a bond pledging no further breach of peace.

Their arrest came after a draconian police crackdown on a CJP march held in the capital two weeks ago to protest alleged manipulation of the country’s electoral roll, which led to the barricading of central Delhi and thousands of young people being detained.

Das and Ranka were taken into custody for a second time days later after they jumped police barricades and held up an Indian flag at a site in central Delhi that police had cordoned off.

The pair were charged with trespass and violating the peace and sent to jail for five days, which was then extended by two further days. Their detention had provoked outrage among leaders and supporters of the youth movement, who accused the government of “cowardice” for jailing CJP leaders for holding a peaceful protest.

After the magistrate’s ruling on Wednesday evening, Das said that “truth has triumphed”.

The release of the two leaders is likely to come as a blow to the ruling Narendra Modi government, which has implemented an unforgiving crackdown on recent protests that have erupted across India.

The CJP, alongside the opposition alliance, student groups and civil society, have been calling for the resignation of the chief election commissioner, Gyanesh Kumar, who is accused of overseeing a revision of the electoral roll that benefits Modi’s Bharatiya Janata party (BJP) and has led to more than 130 million names being deleted from the voter register. The BJP has denied any interference.

The CJP began as an online satire earlier this year, but has since grown into a fully fledged political movement, challenging the powerful Modi government. Over the summer, it was Cockroach-led protests that forced the resignation of Modi’s education minister after sustained demonstrations.

Abhijeet Dipke, the founder of the CJP movement, vowed over the weekend that the Modi regime and threat of jail would not intimidate the Cockroaches. Dipke was also detained on Friday after he called for a peaceful demonstration in central Delhi where CJP supporters would gather and wave the Indian flag in solidarity with Das and Ranka.

He had said that, once his fellow Cockroach leaders were released, they would “gather, re-strategise, and determine how to move forward”.

“The illegal arrests and detentions we have witnessed over the past few days indicate an attempt to intimidate us,” said Dipke. “But we will not be cowed down or retreat. This is a long battle and we are prepared for it.”

#293. A Dating App Is Offering ‘Chivalry Mode’ and ‘Marriage Timeline’ to Get Gen Z Married

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

When Adreanna Pulliam signed up for the Christian dating app Upward over the summer, she was prepared to come across men who identified as hyperconservative.

So when she swiped on a guy’s profile that listed “a progressive donkey or a ‘Trump is King’ elephant” as his relationship deal-breaker, she was shocked. “I hadn’t expected to see a moderate libertarian” on the app, she says.

It only got more bizarre from there. There was the guy advertising his Snapchat with the username “Yaboi Blaze reallysexgod.” Another dater, for reasons she still can’t fathom, included a photo of him at a funeral, standing in front of a poster-size image of the deceased.

While Pulliam was apprehensive about the prospects, she wanted to see how different Upward, one of the lesser-known apps in Match Group’s sprawling portfolio of dating platforms, was from Hinge and Tinder. She hadn’t had much luck with either of them. Many of the profiles she encountered on Upward, she tells WIRED, were “MAGA types of men” who “fit more of that red-pill, trad wife, conservative values” category of dater.

A 25-year-old opera singer in Bloomington, Indiana, for whom marriage has always been “the end goal,” Pulliam is the kind of person Upward is trying to appeal to since relaunching last month.

The relaunch was redesigned around two key data points from Match Group’s 2026 Singles in America study: 72 percent of Gen Zs want to get married, and a quarter of singles aged 18 to 34 “identify with a combination of traditional values around family, faith, and community,” a news release about the survey noted.

The pivot away from an app exclusively for Christian daters was about opening up its doors for people “who share those same goals, but may not lead with a faith-first mindset,” says Lauren DeFord, director of brand marketing at Match, of the app’s new focus on Gen Z singles.

Upward’s expansion is representative of the ongoing change in the dating market. Dwindling engagement and the introduction of AI have pushed companies to reassess user growth. For some, that means abandoning their original mission.

Upward, which runs on a tiered subscription, works similarly to Tinder. You can swipe left or right on profiles, and users are encouraged to include a Faith Statement on their page, explaining what they value most about their relationship to God. Among its new features are Chivalry Mode, which, if you’re familiar with how Bumble used to operate, persuades men to message first, and Marriage Timeline, which lets daters share when they hope to get married with potential suitors.

But while a marriage-focused app like Upward may be tapping into a genuine desire for commitment, that doesn’t mean young people are returning to traditional relationship norms en masse, says Daniel Cox, author of Generation Uncoupled: America’s New Gender Divide and the Crisis of Human Connection. In fact, the opposite is happening.

In his book, Cox argues that Gen Z is entering adulthood with a less clear sense of their “relational priorities,” and a decline in social and civic life has given rise to a “hyperindividualistic culture” that doesn’t always prioritize things like marriage. As a result, fewer Gen Zs are coupling up today. An American Institute for Boys and Men survey found that 44 percent of Gen Z men have had no relationship experience at all during their teen years, double the rate for older men. “This movement makes it a lot harder to ... want to sacrifice and to be there for someone,” he says.

Upward’s relaunch framed the app as the “home of modern courtship,” including video testimonials of singles who’d found love on the app. But aesthetically, there wasn’t anything “modern” about its promotional rollout. In one video, newly married Zach donned a cowboy hat and blue jeans, doing all the things men should presumably do for their significant others: gift them flowers, carry her through a meadow, open the car door. A bold text overlay on the video read “chivalrymaxxing.” According to the company, branding was inspired by an “Americana” theme, and its “Wanted: For Life” campaign draws on Western-style imagery.

Cox, who is the director of the Survey Center on American Life, is convinced that the trad trend is “a reaction to the lack of cultural norms around courtship. People are wanting something over nothing,” he says, adding that daters are desperate for “a script” with direction and defined roles, given how nontraditional approaches to partnership, such as polyamory and relationship anarchy, are increasing among young people. “And something that’s nice about the trad script is it’s very clear. Though I don’t think it’s close to ideal for a lot of young people.”

A 2023 Pew Research survey found that only 23 percent of adults considered being married “extremely” or “very important” to living a fulfilling life, compared with 61 percent who said the same about having close friends. In the last 30 years, the percentage of high school seniors who said they were likely to marry someday had fallen from 80 percent in 1993 to 67 percent in 2023, according to Pew. The decline was driven mostly by changing views among young women who, despite the portion of married adults slightly increasing from 50 percent to 51 percent between 2019 to 2023, remain more likely than men to be unpartnered.

The trend is likely to continue in that direction. “There’s increasingly a belief among young women that marriage makes them less happy,” Cox says. “So it’s not only that they have less of a desire to get married. There’s a concern that if they get married, they won’t be as happy as if they are single. That seems like a view that is potentially not going to change.”

Upward, which declined to share information regarding the number of active users on the platform, is betting that it will.

When setting up her account, Pulliam was baffled to find out that Upward let users list their body type, ranging from “fit” and “curvy” to “chubby,” a feature typically reserved for hookup apps. She was also confused that Upward allows married people on the app. Some of the stranger options for relationship type included “it’s complicated” and “I’m not into labels.” “I’m not knocking open relationships or anything like that, but it was interesting to see such a progressive take from a more of a conservative demographic,” she says.

Tauti, a social work student who lives in California, lasted less than 48 hours on the app, disturbed by the overwhelming conservative majority looking for domesticated partners. Sometimes the hook with mission-specific apps like Upward is that the religious identity associated with the platform can create an assumption of compatibility before users have really gotten to know one another. “I did not know me being Christian and leaning liberal was a big ask,” she said on TikTok. “It’s turned me off from Christian dating altogether.”

Some users believe the recent pivot is diluting the experience for daters who joined seeking out people with purely Christian beliefs. “Why are you on there if you’re agnostic?” Heidi Marais, a teacher and single mom, asked on TikTok in September. “And Upward, why is it an option?”

DeFord, who maintains that Christian singles remain at the core of Upward’s mission despite describing the relaunch as a “brand shift,” pushes back against the assertion that the experience is worsening. “Those users can still interact and match with people who share the same denomination, the same religious views, the same cadence of how they like to practice.”

Since its debut in 2020, Upward has struggled with various hiccups that include everything from functionality issues to user perception. Many reviews in the Apple App Store complain of terrible user support, poor filtering features, and automatic bans, with one user calling the app “janky as hell.”

Cox notes there is ample reason to be wary of Match’s claim that a majority of Gen Z want to get married. Given that Gen Z is younger, it is likely that more people have never tied the knot. “So if they’re comparing Gen Z to millennials or Gen Xers in the survey, it could be that they have already been in relationships, maybe even divorced, so they are not as eager to get married again.” Match’s survey, which the company says is “demographically representative” of the US Census, sampled 5,000 US singles over the age of 18 who were not married, living with a partner, or in a committed relationship. In any case, Cox says, the “norm around what is the ideal age to get married” has vanished.

Pulliam still wants to get hitched but doesn’t consider it a priority right now. She attended a Christian college and recognizes the potential for Upward to be a hit “within communities of Christian private universities,” she says. “But I think the only way that the app works is for maybe someone who has a little bit more of a closed mind.”

#289. Moderna shares plunge after cancer vaccine data leaks — despite positive results

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Moderna shares dropped as much as 25% on Thursday following the leak of partial data on its closely watched cancer vaccine developed with Merck.

Shares of both drugmakers fell even though the data, released in full later Thursday following the leak, appeared meaningful for patients and positive relative to Wall Street expectations.

The personalized vaccine, known as intismeran autogene, paired with Merck's immunotherapy Keytruda reduced the risk of recurrence or death in certain melanoma patients by 42% compared with Keytruda alone.

The data also showed that the shot along with Keytruda reduced the risk of cancer spreading to distant parts of the body or death by 38%, compared with just the immunotherapy.

The companies said about 82% of patients who received the personalized cancer vaccine alongside Keytruda were alive without their melanoma returning at two years after receiving the shot, compared with 70% of those who received Keytruda alone.

"This is a huge development for our patients," said Dr. Hussein Tawbi, a melanoma expert and trial investigator at the University of Texas MD ​Anderson Cancer Center, about the results.

He said that the added benefit of the vaccine also comes with much milder toxicity, or side effects like injection-site pain, compared to other cancer treatments.

The detailed phase 3 trial results were set to be presented at the European Society for Medical Oncology conference this weekend.

"Whenever you put a therapy on top of something that works really well, like Keytruda, sometimes it's harder to show additional benefit. We have no problems of showing additional benefit here," Dr. Dean Li, Merck Research Laboratories president, said in an interview ahead of the results.

The benefit appeared even larger after the six-month vaccine regimen, with a separate analysis showing a 58% reduction in risk of recurrence or death. The companies say it is still too early to determine whether the treatment helps patients live longer overall.

Several analysts acknowledged investors' elevated expectations ahead of the release, but noted that the data is overall positive.

"We assert this relative reduction in recurrence/death is clinically meaningful," BMO Capital Markets analyst Evan Seigerman said in a research note on Thursday following the leak. "Such a [stock] reaction likely reflects inflated expectations into the full data release, rather than a true indictment on the efficacy of the combo therapy."

Oncologists and investors see the targeted vaccine as a possible breakthrough treatment, with the potential to be used to treat other forms of cancer in the future. Merck and Moderna are studying the vaccine in several trials across other tumors, such as non-small cell lung cancer, bladder cancer and renal cell carcinoma.

But Li acknowledged the companies have more work to show the product can be effective across tumor types.

"I can dream of creating a log cabin, but we got some wood chops, and we've just knocked down one tree," Li said. "Knocking one tree does not give you a cabin or a village."

For now, the vaccine is not far from potentially entering the market for the first time and reaching melanoma patients. In an interview ahead of the results, Moderna CEO Stéphane Bancel said the company is finishing a filing with regulators to get the drug fully approved next year.

"It's a very, very happy moment for the field, for patients," he said.

The shot is designed to target the specific mutations in each patient's tumor rather than using a one-size-fits-all approach. The idea is to train the immune system to recognize and kill those unique cancer markers and then pair that response with Keytruda, which helps the body attack the cancer more effectively.

It could also offer Moderna a critical additional commercial product in its pipeline, following vaccines for Covid-19, the flu and respiratory syncytial virus.

In a research note Thursday, UBS analysts said "this is overall a strong data set" and gives more confidence in upcoming studies of the vaccine in kidney and bladder cancers. They added the stock reaction may be due more to massive expectations than disappointing data.

Against the backdrop of those high expectations, Moderna shares have spiked more than 530% year-to-date as of Thursday afternoon.

Shares of Merck, a much larger drugmaker, fell by about 1% on Thursday. The company's stock has climbed nearly 41% this year.

Since the vaccine is personalized, the complicated manufacturing process can take six weeks. But Merck CEO Robert Davis told CNBC last week that the company believes it can shorten that timeline in the future, potentially allowing the shot to reach more patients more quickly.

That long drought looks set to end on Monday, with Harry Kane, born 12 years after Rummenigge's win, the favorite to pick up the trophy at a ceremony held, as a one-off, in his native London.

"It's obviously special for me that the ceremony is taking place in London. "I come from London and lived there for a long time — maybe that's a good omen," Kane told club magazine Säbener51. "The Ballon d'Or would be a massive success for me."

Lamine Yamal, the prodigious Barcelona and Spain forward who won the Euros at 16 looks to be the main competitor. At the end of the 2025-26 season, on which the award is judged, Yamal won the World Cup with Spain, as well as the La Liga title.

Kane's 73 goals for club and country last season make for a strong case, and the Bundesliga's most expensive signing of all time has already racked up 156 goals in 158 games for the serial German champions. But it's not just the numbers that tell the story, according to his club coach.

That attitude has been with Kane from the start. His first-ever coach, Dave Bricknell, from Ridgeway Rovers, an amateur club in northeast London, told DW after he joined Bayern from Spurs in 2023: "Harry's mentality is his greatest strength. Other than Ousmane Dembele, who won it last year after Paris Saint-Germain's Champions League win, and Lionel Messi, a serial Ballon d'Or winner, the last player to take the Ballon d'Or for a season played outside England or Spain was was Kaka at AC Milan in 2007, when Italy's Serie A was considered a stronger league.

Kane will be 35 by the time of the next Euros, in the UK and Ireland in 2028, and has a deal with Bayern until the following year. His form since joining Bayern suggests that he could get better still.

#285. TDM Neo Hybrid Headphones Review: Two-in-One Woes

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Unveiled at CES 2026, the TDM Neo is a pair of on-ear headphones that transform into a Bluetooth speaker. It caught the imagination of the attending media, won a handful of awards, and inspired countless column inches.

Given the current state of the portable audio industry, it's easy to understand why. Most designs seem to revolve around incremental updates and new colorways rather than genuine technical innovation. Neo is an exciting alternative, and it’s little wonder the startup brand went on to smash its Kickstarter targets.

I’ve spent the past couple of weeks listening to the headphones and Bluetooth speaker, and have lost count of the number of times I’ve showed off the clever twist-and-fold mechanism to friends. But has TDM managed to produce a genuine tech twofer, or designed a novelty product that gives you the worst of both worlds?

The twist-and-fold mechanism on the Neo is clever and addictive. Rotate the ear cups around the cushioned but flexible headband, bring them together, and strong magnets snap everything firmly in place. Once connected, the headphone drivers disable, and the outward-facing Bluetooth speakers kick in. Twist them apart and you've got your headphones back.

It’s innovative, and the engineering is impressive. Once in speaker mode, the two rubber TDM logos sit closely together and form a protective base, while the asymmetrical twisted headband cocoons the ear cups together. It works well—it never once popped open accidentally. The action is smooth, the connection reassuringly solid, and switching between modes works exactly as intended.

The Neo has four 40-mm drivers—two for the headphones and two for the speakers. The outward-facing speakers are each rated at 5 watts (for a total of 10). Ten watts is not a lot for a modern Bluetooth speaker, but wattage doesn't automatically tell you how loud or good a speaker will sound, especially in the current age of digital signal processing. The highly regarded $55 JBL Go 5 has just 4.5 watts of output, for instance.

Battery life is quoted at more than 200 hours for headphones and 16 hours in speaker mode, which is pretty impressive, and a quick five-minute charge gives you an extra eight hours.

On-device control is basic but effective, with rubber power and volume buttons together, and a separate one for Bluetooth. One quirk of the twist design is that in speaker mode the volume button is inverted, and on first use I instinctively turned the volume down instead of up. The power and 3.5-mm AUX input are also left facing up, rather than being hidden from view. This also means the USB-C cable sticks out the top—not the most aesthetic solution.

The TDM companion app allows you to customize what happens when the headphones are twisted. You can switch between headphone and speaker playback, pause the music, or power off. You can also lock them in speaker mode to listen with the Neo around your neck, but please, if you have any love for your fellow commuters, don’t do this on the train. There’s also basic EQ settings for both sets of speakers and Find My–style device tracking too.

The TDM are on-ear headphones with no active noise canceling (ANC). That’s something of a rarity these days, with brands eschewing this style in favor of larger, plusher over-ear designs with powerful noise cancellation. See Sony, Bose, Apple, Nothing—you get the idea …

While the TDM don’t have ANC, they do have relatively firm earpads and a tight fit that blocks out noise the old-fashioned way: with noise isolation. Unfortunately, maybe for this very reason, the earpads are not as plush as I’d like, and the whole thing feels tight on my admittedly large skull and less than dainty ears. It’s not eerily quiet like using the best noise-canceling headphones, and I can’t imagine that’s a compromise many users are willing to give up easily.

At 11.9-ounces, the Neo are also heavy for on-ear headphones. It’s no surprise, given that they’ve got four 40-mm drivers inside, but it is noticeable. For comparison, they’re 3 ounces (or 34 percent) heavier than the Sony WH-1000XM6, 1.9 ounces heavier than the more affordable Soundcore Space One Pro, and a full 4.25 ounces heavier than the on-ear Beats Solo 4.

The Neo sound detailed and pleasant, with clear bass and appreciably distinct vocals, but the presentation feels quite boxed in. Music lacks the scale and openness I expect from great headphones, and individual instruments are crowded for space. If they were comfier to wear, I think I’d enjoy the audio a bit more, but I found it all quite tiring, and certainly not on a par with similarly priced alternatives.

They are not awful, but they do feel compromised, and given the price, they also present a bit of a quandary for travel. You gain a Bluetooth speaker at your destination but may spend the journey wishing you'd packed more comfortable headphones with proper ANC.

Just to reiterate, I really like the engineering and execution of the Neo, and I love how small the headphones can fold away. The Bluetooth speaker looks cool too, and it gives the headphones a second function. Sadly, the TDM can’t compete with the best Bluetooth speakers I’ve tested.

There's enough volume for casual listening, but the sound is thin and underwhelming. It keeps pace with the tiny-but-mighty JBL Go 5, but given that it has twice the power and double the drivers, it really should sound better. I also played it alongside my favorite current portable speaker, the $120 JBL Flip 7, and the difference between the two was like racing a speedboat and a paddleboard.

Having a Bluetooth speaker on hand whenever you have your headphones is convenient, and the ability to share a song instantly might appeal, but compact Bluetooth speakers have become so good that the TDM’s subpar audio quality is hard to ignore.

The obvious benefit to the Neo is having just one device to carry and charge. But is that enough to justify the compromises?

The $200 Soundcore Space One Pro are impressive, midrange ANC over-ear headphones that came out on top in WIRED's blind headphone listening test. They're comfortable, adjustable, and foldable, with excellent sound. They’re frequently on sale for $130 or $140, too. Pair them with the $150 but-often-on-sale-for-less JBL Flip 7, and you can get top-tier headphones and a top-tier portable speaker for nearly the same price as the TDM Neo.

Admittedly, there's more to carry, and you lose the instant gratification of twisting your headphones into a speaker. But the Soundcore sound better, are significantly more comfortable, and fold impressively small, while the 1.23-pound JBL Flip 7 is hardly going to weigh you down.

I admire what TDM has achieved. The Neo are clever, entertaining, and a refreshing reminder that innovation doesn't—and shouldn’t—rely solely on stuffing in AI features, adding a fresh colorway, or creating another smartphone app. The mechanism works beautifully, and taking one audio product on the go instead of two has genuine appeal. I just wish I enjoyed listening to them as much as I enjoyed twisting them.

#281. Britain's pound is beating every other major currency this year

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

The British pound crashed to a record low last fall as investors rebelled against budget plans by former Prime Minister Liz Truss. Now, it’s enjoying a comeback.

Sterling hit its highest level against the US dollar in 10 months on Tuesday, topping $1.25 for the first time since June 2022. The pound, which has advanced about 3.3% versus the greenback since the start of 2023, is the best-performing currency among developed economies this year.

The UK currency has been boosted by indications the country’s economy is holding up better than expected. Activity is now thought to have expanded 0.1% in the final three months of last year, up from a previous estimate of no growth at all. Gross domestic product growth in January has been estimated at 0.3% after dropping 0.5% in December.

This resilience is bolstering expectations the Bank of England will maintain aggressive interest rate hikes despite concerns about the health of the global banking sector. Rising rates can boost the domestic currency because they help attract foreign investors searching for higher returns.

Inflation in the United Kingdom also jumped to an annual rate of 10.4% in February, underscoring the need for the Bank of England to maintain its tough approach.

The pound plunged close to $1.03 in September 2022 after the Truss government unveiled plans to boost borrowing while slashing taxes, unleashing panic in financial markets that fueled fears of a recession in the United Kingdom.

The International Monetary Fund predicted in January that the UK economy would contract by 0.6% this year, while all other advanced economies would grow, if only slightly.

“There was a lot of pessimism being priced into the pound,” said Francesco Pesole, a currency strategist at ING.

But the sharp pullback in energy prices and China’s reopening have provided some relief about the economic outlook since the start of the year.

“There was a big re-rating of growth expectations around Europe, and that impacted the UK,” Pesole said.

The euro has also been lifted by these dynamics, rising 2.3% against the US dollar in 2023. The pound’s rally has been sharper in large part because its 2022 declines were more severe, according to Pesole.

Both currencies have been aided by the greenback’s sharp drop from highs reached last September as recession fears have percolated in the United States.

A lack of clarity around the Federal Reserve’s next steps has also restrained the dollar in recent weeks. Investor speculation has increased that the Fed could pause or stop rate hikes due to concerns about the economy following the failure of Silicon Valley Bank last month.

Jordan Rochester, a currency strategist at Nomura, said he thinks the pound could rise to $1.30 this year and “potentially higher.” But he still sees risks given the uncertainty surrounding the Bank of England’s plans and how rate rises will feed back through the country’s economy. And Pesole cautioned that currency fluctuations are often overdone when markets are choppy, as they are now.

“In a volatile market environment, moves are exacerbated,” he said.
