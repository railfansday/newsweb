#4121. Scientists overcome a hidden obstacle to more powerful quantum computers

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Silicon spin qubits could play a major role in the future of quantum computing because they can be produced using the same semiconductor manufacturing methods that make today's computer chips. However, researchers have faced a significant challenge. When multiple silicon qubits are controlled at the same time, their operations tend to become less accurate. Building practical, large quantum computers will require scientists to control many qubits simultaneously while keeping errors to a minimum.

Researchers at the RIKEN Center for Emergent Matter Science and their collaborators have now made important progress toward solving this problem. In a study published in Nature Physics, the team demonstrated highly accurate control of several silicon spin qubits operating simultaneously. Their experiments used an array of five qubits connected to a shared microwave control line. By identifying a major cause of errors during simultaneous operation, the scientists developed a technique to reduce those errors and maintain precision.

Leon Camenzind, a RIKEN research scientist and co-first author who supervised the experiments, explains the significance of the results: "This shows that high-fidelity control can be maintained even when several qubits are operated at the same time and represents an important step toward scaling up silicon-based quantum processors."

Scientists Identify a Hidden Source of Quantum Computing Errors

Unlike conventional computers, which store information as bits representing either 0 or 1, quantum computers use quantum bits, or qubits, that can exist in combinations of both states. Silicon spin qubits store this information using the spin of individual electrons confined within silicon. Performing reliable quantum calculations requires extremely precise control over these electron spins.

Microwave signals provide that control, but operating multiple qubits simultaneously can create unwanted effects that interfere with their accuracy. The researchers traced much of this problem to a phenomenon called the AC Stark shift.

This effect occurs when microwave control signals alter a qubit's effective energy levels, producing changes in the phase of its quantum state. Those phase shifts can disrupt the intended operation of individual qubits, particularly when several are being controlled at once.

To counteract the problem, the team created a calibration method that compensates for these unwanted shifts. Rather than separately calibrating every possible combination of qubits, the technique relies on calibrations performed between pairs of qubits. This makes the approach more practical for systems containing larger numbers of quantum bits.

"Rather than requiring an entirely separate calibration procedure for every possible combination of qubits, our method uses calibrations between pairs of qubits to compensate for the unwanted phase shifts," explains Yi-Hsien Wu, the first author who carried out the experiments as part of his PhD research.

Quantum Qubits Achieve Accuracy Above 99.99%

The researchers combined their calibration technique with an improved device design and carefully adjusted microwave control pulses. Their results showed that exceptionally high accuracy could be maintained even when multiple qubits were operating simultaneously.

When the five qubits were tested individually, each achieved single-qubit gate fidelity well above 99.99%, with the highest approaching 99.999%. Fidelity describes how closely a quantum operation matches its intended result.

The team then tested how well the system performed when several qubits were active together. With as many as three qubits operating simultaneously, fidelity remained above 99.99%. Even when all five qubits were controlled at once, accuracy stayed at approximately 99.9%, approaching the levels needed for practical fault-tolerant quantum computing.

Although the calibration method greatly reduced errors, it did not eliminate them entirely. The researchers found that the remaining loss of accuracy was linked to drive-induced decoherence, an effect caused by the greater microwave power required to operate multiple qubits simultaneously.

Unlike the unwanted phase shifts corrected by the new technique, decoherence involves the loss of the delicate quantum coherence needed for reliable operations.

According to Camenzind, "This is a limitation, but further improvements to device design could help mitigate this source of error."

A New Path Toward Larger Quantum Processors

The findings show that controlling several silicon spin qubits at the same time does not necessarily require a major sacrifice in accuracy. By correcting a key source of errors, the researchers have demonstrated a promising approach for operating more qubits through shared microwave control lines.

This is especially important for the development of large quantum processors. Giving every qubit its own independent control system would make increasingly complex devices difficult to manage. Shared control lines could offer a more practical way to expand these systems while maintaining the precision needed for quantum calculations.

"By addressing a key source of control errors through pairwise calibration," says Camenzind. "Our study supports the use of shared control lines as a practical approach to scaling up quantum processors. Combined with silicon's compatibility with established semiconductor manufacturing, we believe that this advance could help pave the way toward larger quantum processors capable of performing increasingly complex computations."

The broader objective is to develop quantum computers that can reliably perform calculations despite the errors and disturbances that affect quantum systems. Such machines, known as fault-tolerant quantum computers, will require precise, dependable control over large numbers of qubits.

"Our long-term goal is to build a fault-tolerant quantum computer based on silicon spin qubits, and reliable parallel control is an essential step toward that goal," adds Seigo Tarucha, project manager of a silicon quantum computing project under JST's Moonshot Goal 6.

From discovering new books and organizing your reading list to tracking your reading goal and sharing recommendations, book apps make it easier to maintain reading as a hobby. There are numerous apps on the market that promise to do just that, so we’ve compiled a list of some of the best ones to help you pick the right one for you. Whether you’re an occasional reader or a dedicated bookworm, these apps can help you make the most of your reading journey.

Some of the apps on this list are somewhat new, while others have been around for a few years. Amazon-owned Goodreads, which is arguably the most popular book-related app, isn’t included in this list because we’re focusing on newer, lesser-known platforms.

Fable is a reading app that combines book tracking with a social platform. It lets you organize reading lists, set reading goals, track streaks, write reviews, and join virtual book clubs. You also get personalized book recommendations and the chance to discuss what you’re reading with other readers.

The app differs from others on this list because it puts reading and community at the forefront of the platform, which makes it a great option for people who like staying motivated to read by being part of a community and discussing their reads.

Fable is free to download on iOS and Android, but also offers a Fable Plus subscription tier that unlocks additional features like custom goals and advanced analytics for a monthly fee of $5.99.

Bookshelf is a reading app designed to help people organize their libraries, discover new books, and remember more of what they read. The app positions itself as an alternative to Goodreads, and combines traditional reading logs with AI-powered recommendations and tools.

You can create custom shelves, set reading goals, receive reminders, save favorite quotes, and access AI-generated book summaries, key insights, and thematic analyses to recall the books you’ve read and get a deeper understanding of them.

Bookshelf is free to download on iOS, but offers a $6.99 monthly fee to unlock premium features like unlimited collections and enhanced analytics.

Pagebound is a book app that lets readers track their books, create lists, set goals, and discuss individual titles in dedicated forums. Instead of having to join an actual book club group, users can post reactions and thoughts to connect with others reading the same title.

You don’t have to worry about spoilers for the end of the book, as conversations are organized based on reading progress, allowing readers to share theories and thoughts as they read. The app is a great option for people who want to discuss their reads with others without having to commit to a scheduled book club.

Pagebound is free to download on iOS and Android and offers a $9.99 monthly subscription for added features like profile customization.

StoryGraph is a reading app that lets users track their reading, discover new books, and access detailed reading statistics and personalized recommendations. Users can set reading goals, join reading challenges, and find new titles based on their preferred moods, themes, genres, and pacing.

The app is ideal for readers who want to better understand their reading habits and discover books that match their current interests. Its recommendation system doesn’t solely rely on what’s popular or trending, and instead caters to exactly what readers are looking for.

StoryGraph is free to use on iOS and Android, but also offers a paid subscription called StoryGraph Plus that costs $4.99 per month and unlocks custom charts, additional statistics, and more.

Margins is a great option for mood readers, as it offers mood-based discovery alongside a more focused reading experience. It helps users find their next read based on how they feel in the moment by searching for specific vibes, such as “books like 1920s Paris.” Users can also track their reading goals.

The app also lets users customize their virtual library by choosing book covers that match their aesthetic and organizing their shelves in different ways, including by color. #3949. Brazilian stocks rocked as Lula wins presidential election, Bolsonaro reportedly refuses to concede

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva has won an unprecedented fourth term as Brazil's president, overturning a first-round loss to beat Senator Flávio Bolsonaro and catching markets off guard.

Lula took 51.8% of valid votes to Bolsonaro's 48.2%, according to the Superior Electoral Court, with nearly all ballots counted on Sunday.

The results are also historic as it's the first time since Brazil returned to direct elections in 1989 that a candidate has won the final runoff after losing in the first round.

Brazilian stocks plunged following the unexpected victory, unwinding some of the rally from the first-round result three weeks earlier. That reflected expectations of more market-friendly policymaking by Bolsonaro and broader jitters around Brazil's fiscal trajectory.

The iShares MSCI Brazil ETF was 25% lower in premarket at 7:25 a.m. ET. U.S.-listed Brazilian companies were also down sharply in premarket trading. Energy giant Petrobras was down 24%, banking group Itaú Unibanco 26%, brewer Ambev 13%, and mining firm Vale 3.3%. Aircraft manufacturer Embraer was 2.6% lower.

Bolsonaro refused to concede defeat, saying he would seek to have the result annulled, Reuters reported. He also said a ruling by a supreme court justice to extend voting for two hours was "unacceptable," according to the outlet.

The election process was marred by allegations of voter suppression, with Lula's campaign reporting suspended public transportation in some key areas. Supreme Court Justice Cristiano Zanin extended voting by two hours. Bolsonaro's campaign had also requested an extension, citing heavy rains that shut polling stations in the south, a right-wing stronghold.

The victory of the 80-year-old leftist, who had trailed in the first round, blocks a rightward shift in Latin America that U.S. President Donald Trump has backed.

Trump called Bolsonaro's lead in the first round "a very, very big victory."

He would have preferred a Brazilian president in closer alignment to U.S. foreign policy as Washington makes efforts to project U.S. power across the Americas, according to Daniela Portella Sampaio, senior research fellow for Latin American Security at the U.K. think tank Royal United Services Institute.

Lula, who has publicly sparred with Trump, is set to govern from 2027 to 2030, facing a Congress where Bolsonaro's Liberal Party holds the largest bench in decades, and with a substantial public debt that he would need to stabilize.

Under Lula's leadership, Brazil has grappled with worsening fiscal health and mounting public debt driven by double-digit interest rates, elevated cost of servicing the debt, and rigid mandatory spending. Gross debt reached 82.9% of the country's GDP in August, the highest since March 2021, according to the Central Bank of Brazil.

Analysts had expected a rally in Brazilian assets before Sunday's election results, with Bolsonaro holding a narrow lead earlier this month.

Chris Turner, Global Head of Markets and Regional Head of Research for UK & CEE at ING, had forecast both the currency and bond markets would rally if Bolsonaro won.

Lula will need to convince investors that Brazil can stabilize its debt trajectory, said Alejo Czerwonko, a fellow at the Atlantic Council. Lula's administration will likely extend the more "gradual approach," building on the existing fiscal framework while seeking to preserve public investment and social spending, Czerwonko said.

Goldman Sachs analysts expect "modest measures" to contain the government's spending growth, alongside further increases in an already high tax burden, while major structural reforms remain unlikely.

Leaders from across Latin America largely congratulated Lula's historic win.

Peruvian President Keiko Fujimori hailed Lula's victory in a post on X late Sunday, calling it a "democratic day" for the Brazilian people. Uruguay President Yamandú Orsi congratulated Lula over a call, calling the Brazilian president a "friend."

— CNBC's Matthew Tan contributed to this report.

#4194. Hunting Red Leaves with Satellites

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

A modern satellite makes it possible to pursue the ancient tradition of leaf-peeping in a whole new way.

The Japanese tradition of momijigari, or "red leaf hunting," stretches back more than a millennium, as evidenced by ancient poems describing journeys to admire fall foliage. In America, mentions of foliage trips began appearing in the historical record in the 19th century, around the time artists and poets such as Frederic Church and Emily Dickinson celebrated autumn color in depictions of the Northeast.

Modern-day leaf peepers have a new way to track and savor the seasonal phenomenon. The hyperspectral OCI (Ocean Color Instrument) on NASA's PACE (Plankton, Aerosol, Cloud, ocean Ecosystem) satellite observes the planet's oceans and vegetation daily at 286 spectral bands. Among this array are a few groups of wavelengths that allow scientists to track subtle changes in leaf pigments key to fall foliage displays, including anthocyanins (reds), carotenoids (yellows and oranges), and chlorophylls (greens).

NASA scientists can track fall color by measuring how much red, orange, and yellow forests show compared with green. Tracking the ratio over time reveals where leaves are changing and when color peaks. The maps above show vegetation "redness" from September 29 to October 4 (left) and October 5 to 11 (right). Areas shown in green have values near 1, meaning the satellite detects about as much red as green, and most leaves have not yet turned. Areas shown in light yellow and red have higher values, indicating progressively more red pigment relative to green.

The satellite detected particularly high levels of redness in parts of northern and eastern Russia, northern Alaska, and northern Canada from September 29 to October 4. In New England and upstate New York, redness began to fade during the week of October 5 to 11, even as it spread across much of the Midwest and the Mid-Atlantic. (Not all the yellows and reds on the maps represent fall colors. Crops in some agricultural areas, for instance, show high anthocyanin levels as they age and after they are harvested. Also, in some regions, clouds produce what appear to be gaps in the vegetation.)

Many of the reddest areas—such as the Adirondacks in New York, the Green Mountains in Vermont, and the White Mountains in New Hampshire—have large numbers of sugar maples (Acer saccharum) and red maples (Acer rubrum), deciduous trees that turn a brilliant shade of crimson in the fall. Blueberries, bilberries, sedges, mosses, and grasses likely contribute to the intense reds detected in marshy tundra areas in northern Alaska, Canada, and Russia.

The OLI (Operational Land Imager) on Landsat 9 captured an image (above) of intensely red foliage around Ripton, Vermont, on October 3, 2026, around the peak of redness in the region. Poet Robert Frost, author of "October," "The Road Not Taken," "Nothing Gold Can Stay," and several other poems that evoke fall foliage, kept a summer home and farm in Ripton and taught at Middlebury's Bread Loaf School of English.

Some other satellites with hyperspectral sensors can measure fall foliage pigments in a way that is similar to PACE, but only in more localized areas and less frequently. PACE is the first satellite capable of measuring all three key leaf pigments—anthocyanins, carotenoids, and chlorophylls—across the entire planet every day or two.

"It's the ability to collect time-series data of anthocyanins and carotenoids everywhere on Earth repeatedly that is game-changing," said Karl Huemmrich, a vegetation scientist with the Goddard Earth Sciences Technology and Research II center at the University of Maryland, Baltimore County. Huemmrich, who is also a member of the PACE science team, was eager to work with the satellite because of OCI's broad swath and short revisit time. "The time-series data allow us to determine peak redness and yellowness anywhere on Earth," he said.

In the fall, leaves change colors as they lose chlorophyll, a green molecule that plants use to synthesize food. Since chlorophyll is not a stable compound, plants must continuously synthesize it, a process that requires ample sunlight and warm temperatures. As temperatures decrease and days shorten in autumn, levels of chlorophyll drop, causing greens to fade and presenting an opportunity for carotenoids and anthocyanins to show their colors.

As part of the ongoing North American Phenology Campaign run by the Global Learning and Observations to Benefit the Environment (GLOBE) program, NASA data scientist Skye Caplan has been posting weekly foliage maps of vegetation redness. The maps show waves of red emerging in the high latitudes in August and shifting equatorward in September and October. The PACE team is asking the public to submit photos of fall foliage to help scientists "ground truth" the satellite observations and better understand how various factors influence vegetation color.

"Changes in pigments can also occur when vegetation is stressed due to drought or insect damage," said Caplan. She noted that changes like these likely help explain why PACE measures high levels of redness in agricultural regions of Brazil, Australia, and Canada that don't track closely with broader seasonal changes.

"The more photos we can get from the ground, the better," Caplan said. Ground photos help scientists refine PACE's observations of vegetation color, which the team hopes will eventually support several lines of research. These include how the timing of spring greening and fall senescence is changing, how vegetation changes affect the global carbon cycle, and how leaf color influences wildfire risk. Scientists also want to know whether PACE can detect early warning signs of drought, insect infestations, and plant disease.

People can participate in the GLOBE campaign even if they live in an area without dense forests or picturesque fall foliage displays. Scientists also want ground observations of shrubs, grasses, wildflowers, evergreens, crops, and other vegetation. But when it comes to "red leaf hunting," don't delay, Caplan said. "Color changes happen kind of slowly, then all at once, and then they're gone."

The Revolutionary Origins of Language Chris Knight & Jerome Lewis Yale Univ. Press (2026)

The origins of writing and script remain unclear, but those of speech and language are simply unknown. This is because no physical evidence remains of the earliest language, which was probably spoken in Africa, the birthplace of Homo sapiens. By contrast, inscribed marks on clay tablets that constitute the earliest known form of writing — however mysterious — have survived from ancient Mesopotamia, dating to the fourth millennium bc.

Humanity spoke thousands of languages at the dawn of farming — now many of them are lost

Humanity spoke thousands of languages at the dawn of farming — now many of them are lost

That’s left a lot of room for scholars to speculate on how speech and language began, starting with thinkers from the ancient world, including Plato, who could not decide whether language had a natural or a purely human origin. By the nineteenth century, anthropologists had grown tired of so much talk with scant evidence to go on.

In 1866, the founding statutes of the Paris Linguistic Society forbade all discussion of the subject, note anthropologists Chris Knight and Jerome Lewis in The Revolutionary Origins of Language. “Anyone theorizing about the origins of language risked being dismissed as a crank.” Even so, “from time to time, this or that brave maverick would risk whatever reputation they had by attempting to reopen the debate”.

The best known, if controversial, attempts since then come from linguist Noam Chomsky, starting in the 1960s. The authors discuss his contributions at length, drawing on Knight’s book Decoding Chomsky (2016).

How the world’s largest language family spread — and why others go extinct

How the world’s largest language family spread — and why others go extinct

In 1987, Chomsky remarked in Language and Problems of Knowledge that: “There is a long history of study of the origin of language, asking how it came from the calls of apes and so forth.” But, in his view, this investigation is a “complete waste of time”.

That’s because language is based on “an entirely different principle than any animal communication system”, he wrote. Although human gestures might have evolved from animal communication systems, the same could not apply to human language, Chomsky thought.

This view was supported by an academic study, launched in 1973, of a young chimpanzee (Pan troglodytes) with the punning name Nim Chimpsky. Nim learnt from his human supervisors how to make dozens of hand signs to procure food and other rewards, but not how to communicate in American Sign Language or to speak a language. “Give orange me give eat orange me eat orange give me eat orange give me you” was Nim’s longest recorded sign-language sequence, as cited by Knight and Lewis.

Nim Chimpsky the chimpanzee was taught how to make hand signs for food and other rewards.Credit: Mel Finkelstein/NY Daily News Archive/Getty

In Chomsky’s view, human language arose suddenly because of a single genetic mutation, not from Darwinian natural selection or through a gradual, adaptive cultural process. In other words, argue the authors, by declaring language to be “an especially intriguing and mysterious aspect of the natural world, to be investigated using the normal methods of natural science”, Chomsky elevated the study of linguistics to a scientific challenge in the field of anthropology.

However, they add, “there is no measuring device, magnifying glass, telescope or other instrument which can grasp the meaning of a word.” So how can conventional science investigate language?

Rejecting Chomsky’s view, Knight and Lewis instead offer a complex mixture of evidence, both scientific and cultural, to support their own theory. This evidence is mostly drawn from prehistoric and present-day Africa, where Lewis spent three decades studying hunter-gatherers, notably the BaYaka people in Central Africa.

“To qualify as science, a hypothesis must suggest testable predictions,” the authors remark. Given that there can be no direct evidence for events in the distant past, scientists must therefore “construct tests from a wide range of telltale clues”. These include: “recurrent structures of magical myth, initiation rituals, hunters’ taboos, patterns of menstrual prohibition, lunar and solar influences, and critically, archaeological evidence for symbolism in the form of red ochre pigments and personal ornaments”. All of these clues are examined in their intriguing, if rambling, book.

Although anthropologists have often suggested that speech and language arose from hunting or warfare, Knight and Lewis refute these two theories. While hunting in the forest, male hunter-gatherers “do their utmost to avoid having to speak aloud”, so as to lure animals towards them. #3361. Roger Goodell reveals plan for new NFL international series host city

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

According to NFL commissioner Roger Goodell, the league has its sights set on Japan as a host country for a regular-season game in 2027 or 2028.

Goodell broke the news on the "NFL Daily" podcast on Friday, Oct. 23 – two days before the Pittsburgh Steelers and New Orleans Saints play the NFL's first international series game in France.

"Listen, Tokyo is clearly on our list, whether it's next year or the year after," Goodell said. "It's coming at some point in the near future."

Under Goodell's leadership, the NFL has expanded its international reach further than ever. In addition to at least three games per year in London, the league has tabbed Germany, Brazil and Australia as host countries for their international series in recent years.

NFL INTERNATIONAL SERIES: Trump questions NFL decision to play Steelers vs. Saints game in Paris

Looking ahead to 2027, Goodell pointed to the success of the Rams-49ers Week 1 clash in Melbourne, Australia this year – the first-ever NFL regular-season game Down Under – as a reason that a game in Tokyo could be successful.

"On a Friday morning at 10:30 (a.m.), to have 100,000 people in the stadium, it was just an incredible feeling for us. I think it's opened up new opportunities for us," Goodell said. "The travel is something we think we can manage and do it without impacting negatively on the teams. So, each of these we learned something from and we benefit and we also open those other doors.

"We're planning on playing global. That's where we're going."

This year, the NFL will put on nine regular-season games in front of international audiences – the most the league has ever played abroad in a single season.

At the league owners' meeting on Tuesday, Oct. 20, NFL vice president of club business, international and league events Peter O'Reilly said the league is considering a schedule that includes 10 international games next year. That would be the maximum number of international games allowed under the current collective bargaining agreement between the NFL and NFL Players Association.

Ten years later, the NFL began its "American Bowl" series of international exhibition games, a predecessor to its current international series. The league revisited the island nation more regularly for American Bowl games before it abandoned the international preseason game schedule in the early 2000s.

The last NFL game to take place in Japan was a preseason game between the Falcons and Colts in 2005.
