#158. Beijing promised to 'fight back' over Taiwan leader's US visit. But this time it has more to lose

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

An anticipated meeting between Taiwan’s President Tsai Ing-wen and US House Speaker Kevin McCarthy in California this week has sparked concerns of a repeat of the pressure campaign China launched last year when then-Speaker Nancy Pelosi visited Taipei.

At that time, Beijing encircled the island democracy with unprecedented military drills – firing multiple missiles into its surrounding waters and sending dozens of warplanes speeding across a sensitive median line dividing the Taiwan Strait.

It also cut off contact with the United States over a number of issues from military matters to combating climate change, in retaliation for what it viewed as a violation of its sovereignty.

This time, Beijing has already threatened to “resolutely fight back” if a Tsai-McCarthy meeting goes ahead.

It also slammed Washington for allowing Tsai to stopover in the US while en route to and from official visits in Central America, warning it could lead to “serious” confrontation between the two powers.

A defiant Tsai staked out her own ground, pledging as she took off on her 10-day trip not to let “external pressure” stop Taiwan from connecting with the world and like-minded democracies.

But the optics of the meeting, taking place in California and not Taiwan, and its timing – at a particularly thorny moment in China’s foreign relations and ahead of a presidential election in Taiwan that could reset the tone of its relationship with Beijing – may see Beijing tread more carefully this time, or at least not escalate further, analysts say.

“This puts the burden on China not to overreact, because any overreaction is only going to push China further away from the world,” said Yun Sun, director of the China Program at the Stimson Center think tank in Washington.

That doesn’t mean, however, that Beijing won’t be closely watching Tsai’s movements as it calibrates its response – and decides how much military might to flex over her meeting with an American lawmaker on American soil.

The opacity of China’s system – and the potential for competing interests within its vast bureaucracy – also make it difficult to accurately predict its response.

“Every time Taiwan does anything that China doesn’t like, the Chinese react with their own military coercion,” Sun said. But in the current situation, “they have to consider the consequences of overreaction,” she added.

The expected meeting, which McCarthy’s office announced earlier this week would take place on Wednesday, also comes at a precarious moment in US-China relations.

Washington and Beijing are struggling to stabilize their communication amid flaring tensions over issues from a downed suspected Chinese surveillance balloon to semiconductor supply chains – raising the stakes of potential damage to that relationship if Beijing lashes out as it did when Tsai met Pelosi.

Taiwan is still feeling the fallout of that response last August, with Chinese military forces now regularly making incursions over what had previously been an informal but largely respected border of control between Beijing and Taipei in the Taiwan Strait. Taiwan’s official Central News Agency also reported Monday that Tsai would meet with McCarthy, citing Tsai’s presidential office.

But a meeting between Tsai and the leader of the Republican majority in the US House of Representatives, who ranks second in line to the Presidency, would mark another symbolic moment for Taiwan and the US, which only maintain unofficial ties.

For Tsai, who is entering the final year of her two-term presidency, “it’s clearly a capstone event,” according to Wen-Ti Sung, a political scientist at the Australian National University’s Taiwan Studies Program. “She has this image as the Taiwanese president who has taken US-Taiwan relations to new heights, and who … has been able to give Taiwan almost unprecedented international visibility,” he said.

That increased visibility – and enhanced cooperation with the US – has followed China’s mounting pressure on the island, which sits fewer than 110 miles (177 kilometers) from the mainland coast.

China’s Communist Party claims the self-governing island democracy as its own despite never having controlled it, and has vowed to take the island, by force if necessary.

China thinks it's diplomatically isolating Taiwan. It isn't

The party has undertaken a sweeping expansion of its military capabilities over the past decade under leader Xi Jinping – and ramped up its pervasive economic, diplomatic and military pressure on Taiwan.

That’s driven concerns, among some in Washington, that Beijing is preparing for an invasion, though China’s official language still suggests that scenario is not its preferred option for achieving the claimed goal of “reunification.”

It is those pressures – and how to support Taiwan against unilateral actions by Beijing – that are likely to be on the table when Tsai, McCarthy and a bipartisan group of US lawmakers sit down on Wednesday.

Congress has been a pillar of increasing American support for Taiwan in recent years. Lawmakers regularly visit the island and drive bipartisan legislation enhancing support and cooperation.

While the US switched its diplomatic relations to Beijing decades ago, it maintains unofficial ties with Taiwan and is bound by law to provide the democratic island with the means to defend itself.

Under Washington’s longstanding “One China” policy, the US acknowledges China’s position that Taiwan is part of China, but has never officially recognized Beijing’s claim to the island of 23 million.

Though McCarthy does not have Pelosi’s decades-long record of advocacy regarding China, the California Republican is now a leading voice pushing for closer scrutiny of Beijing, and meeting Tsai could help him to burnish that image.

Last month, McCarthy told reporters that meeting Tsai in the US would not impact whether he travels to Taiwan in the future – something he had earlier said he wanted to do.

A meeting in California, on US soil, is widely seen as less likely to provoke Beijing than a McCarthy visit to Taiwan.

Pelosi’s trip – the first from a lawmaker of that rank to the island in 25 years – generated a fever pitch of nationalist and anti-US rhetoric in mainland China.

This time, so far, domestic conversation in China’s heavily controlled media sphere has been significantly muted.

But the stakes remain high – including for Beijing itself – over how it responds, analysts say.

As Taiwan prepares for a presidential election in January, a fierce response could push voters away from Taiwan’s main opposition party Kuomintang (KMT), widely seen as more friendly toward Beijing.

It could also jar with another high profile trip happening now: a tour of mainland China from former Taiwan president and senior KMT member Ma Ying-jeou, the first visit from a current or former Taiwan leader since the end of the Chinese Civil War in 1949.

Ma’s tour is a “once in a half a century opportunity to send a conciliatory message between the two sides, Beijing shouldn’t want to tank that,” said Sung, the political scientist.

What you need to know about China-Taiwan tensions

China is also acutely aware that its actions toward Taiwan are under a significantly brighter global spotlight following the invasion of Ukraine by Russian President Vladimir Putin, a close diplomatic partner of Xi. Putin’s rhetoric over Ukraine has echoes of how Xi speaks of Taiwan.

Beijing has recently sought to position itself as an agent of peace in that conflict – especially as it aims to repair frayed ties with Europe.

This week, as Tsai is expected to meet with McCarthy, French President Emmanuel Macron and European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen will head to China – an important opportunity that Xi may not want to overshadow with military posturing.

An aggressive response also risks stoking confrontation with the US, not yet six months after Xi and US President Joe Biden called to enhance communication during a face-to-face meeting in Bali.

“(A less overtly aggressive response) would imply that Beijing does not wish to escalate tension with the US to a level that can risk getting out of hand,” said Steve Tsang, director of the SOAS China Institute in London.

“A reset in US-China ties is not on the agenda, but an easing of tension is not beyond the realm of possibility.”

#154. The secret to fast startup growth isn’t early sales, top names in tech say

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

The AI era has changed how fast startups can grow. Startups can grow so fast that they hit high annual recurring revenue (ARR) numbers, only to crash and burn a few months later. At this year’s TechCrunch Disrupt, Lovable go-to-market lead Luke Arno, Ben Broca of the AI platform Polsia, and Theory Ventures’ Tomasz Tunguz gave founders some tips on how to set their companies up for success in those first 90 days.

Broca, whose company Polsia uses AI agents to build and run businesses with almost no human staff (Broca says he is its only employee), said that product-market fit is one of the hardest things to find right now, especially in an era when so many products can easily be copied by competitors. In response, Arno said that durable go-to-market strategies are those that can be reliably repeated.

“The most critical thing for founders or earlier-stage companies to do is develop learning density or pattern recognition for where your customers are,” Arno said. “Don’t rush to build out a top-down go-to-market motion if you’re not there yet.”

That’s why, in those first 90 days after launching a commercial product that’s ready to sell, founders should not mistake early, fast revenue for a sign that the startup is ready to scale, Arno said.

Once a startup finds use cases that work, it will only be ready to build out a sales team and go for growth after it can attract new customers by repeating what has worked. If every sale requires new tactics, the startup hasn’t truly hit product-market fit.

“If I bring on 10 new customers in 10 different ways to solve 10 different problems, sure, I brought revenue in, but I don’t have a way to go scale a go-to-market,” Arno said. “If I bring three customers in with a similar motion to solve a similar problem, now I have high confidence that I can go scale.”

Lovable, the AI app-building startup, set a lot of those early benchmarks with how fast it grew, and now founders everywhere are feeling the pressure to do the same. Lovable is valued at more than $13 billion by its investors, says its ARR has passed $600 million, and has raised more than $700 million in funding to date.

“The expectations for overall execution speed are absolutely tremendous,” Tunguz said.

But Tunguz said growth like Lovable’s is still the exception, though founders should still move fast. The pressure they feel is real, he said, because far more money flows through the startup ecosystem at every stage than when he began investing in 2008. Today, a seed deal brought in by one of his partners could just as easily be a $5 million raise, a $50 million raise, or a $500 million raise.

The winners all focus on one thing: understanding their customers well enough to know what will keep them around even after any hype subsides.

“Community building is very important and being close to customers is very important,” Broca said. He said founders can send emails and throw events, and he personally likes to talk to a lot of his customers: “The general rule is always to be talking to customers.”

#149. Sri Lanka: Death of iconic elephant raises habitat concerns

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

The death of what was thought to be Sri Lanka’s largest elephant has drawn attention to human-elephant conflict in rural communities. The island country sees a high number of deadly encounters.

The recent death of an iconic male elephant in Sri Lanka has raised concerns over ongoing contact between humans and the giant animals, whose habitats have been greatly reduced.

The elephant known as "Kavantissa" had been considered to be the tallest living male elephant in the Sri Lanka. Thought to be around 45 years old and measuring roughly 3.3 meters tall (11 feet), he roamed Sri Lanka's northwest near the village of Galgamuwa, and was widely admired for his majestic stature.

In late September, villagers noticed the 5.5 ton-tusker (5,500 kg) was limping, and a closer look reveled a gunshot wound on his right leg. Despite the intervention of veterinary surgeons, the elephant died on October 16.

Sri Lanka records the highest number of elephant deaths annually and the second highest number of human deaths globally from violent interactions between elephants and humans, which usually happen when crop raiding elephants encroach on farmlands.

According to official statistics, since 2019, an average of 370 elephants and 125 humans have died in human-elephant conflict incidents each year.

The situation affects the natural habitats of elephants and the livelihoods of rural communities.

Home to about 10% of the global Asian elephant population and occupying about 2% of their total natural range, Sri Lanka has the highest density of Asian elephants in the world.

The Sri Lankan elephant is an endangered subspecies in the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List of Threatened Species, and protected under Sri Lankan law, with punishment by fines and jail terms for wildlife-related offences.

During the past few decades, forest cover in Sri Lanka has shrunk as the population has grown.

Statistics show the rural population of Sri Lanka increased from 8.25 million in 1960 to 17.8 million in 2020.

Agriculture remains the primary source of income for these rural communities. As a result, the land is cleared for agricultural activities.

According to Department of Wildlife Conservation (DWC) statistics, in 2024, an estimated 7,451 wild elephants were recorded in Sri Lanka. About 70% of these wild elephants live outside protected areas.

Research shows elephants have a massive daily dietary requirement of about 150 kilograms (330 lbs.) of green matter, which drives them into farmland.

"Driven primarily by habitat loss, agricultural expansion, fragmentation of elephant corridors, and increasing competition for land and resources, human-elephant conflict continues to cause hundreds of elephant deaths and more than 100 human fatalities annually," said Ravi Corea, President of Sri Lanka Wildlife Conservation Society, a Sri Lankan non-profit conservation organization.

The conservationist told DW that rural communities are severely affected, and the agricultural losses caused by elephants can threaten a family's primary source of food and income.

"This distinguishes human-elephant conflict from many human-wildlife conflicts in economically developed countries, where wildlife encounters, although sometimes serious, generally do not threaten household subsistence to the same extent," he said.

"Despite the efforts by the government to mitigate HEC, the numbers are rising demonstrating the continuing severity of the crisis," Corea added.

Elephants are shot, poisoned, or killed by improvised explosives hidden in food and train collisions, experts say.

In view of the escalation of human-elephant conflict, the Sri Lankan government in 2020 drafted a national action plan to mitigate the problem.

"The 2020 Action Plan provides a valuable foundation for reducing human–elephant conflict, but its recommendations must be strengthened through a comprehensive national conservation strategy with enforceable habitat protection at its core,"  Corea said.

"The ultimate measure of success must be not merely a reduction in human–elephant conflict, but the permanent protection of ecologically connected habitats, the survival of viable wild elephant populations and the ability of people and elephants to coexist sustainably for generations to come," he added.

Meanwhile, community-based programs by non-government organizations have proven effective and sustainable. These initiatives are complemented by elephant monitoring, scientific research, habitat assessments, community education, and the development of locally appropriate deterrents, Corea said

"Our flagship initiative Project Orange Elephant in Sri Lanka's Wasgamuwa region promotes the cultivation of citrus which elephants do not like to eat as a trophic deterrence. This reduces crop raiding while generating alternative income for farmers," Corea added, pointing to things like electric fencing protecting settlements and agricultural lands while encouraging local responsibility.

Another issue is confrontations on elephant corridors, that in some cases have discouraged children from walking to school.

Elephant corridors are designated strips of land that connect larger forest and habitat patches, allowing elephants to move safely between them for food, water, breeding, and seasonal migration.

To address this, the "EleFriendly" bus service in Wasgamuwa, provides safe transportation for schoolchildren through elephant corridors and has reduced dangerous human-elephant encounters along its original route by over 90%.

"An ethically just solution must protect human lives, livelihoods, and elephant populations simultaneously, said Kamal Shashendra, a Sri Lanka-based environmental ethics researcher.

He told DW that this required scientifically informed land-use planning, protection of elephant corridors, appropriate community-based fencing, effective early-warning systems, and fair compensation for affected families.

#147. Jim Chalmers faces a world of hard choices where the solution to one problem makes the other worse

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

High inflation, low growth and rising unemployment can be added to the federal treasurer’s wall of worries

Get our new political email, free app or daily news podcast

Jim Chalmers in a speech last week offered the scant encouragement that the economy would be “a bit better” in a year’s time.

Fast forward to Friday morning, and the treasurer revealed that his officials were busy downgrading their forecasts ahead of December’s mid-year budget update to show slower growth and higher unemployment.

In other words, things are getting worse, not better.

Overnight, a broadening Middle East conflict pushed the global benchmark oil price to US$113, and within sight of its April high of nearly US$120.

Motorists are paying 60 cents more for a litre of petrol prices than they were before the US and Israel attacked Iran. That’s an extra $36 to fill up a 60-litre tank. Do that once a week and a family’s monthly fuel bill is $144 higher than in February.

The New York Times overnight reported that the US and Israel have drawn up plans to restart attacks on Iran after the upcoming American midterm elections.

Westpac analysts have sketched out an “escalation” scenario for the Middle East conflict where oil climbs towards US$150, echoing warnings from the International Monetary Fund earlier this month.

That worst-case scenario would have huge implications for inflation and interest rates, even as we brace for a potential fifth interest rate hike on 3 November.

Meanwhile, the one bright spot that has helped paper over many of the cracks in the global economy – the huge spending associated with artificial intelligence – has hit some serious wobbles.

Investors are worried about the huge surge in debt among companies racing to build the infrastructure for an AI future – the trigger for another steep sell-off on Wall Street overnight.

Community opposition to the massive datacentre build-out could stall the headlong rush towards an AI future. That is likely a good thing in the longer term, but in the short term is another stress to add to the wall of worries. Rising chatter of a new tech stock bubble is naturally accompanied by talk of an imminent bust.

With all this going on, no wonder that the treasurer can only offer the thinnest of gruel for Australians hungry for any evidence of an end to years of disruption, and of wages that can’t keep up with high and rising prices.

High inflation, low growth and rising unemployment is a central banker’s “nightmare”, as the Reserve Bank’s Andrew Hauser has said.

It’s also a nightmare for a federal treasurer who is increasingly unpopular and desperate for a win in the court of public opinion.

“The pressures on people right now are very substantial and obviously that keeps me awake at night,” Chalmers told Politico.

“And there’s a number of reasons for that, some of them are outside our control, and some of them we have some influence over. You know, providing some cost-of-living help, managing the budget in a responsible way,” he said.

“Those are the things that we can do, but in lots of ways we’re hostage as well to decisions taken in DC or Tehran or in Eastern Europe about two wars which are pushing up inflation around the world and weighing on growth around the world.”

The high level of government spending is not helping the inflation fight, by any means – something the opposition is keen to point out at every opportunity.

Productivity growth is, if not dead, then resting, and shows no signs of springing back to its perch. That means more inflation at lower rates of growth as the economy hits its speed limits sooner.

But Chalmers is right when he says there’s a lot that is out of the government’s control.

His options to alleviate voter discontent are limited, which is why he frequently falls back on platitudes and lines to the effect of “you think we have it bad? You should see other economies.”

Sure, the government can lower petrol prices through another temporary cut to the fuel excise. But the billions of dollars that pumps into motorists’ pockets makes it harder for the RBA to bring inflation back under control.

Labor could go the other way and announce plans for big spending cuts in the December mid-year economic and fiscal outlook.

That would help the central bank’s job, but would drag on an already weak economy and push unemployment higher – would they win any plaudits for that?

This is Hauser’s “world of hard choices” – a world where the solution to one problem makes the other worse. A world with which Australians are becoming all too familiar.

#145. Arc browser sunsets December 22 as The Browser Company doubles down on Dia

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

The Browser Company is sunsetting its design-forward Arc browser on December 22, 2026, and directing users to Dia, its AI-first browser. The company announced the decision on Thursday, citing the rising cost of keeping a browser secure in the age of AI.

“Keeping a browser secure takes constant, dedicated work that is only increasing in the age of AI,” the company wrote.

“Dia was built from the ground up to meet today’s security standards, so that’s where our engineering and security teams will focus moving forward.”

The wind-down is staged into three parts. As of Thursday, new Arc accounts can no longer be created. On November 23, Arc Search on iOS and Android will lose its AI features, including “Browse for Me.” Users will no longer be able to sign in or sync tabs, though the app will continue functioning as a basic local browser.

On December 22, Arc will stop working entirely on Mac and Windows. Accounts will be deactivated, and personal data will be deleted within 30 days. Arc Search will also be removed from the Google Play Store.

While The Browser Company claims Dia now surpasses Arc in speed, stability, and security benchmarks, user reactions on social media were largely disappointed, with many reviving requests for the company to make Arc open source.

But CEO Josh Miller previously pointed out that Arc is built on the Arc Development Kit (ADK) — a proprietary internal SDK that is also the core engine powering Dia. Open-sourcing Arc would mean giving away Dia’s intellectual property.

“While we’d love to open-source Arc someday, we can’t do that meaningfully without also open-sourcing ADK,” Miller previously noted. “And ADK is still core to our company’s value.”

For users making the switch, Dia will carry over equivalents of several core Arc features. Users can still swipe between different work and personal environments using Dia’s “Profiles” (formerly Arc’s Spaces). Arc’s signature vertical tabs will remain as an optional sidebar layout, while Folders have been rebranded as “Pinned Tab Groups.”

Passwords, bookmarks, extensions, and history will seamlessly transfer to Dia. Arc features will remain free in Dia, with AI optional.

The shutdown officially closes a chapter that began in 2025, when the startup stopped active feature development on Arc. The Browser Company — which was acquired by Atlassian for $610 million in 2025 — conceded at the time that while early adopters loved the browser’s novel interface, the “novelty tax” and complexity limited mainstream adoption. Arc was fiercely beloved by a dedicated group of enthusiasts, but it never quite became the mass-market Chrome alternative its makers once envisioned.

#141. France: 1 killed, 4 wounded after shooting near Paris

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

French police arrested a suspect after gunshots were fired on the outskirts of Paris in what President Macron described as a racist and terrorist attack.

One person was killed and four others were injured when gunshots were fired from a vehicle outside Paris on Friday, according to local authorities.

Interior Minister ⁠Laurent ​Nunez said the shooting was "clearly a terrorist crime."

He said shots were fired at the total of three locations in the towns of Trappes and La Verriere, which are located about 20 kilometers (13 miles) west of the French capital, close to Versailles. The "rampage" lasted for 15 minutes, according to the minister.

Nunez said the shooter fired "what appears to have been a military-grade weapon, or at least an automatic weapon."

Three of the wounded remain in critical condition.

Commenting on the shooting, President Emmanuel Macron said the attack "marks a ⁠turning ​point" for France.

"What ‌happened today was a terrorist ​and ⁠racist attack," he told reporters, noting that the victims were targeted due to their religion and the color of their skin.

Shortly after the Friday attack, authorities detained a man in the neighboring town of Coignieres and took him into police custody.

The authorities later identified the suspect as Kevin T., born in 1987. They said the alleged shooter already had a criminal record.

France's anti-terrorism prosecutor said the authorities found a Sten Gun — a British-made WWII-era submachine gun — and a tear gas grenade in his car.  The alleged shooter had also booby-trapped his apartment, according to the authorities.

"An individual fired shots from his vehicle at multiple people along his route," the mayor of Trappes, Ali Rabeh, wrote on social media.

He later told French broadcaster BFM TV that one of the locations at which shots were fired was a playground.

"It is a miracle that there were not more victims," he said.

Rabeh said the person killed was of African origin, while one of those critically wounded was of North African origin.

The mayor also told reporters the attack had "racist, likely Islamophobic, overtones."

“This is probably one of the first attempted mass killings motivated by Islamophobia and racism,” he said.

Aurore Berge, the French minister in charge of gender equality and antidiscrimination, said a 12-year-old girl was very seriously injured in the incident.

"If a racist motive is established, I hope that the whole nation will stand alongside the residents of Trappes, La Verriere, and the victims. We cannot tolerate even the slightest racist remark in the French republic," she said.

French Prime Minister Sebastien Lecornu called the incident "very serious" and said that "all available resources of the security forces and judicial authorities have been fully mobilized."

He added that France's National Anti-Terrorism Prosecutor's Office (Pnat) is monitoring the situation.

Pnat itself said it had opened an investigation into "murder in connection with a terrorist enterprise, with the aggravating circumstance that the acts were committed on the basis of the victim's actual or perceived membership of a specific race, ethnicity, nation or religion."

The shooting came as France braces for yet another day of protests against the high cost of living on Saturday, with major museums in Paris and other major cities closing their doors and the government deploying a significantly larger security presence.

"We are not talking about ​a nice protest," Interior Minister Nunez told reporters, announcing the deployment of 70,000 security personnel. "We are ​ready ⁠tomorrow to be extremely firm ⁠and ​not tolerate anything."

France has also seen a spate of Islamist terrorist attacks in recent years and, although the number of incidents has fallen, authorities have warned that risks remain.

The town of Trappes has experienced its own share of tension, with a local teacher reporting receiving threats after saying that the state did not do enough to protect Samuel Paty, a teacher in another Paris suburb who was beheaded by a radical Islamist in 2020 after he showed his class caricatures of the Prophet Mohammed.

There was no immediate suggestion that Friday's shooting was linked either to the current protests or Islamist terrorism.
