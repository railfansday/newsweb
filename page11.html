#3669. The Tragedy of Having a Wikipedia Page—and Then Losing It

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

What do you need to know—need to know—about Xavier Trudeau? The eldest son of former Canadian prime minister Justin, the teenage Trudeau has recently begun performing R&B under the mononym Xav. But, with all due respect to Xav, do those facts make him a person of note? Should you expect to be able to read about him on Wikipedia? What about Evanescence guitarist Tim McCord, or 2012 Yachtmaster of the Year Rainer Holtorff?

All three have had a biography on Wikipedia; all three of those biographies were considered for deletion this year on the grounds that the subjects were not notable enough to warrant a biography at all. Every day, dozens, sometimes hundreds, of Wikipedia biographies—of child actors turned LA realtors, retired ambassadors, soccer players who have played less than 10 minutes of professional soccer—are purged from the world’s free encyclopedia.

It’s not as though Wikipedia, which hosts over 7 million articles in the English language, doesn’t have the space. But Wikipedia has only so many volunteers to monitor its contents. To this day, Wikipedia editors are discovering hoaxes that have gone undetected for decades. The time it takes to verify the release dates of Xav’s three singles is better spent—the logic goes—by asking whether the American Civil War officer Nathaniel Lyon really drank mustard during battle, as was claimed in his Wikipedia biography for 17 years. Wikipedia removed the mustard claim, not because it was proven wrong, but because there was no reliable evidence for it. “We’re not really looking for what’s true,” Britta Gustafson, who has been editing Wikipedia since 2001, told me. “We’re looking for what’s verifiable.”

Five hauntings from the other side of technology.

Wikipedia editors do not themselves decide that a person is not notable, only that there is a lack of reliable evidence to support the claim. One person who recently lost her Wikipedia biography on notability grounds is Kimberly Small, the CEO of a Nevada nonprofit that supports survivors of sexual assault. The debate occurred in a public page on Wikipedia itself; it’s in that space that the really tricky decisions about what is and what isn’t good enough for the encyclopedia are made. One editor raised a question: “How should we judge not-for-profit executives? By the nature of their job, the focus is, as it should be, on the organization and the work the organization does, not them.” Another editor replied: “I don’t think there is anything wrong with not-for-profit executives being less often individually notable. Wikipedia coverage is not a prize.”

Is it not? “Having an article says something,” Gustafson admitted. “It implies something.” For six weeks over April and May, I reached out to hundreds of people who were about to lose their Wikipedia page, almost all of whom were learning that information from me. “If you want to write something to save the page, I would appreciate it,” a Spanish filmmaker told me. A biography on Wikipedia might not be intended as a prize, but nobody knows its value like the people who’ve had one and then lost it.

Martina Dimoska is a scientist, originally from North Macedonia, who works at NASA’s Johnson Space Center in Houston. Specifically, Dimoska is an “analog astronaut”—she takes part in missions that simulate a space environment—and a “science communicator.” She posts often about her work in the can-do, aspirational register that is de rigueur on LinkedIn, where she cites her “recognition by … Forbes, Bloomberg, the United Nations … and global innovation networks.” A Facebook reel of hers, filmed during the crew announcement of NASA’s upcoming Artemis III mission, shows Dimoska alongside the text, “When you work hard so you belong in rooms where history gets proactively written.” On TikTok, she’s had paid partnerships with a makeup brand whose products are tested for effectiveness during spaceflight.

On April 29, Dimoska posted on Facebook, “Wait … I’m on Wikipedia now???” She went on: “A few years ago, building a career in space from the Balkans felt impossible. And now … somehow, that journey is part of the public record. I’m incredibly grateful that [the biography] is there … My hope is that through my journey, I will help a new generation to begin to see space as accessible, not distant.”

The next day, Dimoska’s page was nominated for deletion.

Anne Clin, who has edited Wikipedia since 2005 under the name Risker, warned me that it’s folly to celebrate becoming the subject of a Wikipedia article. “Especially if you’re what we call ‘borderline notable,’” she said.

The other taboo is editing information about yourself. You can’t do that—even if the existing facts about you are wrong—without disclosing a conflict of interest. Even then, it’s strongly discouraged. Jimmy Wales, the cofounder of Wikipedia, has been caught editing his own Wikipedia biography multiple times; that he did so is now part of his Wikipedia biography. In 2022, the novelist Emily St. John Mandel, unable to update her biography to reflect her recent divorce, sat for an interview solely to create a secondary source for that information.

Some high-profile Wikipedia editors and volunteers have bio pages about themselves. Clin is occasionally asked if she would want one. She doesn’t merely refuse; she actively sabotages the possibility. “You cannot write a proper biographical article about me, because I don’t give out enough personal information to do that,” she said. “Absolutely not. Absolutely not.”

Among the people reading Martina Dimoska’s Facebook post about her new Wikipedia page was Kiril Simeonovski, an economist in Skopje, Macedonia, and a Wikipedia editor since 2008. Simeonovski found the post self-congratulatory and the biography itself entirely promotional. He also doubted the inherent notability of an “analog astronaut,” a role that he later likened to a lab mouse.

A Wikipedia editor can tag an article for deletion, and unless satisfactory edits are made within a certain timeframe, the page will disappear. Simeonovski didn’t do this—he referred the article for debate among his fellow editors.

Arguing the case for deletion, Simeonovski described the Dimoska biography as “merely [a] chronology of overhyped personal achievements,” each meticulously deflated by Simeonovski, that did not credibly establish Dimoska as a notable individual.

More damningly, in his argument, was the timing of Dimoska’s Facebook post, coming “shortly after the article was approved.” To him, this was a smoking gun as much as anything. How could she have so promptly promoted the page if she were not somehow connected to it?

When an editor proposes a Wikipedia article for deletion, they append a caveat to the marked page: “If you created this page, please don’t take offense.” This courtesy is not extended to the subject of the article. When I reached out to these people, in the hundreds, to ask them how it felt to be considered non-notable, I was not surprised that the response rate to this question was about 1 percent. Maybe lower.

Matthew Songer, a Michigan orthopedist with three decades of experience, had his biography flagged for deletion (“Unlikely to be notable”) in April. Songer was among the 1 percent of respondees. He told me it’s not for vanity that he needs that page. He is often enlisted as an expert witness in trials, he said, because of the extra imprimatur of being on Wikipedia. The page “is a good reference for attorneys.”

Maneli Mirkhan, a French-Iranian adviser on international affairs, agreed that having a Wikipedia biography inspires confidence in her services. Her biography was called out by Wikipedia editors for reading like a résumé.

The biography of Andrew Wyant, a Phoenix-area executive who works in fitness and nutrition, was also deemed insufficiently notable, due to a lack of coverage from secondary sources. Wyant said he blamed himself for failing to “feed” his public persona through self-promotion. “What I know to be true is that I am largely responsible for the growth of the profession of personal trainers and fitness coaches by building the entire category over the last decade,” he told me. “I feel my accomplishments and impact are notable, but I don’t feel the need to promote them in media so I can have a Wikipedia page.”

Wyant’s biography was deleted. Songer’s biography was deleted. Mirkhan’s biography was deleted.

Of those, only Mirkahn’s reappeared, rewritten and with new sources, a few days later. I asked Mirkhan if she had been involved in re-creating the page. No, she said, but she wasn’t surprised. Mirkhan had been, and continues to be, sought out by the French media to comment on events in Iran, and more coverage means more demonstrations of notability. But there’s always that chicken-and-egg problem: Mirkhan thinks that what gave the media confidence in choosing her as a commentator was not just her career and her expertise. It was the fact that she was on Wikipedia.

Kiril Simeonovski did not lead with his suspicions that Martina Dimoska wrote her own Wikipedia biography. He argued, on Wikipedia’s public page for editorial debates, that the biography’s sourcing was weak, relying in part on posts from Dimoska’s LinkedIn page. It promoted Dimoska’s social media accounts, where she posted things like—and this Simeonovski found objectionable enough to cite in his argument for deletion—a picture comparing herself to NASA software engineer Margaret Hamilton. Published three separate times, it became one of her most popular posts, engendering sexist mockery and vicious defense.

To Simeonovski, self-promotion like this suggested that the case for Dimoska’s notability was regularly oversold by Dimoska herself. Though he could not prove a connection, it was difficult to imagine the Wikipedia biography as anything but an extension of that project.

That afternoon, a new Wikipedia user added comments to Simeonovski’s public arraignment, noting three existing articles about other individual analog astronauts. A fair contribution, tempered by a shot across the bow at Simeonovski: The user’s name was “Kiril’s mom.” The user was not Simeonovski’s mom, and the account was subsequently blocked. Hours later, another new user asked Simeonovski, rhetorically, “If I publish a blog post about someone and he finds it amazing and celebrates it does that mean that he/she created it?” Their username was a coded reference to the actual name of Simeonovski’s mother. They also got blocked.

Nine days later, another anonymous user—whose IP address, according to Simeonovski, placed them in Skopje, North Macedonia—said Dimoska’s biography had improved “considerably,” with new sourcing from publications like The Telegraph. The revised article, the user claimed, was superior to “85%” of Wikipedia articles about living Macedonians.

Three days later, Simeonovski dismantled each of the 32 new sources in turn. It wasn’t really the British newspaper The Telegraph that had been cited, for instance, but the Albanian news website Telegrafi. “I fail to find a single independent and reputable English-language source,” he later wrote.

The next day, the anonymous user rebutted Simeonovski point by point. They said it was “strange” that Simeonovski could not seem to find a single appropriate source for Dimoska’s biography but had found “10 sources from non-independent websites” when he wrote an article about the former governor of the National Bank of North Macedonia. The National Bank of North Macedonia, the user added to Simeonovski: Isn’t that where you work?

At one point, Simeonovski said he would report his detractor to the Wikimedia Foundation legal department.

Of Rosemary Bush, a professor at Northwestern University in Illinois, a Wikipedia editor recently wrote: “It will be many years until she qualifies as notable.” Bush herself agreed, to a point, that the Wikipedia page about her did not make a strong case, though she found the biography itself to be strange, possibly AI-generated, and not wholly representative of her career. “The world is fine without a Wikipedia page on me,” she said, but added, “I do think the comment proposing deletion is a bit rude.”

A little rudeness is unavoidable in this line of work. Sonia Jones, a Welsh singer best known for the theme song from the 1979 film Monty Python’s Life of Brian, is indeed best known for the theme song from Monty Python’s Life of Brian; therefore, Wikipedia editors decided that her Wikipedia biography should be deleted and henceforth redirect to the article about the song. Jones, when I asked her for a comment, did not agree that her achievements since 1979 do not merit documentation. “No, I do not want them to remove my page,” she said. “Why would they?”

“Canada has a national dodgeball team? Who would have thought?” wrote one editor in their case to delete the biography of Savannah Burton, the first transgender athlete to compete for Canada’s national dodgeball team. “Even though dodgeball is not considered a mainstream sport, competing at the highest level in any sport is a significant accomplishment,” Burton told me. “Given the ongoing attempts to erase transgender people, along with infringements on our rights and our ability to live normal lives, this action is not surprising.”

There is, when you look for it, a careless and cocky tone that can creep into the verdicts of Wikipedia editors who have been doing the job for a long time. “This guy is a nobody. Delete him.” “I’ve got more followers on Instagram than he does.” “All we have here are stand-up comedy, opinion columns, being a guest or collaborator on some non-notable podcasts … This is more or less every twentieth person who lives in NYC.”

Rosemary Bush’s biography was deleted. Sonia Jones’ biography was deleted. Savannah Burton’s biography was retained. “It was easy to find more sources,” wrote an editor who came to the page’s rescue. Editors’ participation in this process is not mandatory. Articles, once marked for death, can be deleted simply because nobody showed up to debate the matter. That anyone would volunteer their time to save an undeserving article from oblivion is a kindness: a good deed in a rude world.

Kiril Simeonovski never did refer the anonymous editor of Martina Dimoska’s biography to the Wikimedia Foundation’s legal team. Instead, he and his antagonist continued their debate on sourcing and semantics for days, and then weeks.

Per her Wikipedia page, Dimoska’s achievements included the command of two analog astronaut missions in Poland, but Simeonovski argued that while this information was verifiable, the missions themselves did not meet the threshold of notability and therefore neither did Dimoska. The anonymous user pointed to Dimoska’s inclusion in a Macedonian book for schoolchildren about 50 Macedonian women: “The book was subsequently recommended by the Bureau for Development of Education as optional reading material for ninth-grade students nationwide, which gives the subject recurring exposure within the educational system rather than temporary media attention.”

“You’ve apparently regressed to using children’s books to support the notability of the person we’re discussing,” said Simeonovski. “I expect [you] to continue gaslighting with multi-paragraph comments, but that won’t change the observation that the person isn’t notable [enough] for a Wikipedia article.”

“Count your words and then mine … and see who is the one gaslighting with multi-paragraph comments.”

“You seem to mix up ‘prominence’ and ‘notability.’”

“Your argument relies heavily on personal anecdotes and assumptions about how Macedonian media operates.”

Other editors with an eye on the debate pleaded for colleagues to lend their perspective. Most supported Simeonovski, but they didn’t linger long. “At this point the most visible thing about this individual seems to be [this] article,” one said, before bouncing.

As this unfolded, I asked Dimoska what she made of it. “The whole thing has become quite challenging to track,” she said, “and I’m already behind on my social media.” She denied editing the page herself or having any knowledge of the people defending it. She added: “Watching strangers debate your life, work, and credibility in such an intense way is definitely a strange experience to process.”

On April 25, a user named “DavidfromArizona” took to Wikipedia to announce that his friend Sheldon Marks—“the world’s leading vasectomy reversal expert,” per Marks’ own website—wished for his Wikipedia biography to be removed.

“It is inaccurate and does not truly represent his achievements,” David wrote. “[His] ego is hurt by having such a page. He either wants a detailed page or wants this one deleted.” A request from the subject of a Wikipedia article is not grounds for deletion in itself, although it can tip the balance if there is no clear consensus. On examination, Wikipedia editors found the biography lacking in sources that demonstrated notability.

On May 6, a Wikipedia user who 17 years earlier had created a biography for Sophie Aston, a British landscape artist, said that Aston had recently asked that the page be deleted. “She has retrained as a psychotherapist and wishes to forget her career as an artist.”

Having seen how much a Wikipedia page matters to certain people, I asked Marks and Aston why they wanted theirs removed.

“A kind and enthusiastic man made my Wiki page as a surprise for me about 17 years ago,” Aston told me. “We’d been on two dates. I found it a bit embarrassing but sweet. I didn’t see him again; I wasn’t into him. The info on the page was never [very] accurate and is now very out of date.” It was “ostentatious,” she added, for Wikipedia to present her as an artist of note, not least because for the past 10 years she has been a professional psychotherapist. The biography, she said, “makes it sound like I am an important artist, and I am not—it’s inappropriate.”

The Wikipedia editors could not treat Aston’s request with undue favor, but given the chance to cast fresh eyes over the article, they found her biography lacking. “The request to delete her article is not relevant,” one wrote bluntly, “but I don’t view her artistic career as meeting [notability] guidelines regardless of the request.” Aston’s biography was deleted.

Sheldon Marks didn’t respond to me. After receiving my email, however, he fled straight to the debate unfolding on Wikipedia. “I did not request deletion of this article,” he wrote. The statements made by DavidfromArizona, whom Marks did not know, were apparently fabrications. Marks wanted to keep his page. He admitted it could be improved but maintained that he was a notable individual: “I am the author of Vasectomy Reversal: Manual of Vasovasostomy and Vasoepididymostomy (Springer, 2018), translated into Chinese by Springer Nature in 2025; the surgical instrument known as the Marks Vas Cutting Forceps is named for me; I have served as Course Coordinator for the AUA post-graduate course on vasectomy reversal; and I have been a contributor to WebMD since 2005.”

The Wikipedia editors briefly conferred, then replied that they had already voted to delete.

There’s a final twist in the biography of Martina Dimoska: She knew Kiril Simeonovski—the guy trying to get her deleted.

“I had interacted with him on social media before and had crossed paths through mutual friends,” Dimoska said. “One recent example I remember clearly is seeing his post after he was injured by a cyclist who had left the scene.” Dimoska recalled the post in detail, though she had not responded or reacted to it at the time.

Simeonovski said he didn’t remember ever speaking with Dimoska in person but admitted the possibility of “virtual interaction.” Both are active on Facebook, and though they do not follow each other, Simeonovski said they have enough mutual friends in North Macedonia that their respective algorithms often put them in each other’s path. Just as Dimoska saw Simeonovski’s post about his accident, so Simeonovski came across Dimoska’s celebration of inclusion in Wikipedia, which led him to discover Dimoska’s earlier Instagram post comparing herself to Margaret Hamilton, then to a much older Reddit thread asking if Dimoska was the smartest Macedonian woman. (“Isn’t it just all hype about that girl?” one Redditor replied.) Simeonovski didn’t comment on any of those posts. But in Wikipedia he had found an opportunity to contest her notability on surer ground.

“It was somewhat surprising to me, perhaps because I have become accustomed to professional environments where people tend to be more openly supportive of one another’s work,” Dimoska told me. “At the same time, having grown up in North Macedonia, I am aware that recognition within a small community can sometimes generate more scrutiny than one might expect.”

One month after Simeonovski nominated Dimoska’s biography for deletion, he lost. The page was kept. “Given the bludgeoning, canvassing, and sockpuppetry evident here, this is a no consensus closure without prejudice against speedy renomination,” wrote an editor in a determination. “The arguments to delete here are stronger on the merits, but sufficient reasoned opposition exists that I do not see a consensus for deletion.” Obstinacy won the day, though Dimoska’s page now carries a disclaimer that it contains “promotional content.”

Wikipedia pages, for better or worse, matter to people. To be included is a validation of a life. If it were not so desirable, then one of the very first things Kiril Simeonovski did upon joining Wikipedia in 2008 would not have been to create a page about himself. Which he did do. He “wasn’t aware about the notability policies” back then, he told me.

The page was promptly deleted. “If your achievements, etc., are verifiable and genuinely notable and thus suitable for inclusion in Wikipedia,” an editor chided Simeonovski at the time, “someone else will probably create an article about you sooner or later.” To date, nobody has.

Searching for the ‘silent majority’ of supporters for Flock’s surveillance technology in one West Virginia town.

It was about to be a long night in Huntington, West Virginia.

Mayor Patrick Farrell stood before 10 members of city council in a white dress shirt and dark blazer studded with an American flag pin. Behind him sat his constituents and residents from neighboring towns. Some had their arms crossed, while others held a steady glare in his direction. Less than a week before the July 13th meeting, a 33-year-old named Jackie Harper had been shot and killed in Huntington. It was the kind of crime Farrell had promised to get under control. But a breakthrough in the case just days after the killing suggested something promising to Farrell. Harper’s suspected killer, said the mayor, had been found in another state — and it was Flock cameras that helped track him down.

The network of automated license plate reader technology, Farrell said, had also helped find a suspect in another tragic double killing earlier in the year of a beloved middle school basketball coach and his brother, a city worker. “Take that in just this year, and then take it back to all of the cold cases that we have,” Farrell said, referencing an unsolved case from more than 20 years ago where four teens were killed in Huntington on their prom night. Shortly after the gruesome shooting, police said they suspected it was linked to crack cocaine dealers from Detroit. To this day, officials still point to a stubborn flow of drugs from Michigan as the central scourge of the community. The prom night murders remain unsolved. “Think about what might have happened had we had this technology,” Farrell pleaded.

For the next seven hours, that’s what dozens of residents of Huntington and surrounding areas imagined as they stepped up to the microphone to address the city council. Their vision of a Flock-filled future sounded far more bleak. Individuals’ rights to privacy would be trampled. They would be stalked and surveilled. Perfectly legal day-to-day activities could be subject to abuse by Flock itself or rogue police officers with access to its recordings. Sometime after 2AM, a few speakers simply read from George Orwell’s 1984, passing a copy of the book back to the next person in line who wanted it, committed to keeping councilmembers up and looking into their own crystal ball as long as they could.

Several members of the city council shared their concerns. Language in the proposed contract was not as tight as they would have liked to protect the city, potentially opening it up to lawsuits, a few warned. And given how strongly opposed so many residents seemed to be — both at the meeting and in private outreach in the days leading up to the vote — even if the technology might be helpful, was it really necessary to vote right now?

Farrell told the officials that he was inspired to bring Flock to Huntington after speaking with other mayors at a conference who said the tool was the key thing that helped them to reduce crime. “Have you spoken with the mayors of any cities who have canceled their contracts with Flock?” one councilmember asked. “I have not,” said the mayor.

But amid hours of argument against the contract, the majority of the council remained unconvinced. They approved the resolutions that would let Farrell enter Huntington into business with Flock, 6-4. After an initial vote rejecting a motion to table the contract indefinitely, a camera recording the live stream of the meeting began to shake. “Is that thunder?” the council chair asked. Someone off-screen responded, mentioning the constituents watching in an upstairs auditorium. “That’s your taxpayers.”

Huntington, several locals tell me, is a place where people would give you the shirt off their back. Every interview during my visit seems to end with a lengthy list of restaurants to try and a firm request that I reach out if I need help with anything else at all.

It’s a fairly spread-out city dotted with a popular downtown food hall, quaint shopping areas, a city park, and anchored by Marshall University, which is led by the former CEO of Intuit. A state delegate I meet, Ryan Browning, describes it as a “small town” with a “big city feel,” and he lights up talking about the local summer concert series and his favorite cafe that turned him into a coffee drinker. On the weekend I visit, everyone seems to be walking around in kelly green in preparation for Marshall’s Division 1 NCAA football team’s home opener, at a stadium that can host more than 38,000 people — just about 7,000 shy of the city’s own population. “I think what makes Huntington a very special little pocket of West Virginia is the people in the community and how they’re just ready to roll up their sleeves,” Holly Smith Mount, a Marshall alumna and city councilmember who voted against the Flock contract, tells me.

Part of that is the fact that the city has “had our share of tragedies,” she says. It’s grappled with a series of large floods, a plane crash in the ’70s that killed 37 members of the Marshall football team alongside coaches and staff (and became the subject of the movie, We Are Marshall, starring Matthew McConaughey), and a drug epidemic that has rattled the community for years. The Cabell-Huntington Health Department found in 2015 that drug overdose was the third-leading cause of death in Cabell County, which encompasses much of Huntington. Following a 2018 study, Johns Hopkins University researchers said the county had the highest opioid overdose rate in the state.

But because of that, it’s also a place with a lot of tenacity. “I know a lot of people who are barely making it, even including myself, a lot of Gen Z people,” says Noah Jasper Gibson, a young musician I meet while he’s protesting Flock. “Even though their situation’s hopeless, they’re still making the best of it. They’re still out protesting after working 12 hours. They’re still donating their money when they have almost nothing left to give … I think it’s the spirit of those who strive for more that makes Huntington what it is.” Longtime Huntingtonian Brian Trippett, who goes by the moniker “The Front Porch Conservative” on his local streaming talk show, says there’s “a certain amount of lovable curmudgeoniness to our city.”

That might explain why Farrell, when asked after the vote on the Flock contract about his constituents’ reactions, described those who opposed it as part of “a very loud and vocal minority.” On the other side, he added, “is the silent majority.” He said he’s heard from hundreds who support the contract.

There may well be hundreds, if not thousands, of Huntingtonians who’d rather bring Flock to town than not. But on my brief visit, I see far more than a few people who oppose it, and most people I meet struggle to put a finger on who this silent majority is. A councilmember who voted in favor of the resolutions, Rob Archer, tells me that he encounters more Flock supporters in person than he sees online, but he also regularly meets constituents who disapprove of it. One group of Huntingtonians is betting they can prove Farrell wrong by putting it to a vote. They’re aiming to get enough signatures — at least 10 percent of registered voters — for a ballot initiative that would effectively void the Flock contract and bar the government from replacing it with a competing service.

As of mid-September, the Huntington Community Action Group (HCAG) said it had 1,600 signatures after a little over a month of canvassing. It aims to collect 4,000, giving it some buffer for the roughly 2,700 needed to force a city council vote on their proposal. Success would start a clock on a process that could put the proposed ban to a citywide vote. If the city council rejects or alters the ordinance, as expected, it will get put to a vote within a year. If there’s no election that year, it would get its own ballot.

It’s “one of those popcorn emoji moments,” Mount says. She finds it exciting to see the democratic process in action. “I don’t want to sit there ‘til four in the morning again,” she says, “but I will if I have to. I absolutely will if I have to.”

It was the first Tuesday of July, less than a week before the marathon city council meeting, and Mount paid a visit to the Huntington Police Department with her fellow Democratic councilmember Tia “Fix” Rumbaugh.

Mount and Rumbaugh, alongside other city councilmembers, had been invited to the police department for a tech demonstration. In a letter from the city’s chief data officer on June 25th, Flock’s name was not mentioned. It invited the members to “an informational meeting” about two resolutions dealing with the “proposed purchase” of a suite of devices for the police force. Members would receive a “detailed overview” of what the tools could do, and the anticipated data security and privacy protections that would come with them.

By the time of the demonstration, there had been reports about police misuse of the database, and neighboring cities employed the technology, but the nationwide crescendo of pushback that broke out this summer had not yet peaked. So on Tuesday, July 7th, Mount and Rumbaugh went in relatively fresh to the demo, where they were joined by Republican councilmember Todd Sweeney, Police Chief Phil Watkins, his deputy, and a screen full of Flock employees ready to present how their technology could help Huntington fight crime.

Over a slide deck, Flock representatives discussed the scale of its reach in other police departments and described the basics of each of its technologies. Flock’s most well-known tool is its automated license plate reader (ALPR), which uses AI to help identify vehicles by specific characteristics, as well as partial or full license plate numbers. What makes Flock’s offering unique is its nationwide network, which lets local police departments search not only their own cameras, but those of others who have opted in. Police can track a single vehicle often in real time, which they say can be a valuable tool, especially in time-sensitive cases like kidnappings. The city didn’t pursue a bidding process for the contract, Rumbaugh recalls being told, because no other tool can replace what Flock offers.

“I couldn’t help but think, If we had this, would that not have happened?”

But as reports at law enforcement agencies across the country have demonstrated, Flock’s unique selling point can also make it uniquely vulnerable to abuse. Officers have been reportedly caught using Flock to track exes or harass residents who have not been suspected of committing a crime. Following a national uproar, Flock updated some of its policies and defaults to safeguard its systems from this kind of abuse. But critics like the American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU) say the protections do not go nearly far enough, and that the expansive use of such tools threatens to degrade civil liberties.

Still, as Flock shared the crime-fighting possibilities in Huntington, the promise seemed almost irresistible. “The picture that was painted in the presentation was beautiful,” Mount says. She coached at the middle school and was friends with the fellow coach who was killed in the shooting earlier this year. “I couldn’t help but think, If we had this, would that not have happened?” she says. She got choked up during the presentation. Flock, she says, assured her they could absolutely help prevent such crimes. “And I thought, if this is the silver bullet that we need to fix the problems with drug trafficking … show me where to sign.”

Rumbaugh, however, had begun typing on her phone under the table, trying to find out more about the company. She saw reports about vandalism of the cameras, and anticipated residents furthest to the political left would be most upset. This was about to be a partisan fight, she thought. Yet seeing Mount, a fellow Democrat and ally on city council, have such a visceral positive reaction made Rumbaugh worry she was out on her own. “My state of mind was, ‘This is going to be a problem,’” Rumbaugh says. “‘Everybody is going with it, and I am outnumbered.’”

When, Rumbaugh recalls asking the city and police officials, would this technology come up at city council? If it were up to Farrell, she recalls them answering, it would have already been done. The next day, the council released its agenda for the following Monday’s meeting, and the Flock resolutions were on it. If passed, they’d allow the city to enter into a contract.

Though Mount left the tech demo dreaming of what-ifs, more practical questions soon settled in. As a self-described “data and tech nerd” with a nursing informatics degree, she began researching the company and its claims about resolving and deterring crimes. She was unimpressed by Flock’s own studies of its impact, wanting a longer observation period and independent, peer-reviewed assessment. Could it have really caught her friend’s alleged killer before the act? The suspect had a criminal history but wasn’t currently wanted for a crime, so he was unlikely to be on a hot list flagging his whereabouts before the shooting. “So how would they have known?” Mount asks.

”Flock has consistently responded to requests from West Virginia State Delegates and Huntington city leaders to explain how Flock LPR technology works and to address common questions and concerns. We have been transparent about what the technology does and does not do, and we will continue providing factual information to lawmakers and the public,” Flock spokesperson Courtney Terlecki said in a statement. Terlecki called Flock’s technology “an investigative lead” that “captures point-in-time images of vehicles in public places,” and said that in West Virginia this year, its automated license plate readers sent alerts about 21 missing people and over 900 stolen plates or vehicles. Terlecki also pointed to two other cases in West Virginia where Flock was credited with finding a missing child and locating a murder suspect in another state.

As Mount’s analytical side began sowing skepticism, she and Rumbaugh were also hearing from constituents. First, from the regulars who are highly engaged in local politics. Then, from constituents who rarely reach out. “The floodgates opened,” Mount says. “I have never received as many correspondences from constituents on any other topic.” Though she eventually stopped counting, Mount estimates she got over a hundred messages on the subject, with “less than a handful” in favor of Flock. Rumbaugh says the level of fear reminded her of the pandemic. “I think our residents feel like they just have no control over it, and not having control is terrifying,” she says.

At some point between the demo and the city council meeting, Mount and Rumbaugh spoke on the phone. Rumbaugh recalls Mount sounding distressed. She felt misled by Flock. Rumbaugh realized she may no longer be alone in her fight, but the resolutions were still likely to pass. “It felt like entering a losing battle,” Rumbaugh says. “But that doesn’t mean that you stop.”

“I have never received as many correspondences from constituents on any other topic.”

Shortly after 4PM the Friday before the vote, a Facebook post from ACLU-WV investigative reporter Kyle Vass lit another match. Through Freedom of Information Act requests, Vass had obtained hundreds of pages of emails between the police department and Flock, going back months. About to leave for a music festival, Vass pored over what he could and posted the rest to his Facebook page for others to mine. It was reshared nearly 700 times.

For Mount, “the glass started to crack” with those documents. She realized the city was already talking to Flock by the time the council approved a line item for public safety capital expenditures — the one that would be used to fund the contract. Farrell’s budget requested $1.5 million for “other equipment” pertaining to public safety, on a line that had been blank in the 2026 budget, and just $169,000 the year before. And as Rumbaugh spent hours reading and answering comments on Vass’ post and on her own page, she noticed some of her usual right-wing “trolls” sounding a note of agreement. “When I noticed that these MAGA conservatives were equally upset about Flock, it made me sit up,” she says. “That’s when I knew it was bigger than I had previously thought.”

The vote was barreling ahead on Monday. Mount planned to raise a motion to table the discussion altogether. At the meeting, Rumbaugh supported the motion, but it failed. Both were left wondering: What’s the rush?

The empty lot of Po-Boy’s Tires didn’t strike me as the ideal place to collect signatures. The used tire shop is on the corner of a residential block and is made up of two structures reminiscent of converted shipping containers painted red. “Po-Boy’s Used Tires” is spray-painted on their sides in big bubble letters in black with a white shadow, and piled-up tires mark an entrance of sorts.

Soon after I arrive, some of the HCAG volunteers I’m shadowing begin to trickle in, some wearing bright yellow T-shirts that say “GET THE FLOCK OUT.” They set up a table with fliers and distribute clipboards on which they’ll collect signatures. Then, they get to work.

As the evening traffic picks up, I begin to understand the logic behind this spot. For the next two to three hours, nearly a dozen volunteers stand by the side of the road with signs and clipboards, waving down cars to sign their petition. “Stop Flock, sign here,” one sign says. Another has a cut-out picture of Farrell’s face with a speech bubble that says “Let us spy on you!” On the wide thoroughfare, there’s consistent honking by drivers cheering on the effort. At one point, a police officer on a motorcycle looks bemused as he drives by. Volunteers begin directing a steady stream of cars into the Po-Boy’s lot, handing the clipboards through car windows after asking if they’re registered to vote in Huntington.

Signatories come from all kinds of backgrounds. Dennis Gibson is an Army veteran with a long white beard and leather jacket full of patches celebrating the US Army, the Second Amendment, and Harley-Davidson. Standing next to his sleek black motorcycle, he tells me he’s a “huge conservative” who actually voted for Farrell. Now, he believes the mayor to be “a Democrat in sheep’s clothing” due to his support of Flock. Gibson says he’s “totally against” Flock and worries about degrading residents’ privacy rights. For the $2.1 million the city is spending on the technology, he says, they could likely solve more crimes by getting more cops on the street, though several councilmembers have warned me police recruitment has gotten harder.

At some point in the evening, a man wearing sunglasses and long hair tied back shows up with what looks, at least from a few feet away, like a cut-down Flock camera. On closer inspection, it’s a hyper-realistic replica that its maker, a carpenter named Josh Pickens, says he constructed in 30 minutes from PVC pipe, cardboard, duct tape, and spray paint. There’s a real solar panel attached to the top, that is actively charging his phone. “The cell phone’s zip-tied to the whole thing so that it can’t be ripped away or anything,” he tells me while standing on a platform where he can hoist the pole into view across the street. “So I can actually record stuff if I wanted to.”

Pickens wears a cardboard sign strung around his neck that reads “FUCK FLOCK,” recognizing his craft project might be mistaken for the real thing otherwise. “I figured people need to see what they’re mad at.” Pickens, who lives in a neighboring town, describes himself as apolitical; his last major protest was an encampment for the Occupy movement around 2011. He first learned about Flock last winter, when he saw the cameras in his hometown in Illinois. “I have firsthand experience of feeling uncomfortable as shit,” he says. So when he learned about the vote in Huntington, he says, “I was just like, ‘No, you can’t have that. Not here.’ West Virginia’s supposed to be too wild and wonderful to have this surveillance crap.”

Candy Tapp, one of the volunteers in the yellow HCAG shirts, tells me between her clipboard duties that she just recently registered to vote, motivated to sign the petition. “I’m really totally for this,” she says. “I want the police to get off their butts and do their job. They don’t need these cameras to do their job.” When it’s time, she says, “by George, I’m ready to vote.”

“I want the police to get off their butts and do their job. They don’t need these cameras to do their job.”

A few of the volunteers are musicians; they’re holding a “Rock Against Flock” concert the next day, organized to raise awareness for the petition. Michael “Roofus” Riley Jenkins wears a T-shirt littered with holes — I’ll later watch him rip it off his chest mid-performance with his band, Roofus and the Rex. At the canvassing event, he tells me he’s occasionally recognized around town for his sign-off at the marathon city council meeting: “I’m not going to waste your time — I’m kind of hungry. But one last thing. Every single Flock camera has about two to three pounds of copper and about one to two grams of gold. Do with that information what you will.”

Jenkins says he doesn’t really have a political affiliation but that his philosophy is best described as “killdozer-esque libertarian socialist.” Or, put another way, “I believe in having gay Muslim immigrants protect their weed farms with guns.” Still, he says the Flock vote was the first city council meeting he showed up to. “This whole situation just kind of lit a fire up under my ass,” he says. One silver lining? He’s found something in common with his Trump-supporter parents. “The only good thing to come out of this is the fact that I can talk to Republicans again.”

After the meeting, Jenkins posted a song he wrote on Instagram. “The mayor thinks he sees a vocal minority, but I think we all know the truth,” he sang. “It’s about the money in his pockets. Flock you.” When I attend the concert the next day, he and his band perform from the side of his front porch to a crowd of at least a couple dozen people in what feels like some combination of a backyard party, performance, and protest. “We’re here because the government decided against what the people want,” he shouts into the microphone, punctuated by a drum and shouts in response from the crowd. “And we the people are the ones who are going to take the power back.”

Jasper Gibson, who also sings at the concert, tells me he wanted to communicate that all hope is not lost just because of the city council vote. “You have a purpose. There’s a fight that can be had. We can make stuff happen,” he says. From the porch, he introduces a song he says he wrote when he was 14 about “helicopter parents” who are always watching, telling you what you can or can’t do. It applies nicely to the current situation, he says. “You call it backtalk, I call it free speech,” the song goes.

Back at Po-Boy’s, the steady honking throughout the evening is certainly loud. The volunteers and signatories are vocal. “I don’t know too many that are for it,” Gina Carr tells me through her window after signing the petition. The volunteers fold up their table and collect their clipboards as the traffic finally dies down. They estimate they collected well over 100 signatures tonight. If they reach their goal of 4,000 signatures, they’ll get the chance to show if they’re the majority, too.

“I don’t want my kids to grow up with stories of, ‘Oh, this used to be a great place 50 years ago,’” Jenkins tells me. “I want my kids to experience this as a great place to live now. Because I am a West Virginian. I’m a proud West Virginian. I want to die a proud West Virginian, and I want to raise hell and praise Dale until people actually start to fucking listen to what West Virginians have to say.”

Making Huntington safer was always a core pillar of Farrell’s pitch to become mayor. “Public safety is priority number one, and it begins with strong support for law enforcement,” he wrote in a Substack post while running for office in 2024. That includes focusing on the drugs that flow into the city and have fueled addiction among some residents. Earlier this year, while the Huntington Police Department was engaging in talks with Flock, Farrell released a public safety dashboard on the city website. “The people of Huntington should be able to see the same trends and patterns city leaders use to understand what is happening in our community, while understanding that some information must remain confidential for public safety,” he wrote on his Substack. “Transparency should not be an afterthought. It should be the starting point.”

Farrell is a clean-cut Huntington native, whose tie-less blazer and button-down look at city council is still a touch more formal than the councilmembers’ attire. He’s the son of a nurse / hospice executive and a judge. “I was raised by a nurse’s heart and judge’s sense of justice,” Farrell told the West Virginia Chamber. Farrell served almost a decade in the Air Force and then pursued his MBA at Duke University, becoming optimistic about the impact he could make through running businesses. When he returned to Huntington, he ran a clean water solutions company and joined several boards, including Marshall University’s board of governors, where he helped recruit former Intuit CEO Brad Smith to lead the institution. City Hall spokesperson Chrisa Hayes declined The Verge’s request to interview Farrell for this article, saying he wouldn’t be commenting on the ballot measure effort. Hayes did not respond to a detailed list of questions seeking comment on this article.

Farrell’s vision for Huntington, according to past interviews and campaign materials, is for the city to become a thriving, safe place, where residents feel they need not look elsewhere for opportunity. So when Farrell attended a conference for newly elected mayors in December 2024, just before taking office, he was keen to figure out what other cities were doing to improve public safety. This, he’s said, is the first time he learned about Flock.

To Farrell, it seemed, the thing that cities lowering their crime rates had in common was this networked surveillance technology. He heard about it again at another meeting of the mayors in the summer of 2025, the first year he was officially in office. Around that fall, he told local TV station WSAZ in an interview that he approached Watkins, the Huntington police chief, about exploring using Flock’s technology.

Farrell told WSAZ that Watkins did nine months of due diligence, rebuffing the idea that the process was rushed. In a lawsuit filed after the city council vote, the ACLU claimed City Hall had still skipped relevant steps to put the process in place.

But the mayor maintains that he’s been listening. “I am not surprised that people have very sincere concerns about privacy,” he told WSAZ. “For eight and half hours I had ears open” at the city council meeting, he said, hearing 50 people speak out. But “just because you come to city council and you speak out, doesn’t mean you speak for the majority of the city,” he said. Throughout the interview, he referred to a separate group of constituents: the silent majority.

Farrell said he’d privately heard from religious leaders and business people who perhaps find the technology “creepy” but understand its potential benefits and accept the trade-offs. But when I speak to even supporters of the mayor, they don’t seem to personally know many of those people. “I haven’t heard anybody really clamoring for this,” says David Williams, better known around Huntington as Alligator Jackson, the moniker of a popular Facebook page where he posts updates about local happenings. He can’t think of many people who support the contract, except for “one guy who messaged me all the time. He ain’t that silent.”

Williams describes himself as a friend of the mayor’s and initially says he’s neutral on Flock coming to Huntington. He sees the fight as a continuation of past squabbles between the mayor and more liberal residents over issues like funding a homeless shelter or an animal refuge. “I think it more or less threw the proverbial gasoline on the fire and ignited it,” he says.

But as we speak, Williams, too, admits he’s “not in favor” of the technology. “We’ve been getting by without it,” he says. “I think it’s a little bit overkill, especially with everything that’s going on… why do we want to get into something that everybody else is trying to get out of?”

“Why do we want to get into something that everybody else is trying to get out of?”

Williams, like many others I spoke with, worries about a slippery slope of allowing expansive surveillance to become the norm. His first concern was that Flock could be used to track down residents for minor traffic violations in an effort to raise money through tickets. Williams said Farrell assuaged this concern in a private meeting, saying he was laser-focused on using the cameras to crack down on “cartel-level drug activity” in the city. Flock could be used to track down drug traffickers as soon as they cross into Huntington. “It just seems all iffy to me,” Williams says. “It’s just a slippery slope.”

He and many others wonder: Why the rush? Williams is a recreational therapist who works regularly with people experiencing addiction, and he says Huntington’s problems with drugs span well over a decade. While his “Alligator Jackson’s Inside Huntington WV” page now shares a mix of local news, restaurant openings, and community events, he started it around 2011 to warn residents about an addiction epidemic he’d seen firsthand. “They’re acting like there’s a keen sense of urgency, and I don’t understand what the sense of urgency is because that would’ve been great 10, 15 years ago. But now it’s not as bad as what it was,” he says.

Jason Arthur, chair of the city council’s public safety committee who voted to approve the contract, believes “not as bad” is still unacceptable. “My mindset is operation zero,” he says. Yes, the city’s own public safety dashboard shows violent crime was down 30 percent in the first six months of the year compared to 2025, but that’s not good enough. “Is one murder better than five murders? Absolutely, but it’s still a murder.”

Farrell insists that though the Flock contract is multiyear, it’s funded annually, so the city council could simply choose to stop funding it after one year if it doesn’t work as expected. But at least under the current budget, the money allocated for the Flock contract couldn’t simply be rerouted to other issues like funding the animal shelter, says Arthur, because it was set aside specifically for law enforcement equipment.

Arthur and Archer, the city council vice chair who also voted in favor of the contract, both say they’ve heard from this quiet faction that Farrell insists exists. Arthur says he listens to the residents who show up at city council, but like the mayor, cautions they aren’t necessarily representative of all of Huntington. “When I make decisions like this, especially with public safety, I think of the people that are voiceless,” Arthur says. “So I’m thinking of our elderly, I’m thinking of our children.”

Archer says most of the emails he’s gotten about Flock have been negative, but the opinions of people who approach him in person are varied and often lean more positive. As an associate principal and wrestling coach at the local high school, Archer says he can instantly tell when someone approaches him at a game or event to bring up Flock. “They walk up and say, ‘Hey, man.’ I’m like, ‘Oh, here it comes,’” he says. But he’s often surprised by where they stand. “It is not a Democrat-Republican issue. It is a personal issue ... You don’t know what you’re going to get, who’s for it and who’s against it. You can’t tell.”

But for all his conviction, Archer says he, too, has concerns. When I ask about the people who read from 1984 at the city council meeting, worried that they were about to bring “Big Brother” to town, Archer doesn’t resist the analogy. “I worry about it too,” he says. “I’m not sitting here saying ‘I 100 percent back this.’” Even so, he thinks Flock could be a “force multiplier” for the dwindling number of police hopefuls he watches try out on the high school track field every year. He believes it might have helped catch the prom night killer, whose impact still ripples through the schools. With some safeguards in place, Archer believes, it’s worth a shot.

About a month after Huntington approved its contract with Flock, the company was in the hot seat again in West Virginia — this time, though, it had a far colder welcome from the jump. Flock’s director of government affairs Kevin Kane faced a skeptical crowd at the state capitol building in Charleston, where he sought to address state lawmakers’ concerns about the technology during an interim session hearing. By the end, lawmakers seemed to only walk away with more concerns.

“I remember going like, ‘Why is he not answering the question?’” Republican state Sen. Patricia Rucker tells me at her office in Charleston in September. She pulls out a sheet of scribbled notes from the hearing. “Require warrant to access certain info. With exceptions for emergencies,” she’d written beneath a cursive header titled “Model Legislation.” “I personally do not want this used for speed checks!”

A few days after the hearing, Flock released its policy updates, including newly mandated multi-factor authentication across its user base. That statement, three state lawmakers charged in a letter to Flock’s CEO, appeared to contradict Kane’s sworn testimony. “Do you guys require two-factor authentication to log into the system?” Republican delegate Ryan Browning asked. “Yes,” Kane answered, saying he didn’t know when that began, but “it’s been a standard practice for a long time.”

That was one of several alleged inconsistencies or misleading statements. The lawmakers requested a detailed list of information about Flock’s policies, safeguards, and technical capabilities. Flock gave a lengthy response that defended Kane’s answers as “the conduct of a witness drawing an accurate distinction, not obscuring one.” But the lawmakers again slammed it as “a two-page exercise in corporate reframing.” After another tense round of letter exchanges, the lawmakers told Flock they expect a prepared witness to testify under oath at their next public meeting on November 9th. Republican delegate Tristan Leavitt tells me at his office in the Charleston capitol building that it’s “extremely likely” the state will pass legislation around surveillance technology next year.

“We have responded to West Virginia lawmakers’ questions in writing, promptly, and remain committed to working directly with state officials,” Flock’s Terlecki said in a statement. “Flock has offered to participate in the November 9 briefing, and we welcome the opportunity to address questions openly and correct any misunderstandings.”

Among the three Republican state lawmakers I meet, each has a different core reason for their skepticism of Flock. Browning sees the ability to track vehicles across locations as a tool that could encroach on civil liberties and privacy, including Second Amendment rights if, say, a camera tracked the comings and goings at a gun shop. “If it’s used to gather data on a high crime area, et cetera, then it could be used as a valuable tool,” he says. “But if you’re using it to just constantly collect and store data that could then be later used to target groups, then it’s a problem. And whether that be the LGBTQ community, or gun owners, or whatever the ideological change is, then it can be a massive problem.”

Rucker is staunchly opposed to allowing a tool to perform searches that in other contexts would require a warrant under the Fourth Amendment. As a first-generation American citizen born in Venezuela, Rucker says she knows government surveillance first-hand. Some of her relatives there will only communicate with her in “coded language,” she says. Flock’s lofty aspirations of eliminating crime strike her as less rosy than the company likely intends. “If we want to consider a society where no one would ever get lost, and no one would ever get hurt, and there would be no way that someone could hurt another individual, what type of surveillance would that take?” she asks. “I’m not going there.”

Leavitt is more confident that the Fourth Amendment concerns could be addressed through careful policy and isn’t even particularly concerned with Flock’s (since-disbanded) pilot with federal agencies, though he asked about it in his initial letter. He’s more concerned about what he sees as purposefully obtuse answers from Flock. “It doesn’t matter what my ideological views are or your ideological views are. If they are going to legislative bodies or public bodies who have a responsibility because they’re entrusted by the voters of their area to make decisions, you give them straightforward information,” he says.

As the legislature waits to officially regroup in January, Browning says nothing is stopping Flock from taking more action on its own. “If you were really genuine about wanting to be a good company, you would be the ones trying to model your technology around the rights of our citizens, not waiting for a legislature — which meets 60 days out of the year and makes laws 60 days out of the year — to come in and regulate.”

By mid-September, it’s been two months since Huntington’s city council approved the city’s Flock contract. The company has since faced national scrutiny over a relentless drip of stories about alleged police misuse, prompting the company to update its data access and retention policies, and its CEO to issue a mea culpa over his past approach. Flock planned to cut about 18 percent of employees as of October, according to Reuters, after an earlier voluntary buyout program.

But some cities that have ousted Flock have simply replaced it with similar surveillance systems from competitors like Axon. At this point, the fight isn’t just about one company — the path forward for an entire technology is at stake.

As Huntington’s city council reconvenes, Farrell still hasn’t signed the contract with Flock, even though he’s been given the power to do so. First, he says, he’d like the government to pass guardrails on the technology’s use. That’s what the council assembled to discuss on this late summer day, with a scheduled vote on an ordinance proposed by the mayor. If passed, it would lay out the acceptable and prohibited uses of data collected by Flock or similar technologies. Authorized uses: homicide, missing persons, stolen vehicles, active warrants, officer safety, and more. Prohibited uses: traffic enforcement, immigration enforcement not required by a federal warrant or court order, “Revenue generation,” and “Personal curiosity,” among others.

The police chief would be expected to maintain a list of authorized users, and officers would be prohibited from sharing accounts. The data would be deleted after 30 days, and there would be quarterly audits for compliance. Officers found to misuse access could face administrative sanctions, which could include a referral for criminal investigation.

“I am assuming good intentions by everybody, and I’d like all of you to as well,” Farrell begins his remarks, clarifying that it would apply to all public safety technologies, not just Flock. He shares residents’ concerns and disdain for mass surveillance, and this ordinance will help safeguard their privacy. Huntington is lucky, actually, he says. Other cities had to enact safeguards after the fact. Huntington is getting to craft them before. This time, he’s read up on those mayors who paused their Flock contracts. Several of them did so to put those guardrails in place, he says, but intend to resume them.

Democratic councilmember Sarah Walling grills Farrell on terms of the contract and ordinance. How could consistent tracking of public locations that can be strung together not be considered warrantless surveillance? Farrell says all of the cameras will be pointed at public places, and the courts would agree that’s okay. “We’ll see,” says Walling.

Rumbaugh asks why the ordinance doesn’t include the ability to charge officers who abuse the system with a misdemeanor. Will officers who abuse the system really go from zero to 100 by committing a felony? Walling doubles down on the point: Is one unauthorized search a felony? Five? Farrell says he doesn’t determine that as the mayor.

He leans on a standard line, that “if we can trust these men and women that have sworn an oath to protect us with firearms, I think we can trust them with cameras.”

“You really have no idea what you’re about to do. You’re about to give away your rights.”

“This is a company that lies,” Walling objects. “It lied to our legislatures, it’s lied to cities across the country, we have at least two states that have said that state money can’t be used for them. It can’t be used on public highways. How on Earth is Huntington, West Virginia, able to make the assurances that Flock is not going to misuse our data when it’s absolutely done it in other places without any repercussions whatsoever?”

The meeting is shorter than the one back in July, but the result is similar. After roundly negative comments from the public, the ordinance passes 7-2. Walling had earlier said the ordinance was “better than nothing,” but apparently changed her mind and votes no. Rumbaugh also votes no, and Mount isn’t present.

When the vote is finished and the floor opens for general comments from the public, a 67-year-old Army veteran eventually steps up to the microphone. “It’s my very first time ever doing this,” says Steve McDilda, wearing a yellow “Get the Flock Out” shirt and gripping both sides of the podium. His tone is that of a father trying to talk some sense into a child. “You really have no idea what you’re about to do. You’re about to give away your rights.” He’d heard that there’d been some talk of a “silent majority.” He says we’ll see about that. Once the people of Huntington get the chance to vote, he says, “you’re going to really find out.” In fact, he says. “You’re going to have an awakening.”

Not two weeks after the Tennessee legislature carved Memphis into three congressional districts this spring, in a move designed to eliminate Democratic representation, Republican state Sen. Brent Taylor made his electoral expectations clear in a photo posted on social media.

Taylor and U.S. Rep. Tim Burchett, a fellow Republican, sat side by side on the U.S. Capitol steps. “My friend Tim Burchett showing me the dadgum ropes,” Taylor wrote in a Facebook post.

On May 7, the same day that the new districts were approved, Taylor had announced his bid to represent the redrawn Congressional District 9, which for more than four decades had stood as the state’s sole majority Black district. During that time, it covered mostly the Memphis area; it now stretches across half the state and is majority white.

Taylor, 58, already has a track record with many of the voters he is now seeking to represent in Washington, D. C.: In his nearly four years as a state senator, he has taken steps to strip authority from local officials elected by many of those same voters. With the backing of a Republican supermajority, Taylor, who is white, pushed through half a dozen laws that superseded the power and priorities of local politicians.

Around the country, state legislatures from both parties have used preemption laws to control local governments. That tactic has been embraced by Republican-dominated statehouses in recent years as a way to restrict the ability of Democratic-led cities to enact policies state leaders disagree with, including protections for civil rights, public health and workers.

Memphis has long struggled with high rates of violent crime that have begun to fall only in the last couple of years. This is where Taylor focused his legislative efforts. He led the charge to prohibit judges from considering a defendant’s ability to pay when making bail decisions. He has tried to oust Steve Mulroy, the first Democrat elected as Shelby County district attorney in 25 years, and pushed through legislation authorizing the state to review every decision made by his office. And he co-sponsored a law that voided the Memphis City Council’s ban on police stops for minor traffic infractions, which had been passed after the 2023 beating death of Black motorist Tyré Nichols.

The question of how to keep communities safe has drawn divergent political approaches in Memphis — from Mulroy’s focus on rehabilitation as part of his effort to lower violent crime, to Taylor’s emphasis on aggressive prosecution and incarceration. Those approaches can mean very different experiences for the people of Memphis, which makes up most of Shelby County. Some elected officials and criminal justice experts warn that the laws Taylor has championed will embolden police to detain drivers without justification and the criminal justice system to lock up more people for longer periods in the already overcrowded county jail, where more than 65 people have died since 2018.

“I think he’s very dangerous for Shelby County, and he’s going to be very dangerous for the Ninth District if he gets elected,” said former Shelby County General Sessions Court Judge Bill Anderson, referring to how Taylor’s policies could especially harm Black men. Anderson retired in May 2025 after clashing publicly with Taylor over bail reform measures. (Anderson supported them; Taylor opposed them.)

#3663. Lykyn Review: A Mushroom Garden Worth Showing Off

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

If your online algorithm detects your having even the slightest interest in growing mushrooms, you will, at some point, probably see an ad for the Lykyn. It looks like something out of a science fiction movie—a clear box with theatrical lighting, fog, and a giant mushroom displayed like an otherworldly specimen.

After trying the Lykyn myself for a month, I can confirm that it really does look like that in real life, and as someone who has tried many different mushroom-growing methods, I was impressed by how well the Lykyn worked as a legitimate fruiting chamber. The thing you’ll have to decide for yourself is whether it’s worth $300 to showcase a process that lasts only a week or two.

Even though the company is based in the San Francisco Bay Area, Lykyn manufactures all fruiting chambers in Turkey and ships them straight from Istanbul for free. I worried about a delay, but my unit arrived in just 11 days, with none of the duty fees or other customs weirdness that seem to plague many overseas shipments these days. The speed was even more impressive given that I had the custom wood handle engraving add-on ($15), which puts your own text on the handle instead of “Lykyn.” (Mine had my name.)

The 13 x 13 x 15-inch unit, available in white or black plastic, arrives mostly disassembled—you will need to peel the protective film off both sides of each acrylic panel, slide them into plastic tracks on the base, add the corner posts, and fill the unit with distilled water. One panel doubles as a sliding door, and installing it was fiddly on my unit; I had to open and close it a few times to loosen it, and even now it still takes two hands to wrench it open. The Lykyn must be connected to 2.5-GHz Wi-Fi and a power source with its 5-volt USB-C cable (there is no provided brick), and the light—a little LED pad that sits on top—plugs into the base.

The only troubleshooting I had to do was with the ultrasonic humidifier, which emits an occasional cloud of fog (and is audible, but no louder than a typical humidifier), with extra humidity being pulled out by a fan. Despite my following directions, the mister produced no mist, and I kept getting notifications from the Lykyn app that the unit was out of water, despite the reservoir being visibly full. I eventually had to push the preinstalled wick farther down into its slot and “prime” the membrane on top with a little bit of water. (I later verified the Lykyn app’s humidity reading against my own hygrometer; the readings were similar within a couple of percentage points.) Other than refilling the water reservoir, the Lykyn needs no maintenance.

The Lykyn is a “smart” fruiting chamber, which means the humidity, airflow, and light schedules are controlled by settings for your specific species in the easy-to-use Lykyn app. There are also options to control these variables manually. (Note also that though the app shows the temperature, the Lykyn is not able to heat or cool.) While you can use any brand of fruiting block in the Lykyn up to 6 pounds, I tested my unit with the brand’s own 5-pound shiitake and pink oyster blocks, which came separately—no fruiting block is included with your Lykyn purchase. There are also no instructions on or with the blocks themselves, so be sure to check online for how yours needs to be prepared—some, like the shiitake, need to be taken totally out of their packaging, while others simply need an X cut into the side of the bag.

Having the block in versus out of the bag had a dramatic effect on the overall aesthetic of the Lykyn, which sat front and center in my dining room during the test. The bare shiitake block looked like a moon rock or quirky scientific specimen under the colorful lighting (which is customizable, but the default indicates the mushroom's “emotions," from green for “happy and thriving” to red for “stressed and worried”), while the black-bagged oyster mushroom block looked like … well, a black plastic bag. With the precisely controlled lighting and humidity, both mushroom blocks fruited within several days, compared with several weeks with other methods I’ve tried. This is especially impressive for shiitake, which are notoriously fussy in their requirements.

Both blocks produced about a pound of mushrooms each with their first flush, which were notably more flavorful than their grocery store brethren. However, the Lykyn is more of a hobby/decor setup rather than one designed for volume. Lykyn founder Ibrahim Berber told me the company wanted the chamber to “read more like an aquarium than an appliance.”

“The mycelium-to-fruit turn is one of the more visually alive things that can happen in a home,” he said. “Something that earns a spot on the kitchen counter, not a place to hide in a closet.”

Lykyn says most blocks should have two to three flushes, but only my shiitake block has had a second flush so far, and it was about three weeks after the first (and less voluminous), which meant most of the time I was displaying what looked like a moldy compressed-sawdust brick. Still, I love the Lykyn and absolutely plan to kit it out with a new fruiting block next time we have people over—a centerpiece you can eventually sauté is hard to resist.

This will be Algeria’s first foreign troop deployment in 50 years as the country shifts its military doctrine amid new security challenges.

Algeria’s parliament has approved a proposal to deploy troops to neighbouring Niger, in a major shift in the North African nation’s noninterventionist military doctrine.

Algeria’s lower house, the People’s National Assembly, ratified the deployment on Sunday after the upper house, the Council of the Nation, unanimously approved the proposal earlier in the day.

Details about the number of soldiers and when they will be deployed have yet to be made public.

It comes nearly two months after Algeria dispatched four Russian-made Sukhoi Su-30 jets, a transport aircraft and an aerial refuelling aircraft to Niamey, following what Algerian authorities described as an attempt to destabilise Niger’s military-led government.

The step comes amid a thawing of relations between Algiers and Niamey as Niger’s President Abdourahamane Tchiani visited Algeria in February and, months later, his defence minister, Salifou Mody, ⁠⁠travelled to Algiers to discuss security cooperation.

The shooting down of a Malian drone by the Algerian army last April triggered a diplomatic crisis after Niger, Mali and Burkina Faso recalled their ambassadors from Algiers. The condemnation of the 2023 coup in Niger by Algeria had also strained ties.

The troop deployment is reportedly intended to protect oil installations belonging to Algeria’s state-owned energy company, Sonatrach. Troops would be sent “to protect Algeria’s economic and strategic interests, particularly its oil facilities in Niger”, The Associated Press news agency reported, quoting Algerian Senator Belkacem Boulghiti.

It also comes as Algiers seeks to deepen security cooperation in the Sahel, a vast belt of territory stretching from the Atlantic Ocean to the Red Sea, including several countries along Algeria’s southern frontier.

Algeria has historically been reluctant to deploy its armed forces beyond its borders, reflecting a longstanding foreign policy built around sovereignty, noninterference and opposition to foreign military intervention.

But that does not mean it has never done so.

Algerian forces participated in the 1973 Arab-Israeli war, although overseas military deployments have remained exceptionally rare in the decades since.

A constitutional revision in 2020 formalised the conditions under which Algerian troops could be sent abroad.

Under Article 91, the president may authorise such deployments, provided they receive the backing of at least two-thirds of the members of each chamber of parliament.

The constitution also provides for Algerian participation in peacekeeping operations under the auspices of the United Nations, the African Union and the Arab League.

The move is particularly significant given Algeria’s military capabilities. According to the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute, the country spent more than $25bn on its armed forces in 2025, making it Africa’s largest military spender.

But the decision also comes against a backdrop of growing diplomatic tensions and shifting regional alliances.

In September, Algeria severed diplomatic relations with the United Arab Emirates, accusing Abu Dhabi of repeated “hostile” and “provocative actions” without publicly detailing the incidents behind the decision.

The two countries have long disagreed over several regional issues, including Western Sahara and the UAE’s alleged interference in North African and Sahelian affairs.

Taken together, these developments suggest that Algeria is becoming increasingly willing to assert its security interests beyond its borders, even as it continues to emphasise the principles of sovereignty and noninterference.

For Algeria, instability in the Sahel is not a distant security concern.

The country shares thousands of kilometres of borders with Mali, Niger and Libya, making developments in its southern neighbourhood particularly consequential.

The roots of the current security crisis can be traced, in part, to the collapse of Muammar Gaddafi’s government in Libya in 2011.

At the height of the Arab Spring uprisings, Algeria took a firm stance against foreign military intervention and warned that the disintegration of the Libyan state could destabilise neighbouring countries.

Following Gaddafi’s overthrow, weapons and armed fighters, including Tuareg who had served in Libya, spread into neighbouring countries.

Their return helped fuel a rebellion in northern Mali in 2012, contributing to a security crisis that preceded the military overthrow of President Amadou Toumani Toure that March.

Over the following decade, armed rebellion expanded across parts of the Sahel, particularly Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger.

Since 2012, those three countries alone have experienced six military coups: three in Mali, two in Burkina Faso and one in Niger.

Many of the coups unfolded against a backdrop of deteriorating security, with military leaders accusing civilian governments of failing to contain increasingly powerful armed groups.

But insecurity was not the only factor. Political grievances, governance failures and rivalries within armed forces also contributed to the coups.

Another factor shaping Algeria’s calculations is the changing presence of foreign military forces across the region.

For more than a decade, France was a central player in counterterrorism operations in the Sahel, deploying thousands of troops to combat armed groups linked to al-Qaeda and ISIL (ISIS).

But that military presence has largely unravelled.

Following successive coups and deteriorating diplomatic relations, France withdrew its troops from Mali in August 2022, Burkina Faso in February 2023 and Niger in December 2023.

The withdrawals were accompanied by growing anti-French sentiment and demands from military governments for greater control over their security policies.

Niger demanded the departure of approximately 1,500 French soldiers following its July 2023 coup.

Just months later, in April 2024, approximately 100 Russian military personnel arrived in Niamey, bringing equipment and providing training to Nigerien forces.

The personnel were associated with Africa Corps, a Russian paramilitary group close to the Kremlin.

Moscow has also expanded its military cooperation with Mali and Burkina Faso, where Russian personnel have undertaken training, advisory and security roles, including combat support in Mali.

The United States has also seen its military footprint shrink.

In 2024, Washington withdrew approximately 1,000 soldiers from Niger after the military government terminated their security cooperation agreement. The withdrawal included personnel stationed at an important US drone base near Agadez.

The result has been a significant realignment of the Sahel’s security partnerships, with Western military forces departing and Russia assuming a more prominent role.

For Algiers, the proposed deployment to Niger carries immediate security and wider strategic implications.

At the most practical level, protecting Algerian energy investments would help safeguard the country’s economic interests in a region affected by armed rebellions and political instability, including the Trans-Saharan Gas Pipeline (TSGP), which would transport Nigerian gas to Europe and traverse Niger and Algeria.

It could also deepen cooperation with Niger, whose military government has sought new security partners since taking power in 2023.

More broadly, the move would allow Algeria to establish a direct military presence in a neighbouring country at a time when the Sahel’s security architecture is being reshaped.

“We are not sending our soldiers to wage war in Niger, but for a mission to secure economic infrastructure,” Senator Boulghiti told reporters after the vote. “It is simply an adaptation of our doctrine to evolving circumstances so we can rise to the new challenges imposed by the situation in the Sahel region.”

#4071. Archaeologists may have just found the world's oldest shipwreck

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

A shipwreck off the coast of Turkey is the oldest shipwreck in the world, our team of archaeologists believes.

There’s little left of the ship itself but based on clues from its cargo – including pottery vessels – a research team I was part of has tentatively dated the wreck to around 2700–2400 BCE.

That’s about 4,500 years ago. Prior to this discovery, the world’s oldest shipwreck was a wreck off the coast of Dokos, in Greece, thought to have been from about 4,200 years ago.

Much work remains to be done to finesse the conclusions, and a paper outlining the results is yet to be published in a peer-reviewed journal. Yet the discovery could transform our understanding of ancient Mediterranean connections during the Early Bronze Age.

Here, and perhaps even earlier, lie the foundations of extensive trade routes as we know them today.

Associate Professor Hakan Öniz of Akdeniz University, Antalya, has worked along this coastline for decades excavating ancient shipwrecks.

Acting on clues from local seafarers, Öniz found a shipwreck in shallow waters off Kekova, west of Antalya in Turkey, in 2024. Earlier this year, he contacted me and asked if I would help date some of the pottery vessels found on this wreck.

As an archaeologist specializing in desert sites, this was a singular experience. New technology, water-logged methods of excavation and laborious conservation techniques were the new normal. Yet there is always room for learning new skills in life.

Of the ship itself there is now nothing to see, owing to underwater exposure. Yet the research team has now estimated the ship and its cargo dates to around 2700–2400 BCE. However, the precise dating is still under consideration. Earlier or later dates on either side of this initial determination are also possible.

This discovery promises to re-write our understanding of early trade networks from around 4,500 years ago.

The ship is a “missing piece” in the story of networks, trade and commodity exchange in the wider eastern Mediterranean. This era includes the pyramid age of Giza and its mighty pharaohs.

What remains under the sea is a time capsule of ancient trade.

Although its direction was not known, the boat was evidently traveling along the coast of southern Turkey.

Now resting on the seabed between two islands, it is apparent the ship likely sought refuge in the lee waters during a storm. One could imagine the terror of the sailors – possibly coastal people from the Levant region – as their ship crashed against the rocks.

While timbers from the ship disappeared long ago, the remains of its precious cargo were embedded in the rocky slope along the Kekova coast.

Divers worked over the past four months to extract its pottery from barnacles clinging to the rocky underwater slope.

Pottery is like plastic: it never goes away and just breaks down into smaller and smaller pieces.

Yet humble pottery vessels hold many clues about the past.

In this case, the cargo included large ceramic storage vessels or amphorae which once contained products from regions of the eastern Mediterranean.

The pots were large ovoid, flat-based jars with two handles, many featuring a raised horizontal clay “belt” around the widest part of the jar.

Through these vessels was possible to ascertain many pieces of information. This includes the likely origin of the jars and the contents exchanged across the region.

Importantly, the shape and manufacturing technique of the pots themselves help us date the cargo. By extension, this includes also the date of other pottery, and the ship itself.

Compared to later periods, trade and exchange in the eastern Mediterranean during the third millennium (3000–2000 BCE) is poorly understood.

Maritime voyages during the that period are known from Egyptian texts, pottery vessels and raw materials from ancient tombs.

Around the same time as this ship struck the rocks, the ancient Egyptians were trading in the eastern Mediterranean through the port of Byblos in Lebanon.

Commodities such as cedar, tin/lead, oils, tree resins, lapis lazuli and silver, all materials that Egypt lacked, were imported by the Egyptian state.

Recent scientific research also reveals that silver was acquired by the Egyptian state around 2600 BCE. Analyses showed this silver was acquired from the Lavrion mines east of modern Athens.

While it is unlikely Egyptian ships made this journey, boats like the Kekova ship may have used the route as part of an extended international maritime network.

And what was in the jars? Remarkably there were still residues visible to the naked eye. Again, scientific analyses are pending, but could be some of the products mentioned in the Egyptian texts, possibly olive oil, wine or tree resin.

The ship may have acquired its pottery from several ports on their journey. Our best assessment at this stage of research is that some of the “belted” jars (or clays used to make them) may have originated in the Central Jordan Valley.

The coast of southern Turkey is littered with ancient shipwrecks. While maritime archaeology is a relatively recent phenomenon, some of the world’s most famous wrecks have been discovered there. Who knows what other secrets these waters hold.

Israelis vote for parties rather than directly electing a prime minister, with the Knesset’s 120 seats allocated through proportional representation. Parties must win at least 3.25% of the national vote to enter parliament.

Because outright majorities are rare, parties typically form coalitions. The president asks a Knesset member — usually the leader best placed to assemble a 61-seat majority — to form a government. If repeated attempts fail, another election can be called.

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, who leads the right-wing Likud party, returned to power in late 2022 with a coalition including far-right and ultra-Orthodox parties. The alliance has since been strained by disputes over a Gaza ceasefire and the conscription of ultra-Orthodox men.

Recent polls put both Netanyahu's bloc and the main opposition bloc, led by centrist former military chief Gadi Eisenkot, short of the 61 seats needed to govern, making smaller parties potentially decisive.

Arab parties are also in the running, but most of the main Jewish parties have ruled out alliances with them. More than 20% of Israel's population is Arab, but Arab parties have rarely joined governing coalitions.

The impending vote is effectively a referendum on Netanyahu, with the Hamas attacks having "transformed Israeli society," according to László Csicsmann, a Middle East expert at Corvinus University of Budapest in Hungary.

"Israel has been at war since October 7, 2023, and the so-called 'seven-front war' launched by Netanyahu has undoubtedly worn down Israeli society," he told CNBC. "The side that can better convince voters that it can protect the population is likely to win. Other campaign issues include mandatory military service, which is particularly sensitive for religious Jewish parties and Haredi communities."

Military service is compulsory for most Jewish, Druze and Circassian Israelis over 18, while most other Arab Israelis are exempt. The conscription of ultra-Orthodox men remains a major legal and political dispute.

The economic toll of the war is also a major issue, Csicsmann said.

"Due to the war and high military spending, economic issues are also dominating the political campaign," he told CNBC, noting that Israel currently spends around 8% of its gross domestic product on military expenditure. "In addition, nearly every family has a soldier or reservist who has been called up and has left their job for months."

Defense and security are also at the forefront of the election, said Daniel Schatz, a political scientist and former visiting scholar at Harvard, Stanford and Georgetown.

"For Israel, the stakes include restoring confidence in government and deciding how military power translates into lasting security," he told CNBC in an email. Schatz said that while Netanyahu's supporters see the incumbent prime minister as "an experienced leader confronting formidable threats," his critics see failures of preparedness, accountability and strategic direction.
