Growing plants in space? Yes indeed! NASA astronauts are conducting botany experiments aboard the International Space Station (ISS).

In this snapshot — captured by NASA astronaut Jessica Watkins, commander of SpaceX's Crew-13 mission to the ISS — we can see fellow NASA spaceflyer Luke Delaney hard at work aboard the ISS. And while he's orbiting around our planet aboard a laboratory in the vacuum of space, he's also growing plants.

In the photo, Delaney is working petri dishes and experiment modules that support our growing understanding of how plants grow in the microgravity environment aboard the station. Specifically, the experiments Delaney is working on will investigate the genetic responses of plants in this environment.

This is not the first time that researchers have studied plants in space. In fact, ISS astronauts have conducted a wide variety of plant experiments and investigations over the years. They've grown peppers and other edible produce and even proved that moss can survive outside the station, in the harsh environment of space.

When you picture the inside of an orbiting space station, you might imagine the white and gray walls with computers, buttons, switches and other such tech speckling the scene. But the space station is also full of (non-astronaut) life, thanks to the incredible variety of biological experiments onboard.

While space is "the final frontier," and we look outward to study everything from black holes to exploding stars, outer space can also help teach us about own planet. Satellites look back at Earth, studying its changing landforms, migrating wildlife, diminishing glaciers and natural disasters. Onboard the ISS, astronauts conduct experiments with plants, animals, microscopic organisms and an incredible amount of studies supporting human health and medical care. While space might be the final frontier, so much of the work done in space is designed to benefit us back here on Earth.

#588. Albania: Students demand political change and resignation of Prime Minister Edi Rama

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Empty lecture halls, burning buildings and demands for change: Albanian students are spearheading a protest movement that has become the biggest challenge to Prime Minister Edi Rama in years.

The luxury high-rises transforming Tirana's skyline are meant to project the image of a modern European capital. But at the foot of one of these buildings, Samanta, a 22-year-old master's student in economics, is in tears.

She removes the mask she had been wearing to protect herself from tear gas.

"How could I ever afford an apartment in one of these towers on a salary of €540 [$608]?" she shouts, as she stands facing a line of police officers. "I share the rent with people I don't even know because my family has emigrated. We're exhausted."

University lecture halls in Tirana stand empty as students boycott classes and take to the streets, demanding the resignation of Prime Minister Edi Rama, who has been in power since 2013.

Albanians have been protesting since late May as part of a broader anti-government movement known as the "Flamingo Revolution." The protests began in response to a multimillion-euro tourism development linked to Jared Kushner, son-in-law of US President Donald Trump, in the protected coastal area of Zvernec.

The movement has, however, since grown into a broader challenge to Rama's government.

Over the past three days, the student demonstrations have erupted into violent clashes. Bins, businesses, buildings and banks have been set ablaze, with some completely burning out. Tear gas has filled the air.

A total of 152 people have been arrested and 324 are under investigation over the three days of protests. Injuries have been reported among both protesters and police officers, with the police saying 71 officers have been injured in the clashes.

The students are angry about conditions at universities, the cost of higher education and poor employment prospects after graduation. But above all, they want the government to step down. They say they are not willing to negotiate any of their other demands with the prime minister.

"You didn't listen to us for four months while we protested peacefully. Now you'll listen as the city burns," said Samanta.

During the first two days of the student protests, the prime minister remained silent.

Then, at 8:03 a.m. local time on Thursday, Rama appeared in a video message recorded inside the fire-damaged College of Europe building, just meters from his office on Tirana's main street, the Boulevard of the Martyrs of the Nation.

"These are the hands of an opposition that, hiding behind masked people, wants to drag Albania back to a place it will never return to," he declared.

Later that day, Rama addressed the protesters' central demand — his resignation. In comments to Reuters, he ruled out stepping down, saying governments are changed in elections.

The following day, he also accused "foreign hands" of seeking to prevent Albania from joining the European Union.

The unrest comes at a critical moment for Albania's bid to join the EU. Brussels has called for calm and restraint, urging all sides to avoid further escalation and engage in dialogue.

Sali Berisha, the 82-year-old leader of the opposition Democratic Party and a former prime minister, rejected Rama's accusations that the opposition was behind the violence.

"We have no idea whether it's Greece, Iran, Serbia or someone else," he told a press conference, mocking Rama's claims of foreign interference. "The Democratic Party fully supports this protest and will stand by it until the end. Resign and leave immediately!"

For Rigels Xhemollari of the Albanian civil society organization Qendresa Qytetare (Civic Resistance), the student revolt is the culmination of months of peaceful protests.

"The main reason is the prime minister's failure to respond during four months of protests and his mockery of the demonstrators. Other reasons include the economic situation, corruption cases and successive scandals," Xhemollari told DW, referring to a number of corruption investigations involving senior government officials.

But the anger is not directed at Rama's administration alone. Protesters have also voiced their frustration with Albania's political opposition.

"We want the political class of the past 35 years to go. We want a new Albania with new leaders," Skerdi Topalli, a 20-year-old journalism student, told DW.

For political analyst Mark Marku, dean of the Faculty of History and Philology at the University of Tirana, the protests mark a turning point.

"This is the reaction of a generation. In a society that was not responding, they woke people up and made us think about change," he told DW.

But Marku warns that even Rama's resignation and fresh elections would not necessarily bring new political leadership.

"Challenging the government is not an end in itself. A protest must produce a result," he said. "There must be a platform, a political alternative. You cannot vote for a protest."

Xhemollari, meanwhile, sees Rama's resignation as the only way to end the revolt.

"The protest has just one political demand, and it requires a political solution: the prime minister's resignation," he told DW.

Xhemollari says that Albania's president should bring political parties together to form a caretaker government led by a non-partisan prime minister, paving the way for local and parliamentary elections in May 2027.

Dimensions: 3.35 x 4.06-in (85.2 x 103 mm)

As you know, prime lenses come in a wide range of sizes and focal lengths, and every once in a while, one comes along and seems to grab you by the scruff of your neck. One such lens is the Viltrox AF 16mm f/1.8 FE, which is an impressive and useful lens for astrophotography thanks to its wide-angle field of view and fast maximum aperture.

This lens is one of the wider prime lenses that are available and could be one of the best third-party lenses for astrophotography. With a price of just $464 (£533), this is one of the most cost-effective wide-angle primes, but rather than being a second-rate lens, its build quality and image quality are fantastic. It’s not the best, but it’s undoubtedly a lens you would enjoy.

Image quality is excellent overall, with no need for lens corrections, except for vignette removal when shooting wide open. With near-perfect sharpness and minimal distortion and aberrations straight out of the camera, perhaps the weight of the lens is a small price to pay.

The lens is made up of 15 elements in 12 groups and is built like a tank, with an all-metal body. It is pretty heavy, though, weighing in at 19.4 oz (550 g) despite its relatively compact size. Attach this lens to one of the best Sony cameras and you will have a formidable combination. The 16mm f/1.8 is also available in the Nikon Z-mount.

With a focal length of 16mm and a maximum aperture of f/1.8, this is clearly a great lens for astrophotography from a technical point of view. The light-gathering capabilities are excellent, to say the least, and during testing it was possible to shoot at a range of ISO levels with a moonless sky. Even ISO 800 was often high enough for a correct exposure.

The image quality produced by the 16mm f/1.8 is great, with excellent levels of sharpness across the frame. What’s most important here is that even corner sharpness is fantastic when wide open at f/1.8. This is the aperture setting where vignetting is at its strongest, but it reduces drastically when you stop down to f/2.8 and is completely gone by the time you stop down to f/4.

Luckily, though, there is a lens profile in Adobe Camera Raw and Adobe Lightroom that completely removes vignetting. There’s also only minimal chromatic aberration and a tiny amount of barrel distortion, which is impressive given the wide field of view produced by the lens.

As mentioned before, sharpness is great wide open and improves incrementally as you stop down up to the sweet spot of the lens, which is f/8. Sharpness drops slightly at f/11, and it’s at f/16 where diffraction begins to soften the image overall. This increases further when you stop down to f/22.

These latter apertures aren’t relevant to astrophotography; the medium ones aren’t either but the sharpness produced at f/1.8 and f/2.8 is exactly what you want.

The 16mm f/1.8 lens is something of an oddity in one respect: it’s both compact and heavy considering its size, although this comes down to its optical construction. The lens weighs 19.4 oz (550 g) with a size of just 3.35 x 4.06 in (85.2 x 103 mm). It does, however, balance well with Sony A-series cameras.

The 16mm f/1.8 is made up of 15 elements in 12 groups, including four ED glass and three aspherical lenses. The optical construction is designed to deliver sharpness, detail and high contrast with minimal distortion. It’s fair to say it achieves these. Of course, you will experience some perspective distortion when tilting the lens up to capture more of the night sky when there are ground elements in the foreground.

Unlike some wide-angle lenses, the front element isn’t bulbous, and it features an HD nano multilayer coating with a water-resistant and antifouling coating. The filter thread is also just 77mm, so you can easily attach screw-in and square filters if you use them for your astrophotography.

The minimum focusing distance of the lens is 10.6 inches (27 cm), which is great for landscape photographers but less important for astrophotographers.

Build quality is impressive thanks to an all-metal body and weather-sealing. This gives the lens a premium look, and it feels robust in the hand. There’s also a useful digital color screen that displays lens information.

When it comes to controls, there are possibly a few more on this lens than you might find on the average prime. There’s a manual focusing ring that’s wide enough for use when wearing gloves, a manual aperture ring that can be set to stepped and stepless operation using a switch on one side of the lens, two customizable Fn buttons and an AF/MF switch.

The Viltrox AF 16mm f/1.8 FE is a full-frame lens offering a generous 105.6-degree field of view, allowing you to capture more of the night sky. This lens naturally works with all Sony FE bodies, and if you shoot APS-C, it will give you an equivalent focal length of 24mm. This means if you currently own an APS-C camera but plan to upgrade to full-frame, this lens could comfortably join you on that journey.

As prime lenses go, this one has a few more bells and whistles than most, so there are a few more things to talk about than usual. Focusing is internal, so the front element doesn’t rotate or extend outwards, which is great when using filters.

For astrophotography, manual focus is the only way to go, but this lens also has autofocus for when you’re shooting other subjects. The autofocus isn't the fastest we’ve seen, taking about 0.25 seconds to lock onto subjects when the lens is wide open, or 0.5 seconds when stopped down. This is driven by an SMT stepping motor, which is quiet but audible.

One feature that you don’t see on many lenses is a small color digital screen on the top of the lens. This shows the focus distance, aperture setting and the active Fn control. Is it a gimmick? A bit. Is it useful? Yes. For astrophotography, the screen is definitely useful because it’s illuminated.

As with most Viltrox lenses, there’s also a USB-C port on the lens mount so it can be powered. This allows you to connect the lens to the Viltrox Lens app via Bluetooth to update firmware and customize the Fn buttons and the digital screen’s welcome screen. This makes firmware updates incredibly easy, which is a great feature to have.

In online reviews, users consistently highlight the lens's value and image quality. Build quality comes up time and time again, thanks to the all-metal build.

Many users report that overall, the lens offers much more than you’d expect for the price and even more than some more expensive lenses.

The Viltrox AF 16mm f/1.8 FE was tested in combination with the Sony A7R V, which offers an unforgivingly high resolution. The lens was tested at night with a focus on astrophotography, and in daylight to assess sharpness, distortion and general functionality for an all-round approach to testing.

✅ You’re on a budget: The Viltrox AF 16mm f/1.8 FE is less expensive than alternative lenses offering the same focal length and maximum aperture.

✅ You want a wide-angle prime: Thanks to its wide field of view, you can capture more of the night sky, while the fast maximum aperture is ideal for astrophotography.

❌ You would like flexibility: If you would like the flexibility of a variable focal length, a zoom lens is the only way to go. They are typically slower than primes, though.

❌ You'd like a wider view: This is a lovely wide lens, but you can get wider options as both primes and zooms.

Since there are only a few alternatives to the Viltrox AF 16mm f/1.8 FE, and given that it offers excellent value for money, this is absolutely a lens worth considering if you need a wide-angle prime.

Furthermore, you’ll struggle to find a similar alternative with the same balance of overall build quality and price. Image quality is excellent, even in the corners and edges of the frame when shooting wide open with minimal distortion.

The two customizable Fn buttons and the illuminated color screen that provides useful lens information are extremely helpful when shooting in the dark.

With a price of $464 (£533) and given its build quality, image quality and overall handling, this lens represents excellent value for money. And while it may be a full-frame lens, APS-C users can still enjoy a 24mm equivalent focal length, so it’s a useful lens for astrophotography whichever format you shoot with.

This is a professional-quality lens with a mid-range price tag, and like other Viltrox lenses, you get the lens, a lens hood, front and rear lens caps and a soft lens case in the box.

The Viltrox AF 35mm f/1.2 LAB FE is a slightly less wide Viltrox lens with a much faster aperture and is part of the company’s flagship LAB series.

The Sony FE 20mm f/1.8 G is a fantastic wide-angle prime offering excellent image quality in a compact and lightweight form factor.

The Sigma 14mm f/1.4 DG DN Art is something of a beast, both in terms of specs and its size. With a 14mm focal length and f/1.4 maximum aperture, it’s perfect for astrophotography.
