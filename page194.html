#4280. I’ve Spent Years Tracking Nordic Wellness Trends, and This Low-Impact Finnish Ritual Is About to Take Over

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

I can smell the embers of a Nordic trend in the making from miles away. After all, even before the Swedish ethos of lagom (optimal balance) captured the world a few years ago, I’d been unpacking this Nordic mindset around wellness and lifestyle in my storytelling for years. I would go on to write an international bestseller, Lagom, with over 15 foreign editions.

From Swedish massages to Finnish saunas, the region is synonymous with wellbeing. While Denmark gave the world hygge (intentional presence), Norway friluftsliv (open-air living), Finland sisu (grit), and Iceland birthed “Viking wellness” with hot baths and cold dips, the reality is that all Nordic cultures share these values under different names. What makes lifestyle trends from the region particularly appealing globally is the foundations on which they’re built.

From sustainability-focused mindsets and a return to simplicity to curb undue stress, to government-sanctioned free access to nature (think Allemansrätten in Sweden), which fosters symbiotic relationships with the outdoors, and overall accessibility for everyone because they are egalitarian countries. The Nordic communal goal is a focus on holistic well-being that can be sustained over the long term by every individual, from professional athletes in their prime to elders.

The latest Nordic wellness trend, however, has rather practical origins. Ever wonder how cross-country skiers train when there’s no snow? Introducing Nordic walking; not to be confused with hiking with trekking poles.

According to its governing body, the International Nordic Walking Federation (INWA), which was founded in 2000, Nordic walking is now practised in over 40 countries for health, fitness, recreation, social connection, and competitive sport. As early as the 1930s, competitive cross-country skiers used poles with running shoes for summer training. But it was physical education (PE) teacher Leena Jääskeläinen who brought the concept to the general public in 1966, making it the first documented use of ski poles outside competitive training.

“I started Nordic walking in 1998 because it is a fun, social and efficient form of physical activity,” shares Aki Karihtala, president of INWA. “It takes people back to nature.” At the time, he headed the Sports division at Finnish Sporting goods company Exel, the world's largest cross-country ski pole manufacturer.

It was Karihtala himself, alongside his team at Exel, who coined the term Nordic walking, which has been widely adopted “regardless of age, religion and culture, because there are no boundaries,” notes Karihtala, highlighting its accessibility. In 1997, Exel designed the first Nordic Walker® poles specifically for the activity. A year later, over 500,000 Finns, in a population of roughly five million at the time, had tried Nordic walking. It's said to be especially effective because it engages the whole body while reducing pressure on your knees with ergonomically designed poles. The federation developed its progressive INWA 10 Step Method™ to teach people the simple technique safely and effectively.

Finnish trend and branding expert Katja Presnal is a newer convert to Nordic walking. “I’ve studied trends for over 20 years,” shares Presnal. Her personal lifestyle digital platform was read by over 100,000 monthly readers and millions annually. The irony of Nordic wellness being the hottest lifestyle trend at the moment isn’t lost on Presnal, who grew up in the Turku archipelago surrounded by bays and lush forests. According to Presnal, after two decades of travelling, she stopped “preaching trends” and started “walking the walk” by purchasing a 100-year-old former dairy tucked away in the Finnish countryside for a slower, more intentional way of life surrounded by nature, lakes, and walking trails.

“It was about making everyday life a wellness regimen and creating my own 365-day retreat,” adds Presnal, noting that these were the type of slow living wellness experiences her family would seek in places like Tuscany while travelling. “So I have, in essence, returned to my Nordic wellness roots, which in hindsight was the richness of my childhood.”

The low-threshold accessibility, from a physical and financial standpoint, is what makes Nordic Walking perfect right now for newbies like Presnal. Another benefit Presnal notes is connectivity with people.

“People are now discovering Nordic lifestyles,” explains Presnal. “You don't recognise your Nordic way of living as something special that could become a global trend or could be taught and shared with somebody because it's just a part of who you are.”

#4289. 7 NYC Hotels That Make Christmas Magical for Families

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

There's nothing quite like New York at Christmas. When I lived in the city with my family, December was always our favorite month. We'd bundle up to see the windows on Fifth Avenue, drink hot chocolate while watching the skaters at Bryant Park, and crane our necks at the tree in Rockefeller Center. The whole city feels magical this time of year, and kids pick up on it the second they step outside.

My family and I just moved to Barcelona, but we're heading back to NYC for the holidays. And since we'll be visitors this time, I started looking for hotels that would make the trip feel extra special for my kids. It turns out that many of the city's most popular properties go big for Christmas—and plenty have kids in mind when it comes to programming. Some will set up a decorated tree in your room before you check in; others will bring Santa to the lobby, pour free hot cocoa, or hide candy canes around your room for a scavenger hunt.

After a cold afternoon of skating and window shopping, it's nice to come back to a place that feels like Christmas too. Here are the seven hotels that make Christmas special in NYC, and what each one offers for families.

Anyone who grew up on Home Alone 2 will want to know about this one. Check into The Plaza (don't worry, it's not closing for renovation until January 2027) for its Home Alone: Fun in New York package, which gets you a four-hour private limo tour of filming locations, a large cheese pizza to eat on the way, and a 16-scoop sundae sent up to your room (Kevin would approve). You can also request a seven-foot Christmas tree for your suite, which will come with holiday flowers and welcome treats. With younger kids, book Holiday Afternoon Tea at the Palm Court or Storytime with Santa, available to hotel guests only.

Picture this: You've spent the afternoon admiring the Fifth Avenue windows, everyone's cheeks are rosy from the winter air, and you step into a lobby glowing with holiday decor by Patrick J. Clayton Productions. On Fridays and Saturdays in December, things at the Regency get even sweeter: from 4 to 6 p.m., the hotel serves free hot cocoa for the kids, and Champagne for you. Kids can also join the Loews Little Legends program, which turns the whole stay into a citywide adventure. One minute they're high-fiving a bellman, the next they're hunting for squirrels in Central Park—the whole time earning stamps toward a special prize. Before you head home, stop by the concierge desk for the hotel's hand-painted ornament, a sweet souvenir to hang on your tree back home.

Most NYC holiday activities are in Midtown, so staying at the Conrad in Battery Park City makes for a nice change of scene. The hotel has done a big lobby display every year since 2013, and this year's centerpiece is a 20-foot red spruce with more than 300 blue, silver, and white ornaments, surrounded by 22 flocked trees and snowflake lights projected around the lobby (you can add an in-room tree, too). Santa comes every now and then, carolers stop by, and kids can write letters to the North Pole and hit the hot chocolate station. Bigger families can grab a two- or three-bedroom suite, or the suite with a Statue of Liberty view. What better gift than the ability to marvel at Lady Liberty from your room?

The Ritz-Carlton's Ultimate Rockefeller Holiday Experience is basically a classic New York Christmas checklist in one booking. You get VIP access to the Rockefeller Center rink with a private chalet to change and warm up in, a trip up to Top of the Rock, and an up-close family photo under that iconic tree. (If you've ever tried to get a decent picture there on a December weekend, you'll appreciate that last one.) The hotel sits on Central Park South, so the park is right across the street when the kids need room to run.

Park Lane is another Central Park South option, and the park views are the main draw—that, plus the decorations that go up the week before Thanksgiving. For families, there are connecting rooms and a new Central Park Panoramic Studio Suite with a sofa bed so that the kids can sleep in the same room as you. The perk I'm most excited about is a holiday shopping package with Bergdorf Goodman. Whatever you buy gets delivered to your room so that you can shop Fifth Avenue with a free hand for your kid.

The Knickerbocker, at 42nd and Broadway, has two holiday packages built around an in-room tree. The first, A Little Christmas Magic, gets you a four-foot, pre-lit tree with a skirt, set up before you arrive. A Little More Christmas Magic adds decorations around the room, holiday touches in the bathroom, and stockings you can fill for Christmas morning. (My son would love it.) Bryant Park's Christmas market and ice rink are a block away, which is handy when everyone needs to burn off some energy before bed and you need to do some last-minute shopping.

#3738. Pluralistic: Critique and writing (23 Oct 2026)

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Pluralistic: Daily links from Cory Doctorow

Through my own writing life, nothing has done more to improve my writing than diligent participation in peer-based workshops; that's where a group of writers, all at about the same stage in their artistic development, gather to read one another's work and offer feedback specifically aimed at improving each other's stories.

This is a deceptively simple exercise, and the reasons it works so well are quite counter-intuitive. The most obvious benefit is the way that a workshop encourages you to write at all. When you're at the start of your writing career, you are almost totally reliant upon your own internal self-discipline to keep producing.

Acquiring that self-discipline is infinitely valuable, but it's also incredibly hard. Being part of a group of writers who expect you to regularly turn in new work adds a very useful bit of external motivation that augments your still nascent self-discipline. Knowing you have to turn in a story for the group by Friday can get you in gear when you feel discouraged or blocked.

Before you're getting published regularly (or at all), your critiquing circle might be the only source of validation you get for your work, so the knowledge that you're in for that validation if you get that story in on time is a great motivator. But this is where the counter-intuitive part comes in: as great as it feels to be the center of your peers' attention and to get close feedback on the work you sweated over, that's not the real value you get from a writing workshop, at least not at the start of your career.

Your peers' critiques can help you fix weaknesses in your story, and even teach you a little about how to avoid those weaknesses in future work. You might get feedback that turns an unpublishable story into a published one. But the point of a writing workshop isn't merely to make your stories better – it's to make you better at writing stories.

That's where critiquing other writers' stories comes in. The process of reading and re-reading someone else's imperfect work and devoting real mental energy to figuring out what's not working in it and how to fix it is how you develop the creative instincts that allow you to automatically and unconsciously avoid pitfalls and make breakthroughs in your own work.

Even if none of the feedback you offer is ever incorporated into the story you're critiquing, the act of preparing and delivering that critique is the most reliable fast-track to becoming a better writer yourself. There's something very valuable in the combination of thinking critically, being exposed to other workshop members' ideas about the story under discussion, and working with source material that you can look at with more objectivity because it didn't come out of your own head.

I bring all this up because I've now heard from a few beginning writers who tell me that they're finding LLMs to be good editorial feedback generators. They value the editorial suggestions they get from a chatbot after they finish their work, and use it to help them with a new draft. Without commenting on whether I think LLM feedback is editorially useful (I certainly wouldn't ever ask a chatbot for that kind of feedback), I want to point out that when it comes to improving your writing, getting editorial notes on your work is distinctly inferior to offering criticism of other writers' work.

The reality is that when you're starting out as a writer, the vast majority of work you produce will be artistically unsalvageable. The much-vaunted "million words of crap" you write on the way to becoming a publishable writer are about learning to marry technique to artistic vision and to sharpen both. Getting editorial feedback that helps you shore up the weakest spots in a story that is ultimately never published and constitutes a learning exercise has value, but it has far less value than applying the things you've learned from making and fixing those mistakes to your next story.

With vanishingly few exceptions, a commercially and artistically successful writer isn't someone who writes one great story or novel: a writing career is defined by a body of work, which means that you have to do more than rewrite. You have to write the next thing, and the next thing, and the next.

Having an editor-on-demand of highly variable (and often dubious) quality might help you improve your writing, but it's no substitute for participating in a peer workshop. The idea that an LLM might be able to stand in for a workshop is grounded in the fallacy that the point of a workshop is to have people tell you how to fix your story, rather than analyzing other people's writing in order to develop your own critical skills.

Look: it's a cliche to say "every writer is different," but I really believe that every writer is different, and that's down to the legibility of the writing process to other writers. If you're a painter or sculptor or other visual artist, you've likely spent many hours in a studio with many other practitioners while all of you work from the same life model or still life. If you're a musician, you've almost certainly played music with other musicians. If you're a dancer, you've definitely danced with other dancers.

But writers get precious little exposure to other writers' process. At best we might look at each other's drafts. A musician can listen to every note another musician produces while riffing or composing a new work or interpreting a work. Writers don't get to watch words emerge from each other's fingertips. Being observed that closely while you write would be ghastly and paralyzingly invasive.

I've taught a lot of writers over the years and I've had many discussions with my peers about their writing process, and I'm here to tell you that pretty much every writer finds every other writer's process baffling. We each of us invent an idiosyncratic process for ourselves that will never fully work for anyone else.

So if you value editorial feedback from a chatbot (ideally, a local one running on your computer and not one of the "foundation models" that are torching the planet and elevating the worst people on earth to positions of political, technical and commercial prominence), that's fine, you do you.

But even if that's the case, I still want you to know that whatever you're getting out of the chatbot is fundamentally different, and (I believe) much less useful than participating in a peer workshop.

That's what I wanted to say today, but I predict that an appreciable number of readers are about to email me to ask how these "peer workshops" work. There's a ton of variability in how these are conducted, but the basic shape of them is that in advance of each session, one or more writers submits a finished work to the other writers, who then read through the manuscript at least twice, making notes that include both minor copyedits and broader, thematic notes. They may write a brief editorial response as well, ranging from a paragraph to a couple of pages.

Then the workshop convenes. Each writer briefly expresses their views about the story under consideration, focusing on those thematic and structural notes on what worked and didn't work in the story, with suggestions for improving the latter. You don't need to point out every typo – they're noted on the manuscript. Critiquers don't need to repeat one another. If another workshop member says the thing you were planning to say, you can just say "Ditto what so-and-so said about whatever." If you have nothing to add when your turn comes about, it's fine to pass.

Those are pretty much universal characteristics of a peer workshop. Here are some variations that I am dogmatically committed to, but which other workshoppers might disagree with:

The subject of each session must not speak during the critiques, except to answer direct questions that are not related to the writing itself (e.g., "I couldn't figure out what word this was supposed to be – can you clarify?"). The subject can speak after all the other writers have spoken.

Each person in the critiquing circle speaks once, in turn, without being interrupted. No cross-talk allowed. It is useful to set a time-limit for critiques.

No partial manuscripts. It's not really possible to critique a story unless you have the complete tale. Novels can be workshopped as three chapters and an outline.

Stories should be distributed in advance and read in advance. Don't have writers read their work aloud for critique, as this can lead to critiquing the delivery rather than the material.

Critique the story, not the writer. It's fine to be offended by something in the story, but not OK to accuse your workshopmates of being bad people for having offended you.

Discourage resubmission of revised stories. Workshopping produces sharply diminishing returns. If you really want feedback on a rewrite, approach people individually and ask if they'll read the new draft.

Any group of three or more writers can start a workshop. You'll need to locate writers who are at about the same stage in their artistic and professional lives as you, and find a physical place (or a videoconference service) where you can meet. Library meeting rooms are a favorite, and so is rotating between workshop members' homes. Set a regular schedule – weekly, fortnightly, monthly, possibly quarterly – and stick to it.

#3752. Call of Duty Modern Warfare 4 Review: A Dolphin Dive in the Right Direction

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Our gifting experts are obsessively searching for holiday gifts that are unique, thoughtful and suitable for any budget.

Our expert, award-winning staff selects the products we cover and rigorously researches and tests our top picks. If you buy through our links, we may earn a commission.

The final Modern Warfare is a fitting swansong that rights the wrongs of previous outings.

The Call of Duty series needed a win this year. Last year’s Black Ops 7 was a clown car of bad decisions, featuring an always-online campaign mode with ridiculous zombie bosses and open-world Warzone. Worse still, the multiplayer mode (the only reason that many people buy the game) was entirely forgettable. Developer Treyarch was tasked with putting out Call of Duty games two years in a row — that crunch was a recipe for disaster.

On the other hand, that means Modern Warfare developer Infinity Ward had plenty of time to cook up the fourth and final entry in its own series. Thankfully, the studio used that extra runway to its advantage. Modern Warfare 4 may not have returned Call of Duty to the highest highs of the original trilogy that ended in 2009 (and which Infinity Ward rebooted over the last decade), but this is a leap in the right direction for a series that has become increasingly sloppy in recent years.

Players will find plenty of cinematic action in the single-player campaign, and the multiplayer mode has slowed down just a touch, which I think the game largely benefits from.

More importantly, Modern Warfare Zombies has been completely dropped in favor of DMZ, an accessible player-versus-player-versus-AI extraction shooter experience. The experimental swing feels like a lifeline for the series, and DMZ is the standout mode for the game.

Modern Warfare 4 still suffers from some of Infinity Ward’s worst impulses, including open-world campaign sections, but this is still one of the better modern Call of Duty games. If this is a formula that works for you, MW4 executes it pretty darn well.

Modern Warfare 4 is a tighter game than Infinity Ward’s previous project, and it’s leagues better than Black Ops 7.

At the same time, it has a complicated campaign to crack open (and not because the plot is hard to follow). Players flit back and forth between the perspective of Captain John Price, longtime protagonist of the series, and South Korean soldier Park Hyun-Jun, a newcomer whose squad is fighting for his homeland. A battle rages against a united coalition of Russian and North Korean ultranationalists, who are blazing a path into other territories.

Russian bad actors have been the primary antagonists of every Modern Warfare game, and it’s actually really cool to see South Korea’s positive representation in a Call of Duty title. But the propaganda is laid on thick — Russia and North Korea are presented as sneering colonialist powers, while the Western world’s soldiers are a shield on every front.

Americans spearhead the fight against imperialism; forget about the Strait of Hormuz and gun down these unambiguously bad guys. The whole plot hinges on a somehow less-subtle Red Dawn-type of scenario, and the gung-ho military politics are absolutely nauseating this time around. Sure, Price is framed as morally gray — he’s working with a cartel to achieve his goals, and there’s boring melodrama where he comes to blows with his former allies — but he’s vindicated in the end because he is the type of Call of Duty leader that prevents global conflict.

Modern Warfare 4 also features an obligatory shockingly graphic mission, which is something of a tradition for the series. In it, you play as a North Korean soldier rounding up and executing the occupants of a small village for hiding a defector. During my playthrough, I earned an achievement for successfully interrogating a villager without beating them down. The whole affair is gratuitous, gross and gamified in a problematic way, exacerbated by the bizarre decision to continue making this kind of mission a mainstay in each game.

It’s a shame that the story is so trite and icky because the tentpole action is really good. The best mission takes place early in the campaign, when Price infiltrates a New York nightclub to search for a Russian oligarch. Bright lights and pulsing music contrast with brutal fistfights and gunplay — it’s like playing through the climax of a John Wick film. From there, the highlights keep coming: There’s a fight in a train yard where the cover moves on rails, a chase scene across Mumbai rooftops, a high-stakes nighttime penthouse raid and a full-out assault on a dictator’s compound.

But the worst mission in the campaign repeats the mistakes of other modern Call of Duty games. When the player’s squad initially counterattacks North Korean airspace, they’re shot down and have to survive behind enemy lines with scavenged gear. It’s an interesting setup that is completely wasted on a Warzone-like, open-world mission with a checklist of more than 50 optional collectibles to seek out.

The few puzzles that exist are mind-numbingly easy — despite my best efforts, I checked out after completing around half of the activities. I focused on the main objective to end the mission as soon as possible, which is just about the worst feeling to have while playing a game.

I don’t know if there’s an edict from up top to put one of these open-world missions in each new game to funnel players into Warzone, but they don’t work at all and are the worst part of any campaign.

Still, the folks that play the campaign will probably have a good time here. Players can explore tons of cool locales, and the enemies are surprisingly difficult this time around. Still, if you care about the loose narrative implications of a Call of Duty campaign, it’s tough to wash the bad taste out of your mouth.

DMZ, a fairly accessible extraction shooter game mode, returns with new content for the first time since its “beta” ended in 2023. Players have been mourning what could have been for years, but it seems like the developers at Infinity Ward have been tinkering with it in the background for quite some time.

And their hard work has paid off because I’m confident that DMZ is the standout mode for Modern Warfare 4.

This extraction shooter is perhaps simpler than even ARC Raiders, but that’s part of the appeal. Players drop into North Korea to loot, fight each other and take down key targets throughout the map.

Everything is streamlined and easy to understand, including the loot. During play, you will come across several types of scrap, an assortment of healing items and tons of weapons, so you don’t have to think too hard about what to grab. Just move, shoot and pull out high-value items, then run the loop again.

The enemy AI is surprisingly aggressive and fun to battle against, especially when you run into an overwhelming number of troops. Armored vehicles and helicopters will try to smear you into the dirt. It’s an adrenaline rush, and as far as I’m concerned, Infinity Ward nailed the really important part of DMZ. It’s tough to get the player-versus-AI balance right, but these enemies feel like a real threat.

Other players might also come for your loot, which is what sometimes puts folks off of the extraction genre altogether. But DMZ’s in-depth bounty system provides a foil to player-versus-player gameplay.

Folks who hunt down other players rack up a bounty and appear on the map, so pacifists have a better chance to steer clear while bounty hunters stalk them down. It’s a pretty genius compromise that encourages the player-versus-player enjoyers to butt heads while the player-versus-environment crowd hides away because the tension of a potential run-in with aggressors is never truly alleviated.

I’m concerned that the pared-down simplicity of the gameplay loop could hurt DMZ’s long-term success, but Infinity Ward seems committed to adding new world bosses. That could really shake up how matches play out, even if there’s nothing as over-the-top as Marathon’s Cryo Archive boss.

DMZ isn’t perfect, but I see the groundwork laid for one of the better extraction shooters I’ve played. Depending on how the development team builds on this game mode, I can see it becoming something special (and maybe even a Call of Duty mainstay for years to come).

Call me an oldhead, I don’t care: Black Ops 7’s omnimovement system, which allowed players to run, jump and slide around while looking in any direction, was disastrous for Call of Duty game balance.

Treyarch’s update to the formula might work for massive Warzone maps where there’s plenty of open space and a higher time-to-kill for other players, but the smaller multiplayer maps become impossible to breathe on. Call of Duty is, first and foremost, an arcade shooter, but the multiplayer formula still works best when there’s time to recoup and recover, make tactical flanks and call in your scorestreaks.

Infinity Ward’s Modern Warfare 4 removes the omnimovement system, but it still allows players to sprint, slide and dolphin dive around at appreciably high speeds. The new mantling mechanic adds some traversal options, but everyone feels a little heavier.

The slightly slower six-versus-six matches feel great, and the larger modes are a bit less chaotic. The Modern Warfare games have always skewed closer to classic boots-on-the-ground Call of Duty, and the latest entry doesn’t disappoint in that regard.

Still, the fatal flaws of the modern Call of Duty formula have gone untouched. There remain more than 70 attachments for many weapons. Many modes have maps cut out of larger Warzone areas. The seemingly mandatory 24/7 Shipment playlist is being added soon to prey upon your nostalgia. Progression needs to synergize across a complete Modern Warfare 4 product, so unnecessary bloat exists everywhere you look.

This is a solid modern Call of Duty game, but the overall quality is still a far cry from the series’ heyday. In a post-Warzone world, this is about as good as it gets — but if that formula doesn’t work for you, it might be worth steering clear of Modern Warfare 4.

#4149. Scientists Built a 3D Cloak That Makes Objects Almost Invisible to Thermal Cameras

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

An intricate metal-and-rubber lattice steers heat around objects, concealing their presence from thermal detection.

An object doesn’t have to be visible to give away its location. Sometimes, the way it disrupts the flow of heat is enough to reveal that something is hiding there, much to the despair of camouflaged soldiers who have to endure thermal vision drones on the battlefield. Now, engineers have developed a device that can make that thermal signature almost disappear (although sadly it won’t work for infantry).

Researchers at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and the Technical University of Denmark have built a three-dimensional thermal cloak that redirects heat around hidden objects, leaving the surrounding temperature pattern nearly undisturbed. Unlike earlier designs restricted to simpler shapes or only blocking infrared in a single plane, their cloak can accommodate complex geometries and work regardless of the direction of heat flow.

The breakthrough could eventually help protect sensitive electronics, improve heat management in computer chips, and potentially conceal equipment from certain forms of thermal detection.

There’s a catch, however. This isn’t the kind of invisibility cloak that could make a person disappear from a thermal camera. The device redirects heat flowing through solid materials, whereas the human body continuously generates its own heat and emits infrared radiation into the surrounding environment

Heat naturally flows from warmer regions to cooler ones. But if you place an obstacle in the gradient’s path, then the heat flow changes. A poorly conducting object, for instance, forces heat to spread around it, creating distortions that betray its presence.

A thermal cloak must do two things simultaneously: steer heat around the hidden object and restore the original temperature pattern on the other side.

Researchers have pursued this idea for years. In 2014, scientists in Singapore demonstrated a three-dimensional thermal cloak that concealed an air bubble inside metal. More recently, a 2024 study by some of the same researchers produced intricate thermal cloaks, heat rotators and concentrators, primarily using two-dimensional designs.

The challenge was making the effect work around genuinely complex three-dimensional objects, regardless of the direction of heat flow.

Please check your inbox and confirm your subscription.

“A real thermal cloak should work no matter where the heat comes from,” said Shelly Zhang, an engineering professor at Illinois.

“Our device can hide a complex 3D object in an infinite number of directions while keeping the temperature inside stable and protected.”

To accomplish this, Zhang and her colleagues developed a lattice made from tiny intersecting aluminum bars, surrounded by a rubber-like material called polydimethylsiloxane.

Aluminum conducts heat readily; the rubber conducts it poorly. So by changing the thickness and orientation of the metal bars in three dimensions, the researchers could control precisely where heat traveled.

They used mathematical models to calculate the required properties throughout the cloak, then translated those figures into a continuous structure that could actually be manufactured.

The researchers used metal 3D printing to manufacture the aluminum framework, then cast the rubber-like material into the remaining spaces.

To test their design, they created an apple-shaped core concealed inside a pear-shaped shell. They placed the completed specimen between two aluminum plates. One plate was heated to 40 degrees Celsius (104 degrees Fahrenheit), while the other was cooled with iced water.

After an hour, an infrared camera recorded the temperature distributions across the specimen.

Outside the cloak, the temperature bands remained nearly straight and evenly spaced, as though the hidden core were absent. Inside, temperatures varied much less, indicating that relatively little heat crossed the protected region.

The team also fabricated and successfully tested a different, heart-shaped cloak.

But perhaps the most striking demonstration involved human faces. The researchers designed a lattice that could conceal a detailed face-shaped object, complete with glasses, inside another face-shaped structure.

The researchers even 3D-printed intricate, face-shaped cloaks, but only tested their ability to hide heat using computer simulations, not physical experiments. The cloak works by redirecting heat around an object, not by hiding heat produced by the object itself. That means it can’t yet conceal a person or other heat-generating object from a thermal camera.

Still, the work suggests that engineers could eventually route heat around delicate electronic components or maintain more stable temperatures in demanding environments.

“Any field that needs precise control of heat or needs to protect something from being detected thermally could benefit from this work,” Zhang said.

The 3D thermal cloak was described in the journal Nature Communications.

#4157. A Tourist Spotted Strange Footprints in Thailand. They Were 120 Million Years Old, Made by a Giant Dinosaur

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

A Facebook photo led researchers to six ancient footprints left by a giant carnivorous dinosaur.

Pichai Prathetsena was exploring a forest stream with his family in northeastern Thailand when he noticed something peculiar in the rock beneath his feet. Three long toes seemed to emerge from the stone, forming an impression far too large for any known land animal.

Curious, he photographed the strange markings and posted them in a Thai Facebook group called “What is this creature?”

It didn’t take long to get an answer. Pichai had discovered an extremely rare dinosaur footprint.

On October 20, just three days after Prathetsena’s discovery, Thailand’s Department of Mineral Resources confirmed that the markings were 120-million-year-old dinosaur footprints. And there wasn’t just one. Investigators found six impressions forming a continuous trail left by a large, meat-eating dinosaur.

The discovery, made in the Phu Hin Chom That–Phu Phrabat National Park area of Udon Thani province, is now officially the first confirmed carnivorous dinosaur tracksite in the province.

The footprints belong to a theropod, a group of predominantly carnivorous dinosaurs that walked on two legs and included formidable predators such as Tyrannosaurus rex. The exact species, however, cannot be determined from the footprints alone.

Each three-toed impression measures approximately 29 centimeters (11 inches) long and 26 centimeters (10 inches) wide. The tracks are preserved in fine-grained sandstone belonging to the Phu Phan Formation, which dates to the Early Cretaceous period.

Based on their dimensions and spacing, researchers estimate the animal stood approximately 1.42 meters (4.7 feet) tall at the hip and measured 6.3 meters (21 feet) from head to tail — about as long as a large delivery van, with hips nearly as high as the roof of a typical car.

Please check your inbox and confirm your subscription.

The footprints were separated by about one meter (aprox. three feet), with a stride length of approximately 2.2 meters, according to Thailand’s Department of National Parks.

Local paleontologists compared the animal’s estimated size with Siamotyrannus isanensis, a large carnivorous dinosaur previously discovered in Thailand. However, the exact dinosaur species remains undeterminable.

The ancient landscape also looked rather different when the footprints were made in mud, and then some sudden event preserved them, leaving the impression locked in hard minerals over the course of millions of years. Investigators believe the area was once a waterside environment with relatively gentle flowing water.

Unlike fossilized bones, which tell you what a dinosaur looked like, footprints offer paleontologists evidence of prehistoric animals in motion. Their shape and spacing can help reconstruct how dinosaurs walked, how quickly they moved and, when multiple trackways occur together, potentially whether they traveled in groups.

Thailand has a surprisingly rich history of such discoveries. The country’s first documented dinosaur footprints emerged in the 1980s at Phu Luang Wildlife Sanctuary. That site preserves 15 three-toed impressions attributed to large theropods, according to a 2026 scientific review of Thailand’s prehistoric footprints.

At the moment, authorities are most concerned with protecting the trackway. The Thai government has temporarily closed the discovery area while specialists assess how to prevent damage from visitors and water erosion.

The goal is to ultimately develop the location into an educational and paleontological tourism site.
