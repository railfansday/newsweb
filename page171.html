#260. Split Tunneling Is Your VPN’s Best Feature. Here’s How to Make the Most of It

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

While you may well be familiar with virtual private network apps, you might not have come across one of the best features available inside these privacy protectors: a feature called split tunneling.

In simple terms, split tunneling lets you choose which apps or sites use the protection of a VPN and which don't. While VPNs are worth using for extra security and privacy online—foiling trackers, spoofing your location, and stopping your internet provider from seeing your browsing—they do by their nature come with some compromises in speed.

If, for example, you want to get the fastest possible speeds with your online games, you might want to leave your games outside of the VPN's remit. Likewise, since VPNs typically obfuscate your actual location, if you want to make sure you can always check movie showtimes in your local area, you can use split tunneling to keep those ticketing apps outside your VPN. Putting a VPN in place can also interfere with access to other devices on your local network, like smart home gadgets and printers.

For all these reasons, you might want the extra control that split tunneling gives you, and it's available in a lot of VPN packages. Here's what you need to know about it.

VPNs work by offering an encrypted bridge for data going to and coming from the web. This bridge means your internet provider, law enforcement agencies, and people connected to the same Wi-Fi can't see what you're doing online. Split tunneling lets you route some online traffic away from that bridge, without you having to constantly turn your VPN on and off.

App-based split tunneling is the most common type you'll come across, as it's the most straightforward and easiest to understand. Some of your apps use the VPN, and some don't. So maybe you want your banking app connected to a VPN for maximum protection, but you're less concerned about a weather app, and you don't want to see forecasts from whatever location the VPN is spoofing.

Depending on the VPN, you might also see URL-based split tunneling. This lets you set the rules based on domain names. It's the same idea as with apps: For websites that need fast internet access and where privacy is less important, there's no VPN. So you can keep the VPN turned on for a search engine page where you're typing information you want protected, but off for a local news site.

There are other types of VPN tunneling too, including route-based tunneling (based on specific IP addresses on the internet) and policy-based tunneling (essentially a set of overarching rules), but these are less common in consumer VPN software, and you're unlikely to come across them unless you're somehow managing other users.

It's not a complicated technology to understand, and it can be genuinely useful—but it's also a feature that users tend not to notice or think about when they're enabling and disabling VPNs on their devices.

The exact steps needed to enable split tunneling will vary depending on the VPN software you're using, but they shouldn't be too difficult to find. Most, but not all, VPN programs now offer some kind of split tunneling feature. If you're using a popular app, then you should find the option somewhere, especially if you're on a paid tier.

To give you some idea how it works: In ProtonVPN for Windows, on the main console there's a split tunneling link on the right, together with other features like a kill switch and port forwarding. Click Split tunneling, and once it's enabled, you can start picking your apps. You can either select a list of apps to be included by the VPN (with everything else excluded) or a list of apps to be excluded (with everything else included).

For NordVPN on Windows, you can access the feature by clicking the cog icon in the bottom left of the main interface, then choosing Split tunneling and enabling the Split tunneling toggle switch. Again, you'll be able to choose which apps are included in the VPN protections and which ones aren't.

On mobile, it's a similar process: Delve into the settings for whatever VPN app you're using, wherever those settings might be. Note, though, that iPhones don't allow as much freedom for VPNs here as Android devices and desktop platforms: You'll typically be limited to selecting websites and IP addresses to exclude, rather than apps.

Most of the time, it's going to be a set-it-and-forget-it type routine, and you're not going to need to keep diving back into the options screen to change around the apps that have VPN access—though you can if you want. It means VPN peace of mind where you need it, without the inconvenience of having a VPN applied where it's not useful.

#258. Three things you should know about permafrost

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

A primer on this crucial and frankly mysterious piece of climate science.

Maybe, like me, you’ve been aware that this layer of frozen soil is thawing, that it’s important in the global puzzle of climate change … and that’s about the extent of it.

The great news for both of us is that my colleague James Temple just published two in-depth stories about permafrost. His reporting took him to the boreal forests of Alaska, where he helicoptered into the wilderness to learn more about how this material could affect our climate both now and in the future.

I’d recommend you give both of James’s stories a read. There’s this feature about efforts to tackle the intensifying fires that are threatening permafrost, as well as this look at how researchers are trying to understand the complicated tangle of potential climate effects. Here are three things I took away.

Permafrost is the frozen layer of soil that’s under about 15% of land around the Northern Hemisphere. While technically material needs to be frozen for only two consecutive years to count as permafrost, most of our current permafrost formed during the last ice age.

That means there’s organic material that’s been frozen, and essentially locked away, for thousands of years. But as global temperatures tick up and this layer thaws, microbes are able to break down that organic matter, releasing carbon dioxide and methane—powerful greenhouse gases that drive climate change. Those emissions form a feedback loop, accelerating warming further.

The steady rise in global temperatures has already shrunk the footprint of the Arctic permafrost by about 12% since 1850, and the pace has been picking up in the last 50 years.

The wild thing is, only a minority of the models that have been used in UN climate reports actually take into account the gradual loss of permafrost. And none of them include the abrupt thaw and collapse that can happen after events like heat waves and fires (more on those fires in a moment). There are efforts to make sure future UN reports reflect the growing science, but for now, all this means that we’re probably underestimating how much and how fast the planet is going to warm.

Fire is a natural part of boreal forests’ life cycle, but they’re getting more intense because of climate change. And crucially, fires can accelerate the thawing of permafrost. By the end of the century, this effect could release the equivalent of 80 billion metric tons of carbon dioxide.

Today, the policy across remote parts of Canada and Alaska is generally to let wildfires burn as long as they’re not threatening human habitats. But some researchers say we should explore fighting these fires to save the carbon trapped in the forests and the permafrost beneath them.

Researchers at the Woodwell Climate Research Center are developing a map of burn risk and carbon vulnerability across Alaska, essentially highlighting areas where fires are most likely to spread and which areas are home to certain types of permafrost that would release the most emissions. (Yes, apparently there are different types of permafrost. Yedoma is a particularly deep, carbon-dense form from the late Pleistocene era.)

That’s all just the beginning—once we figure out potential targets, the question is what to do about it. Some suggest setting more prescribed fires, something Indigenous groups have done for thousands of years, partly to reduce the danger of massive blazes. Or resources could go to early detection and efforts to put out fires before they grow.

There’s about 1.5 trillion tons of carbon locked away in permafrost, about twice the amount in the atmosphere. We know that permafrost thawing can release greenhouse-gas emissions and drive climate change. But there are complications.

One particularly big wrinkle: Other effects from permafrost thawing and fire recovery might counteract the carbon lost from those events. For example, as temperatures warm, plants can gradually move north and take hold where they couldn’t have before. Those plants soak up carbon from the atmosphere as they grow, at least partly locking away what was emitted.

Researchers are working to better understand this ecosystem. And the more we learn, the more complicated the picture looks. How much carbon is lost from permafrost, and how much a given area will recover its carbon, depend on a huge array of factors. How severe are the fires that have accelerated local thawing? What’s the local climate and environment? And what kind of permafrost is at stake?

The higher we push the planet’s temperature, the more we mess with complicated natural phenomena that we’re only beginning to understand.

#256. Hackers find a way to turn users own AI agents and Downloads folders against them

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Your Downloads folder probably contains a few PDFs, forgotten screenshots, and several files named some variation of "final." But according to a recent report by security company Forever, it could also contain instructions telling your AI assistant to install malware.

In an Oct. 22 blog post, the company reported that they caught an AI coding assistant following those instructions on a customer's computer. The company blocked the installation, then traced the request to a text file in Downloads.

How does a text file get that kind of power? Tools like OpenAI's Codex and Anthropic's Claude Code can, depending on their permissions, edit files, run commands, and install software. Developers leave them instruction files called AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md to explain how to work on a project. If you're building a website, those notes might tell the assistant which fonts to use or what to test before finishing.

An attacker can leave instructions, too. In this case, the file told the assistant to install "function-color," a software package linked to the MALFEX malware campaign documented by Checkmarx.

Forever didn’t establish how the file reached the customer's computer, but its researchers demonstrated one possible route. Depending on browser settings, a website can drop a file into Downloads without asking for approval. If someone then launches their coding assistant in that folder, it could read the file and follow its orders.

When Forever tested the customer's file on an isolated computer, it said Codex downloaded and ran the malware. It also changed settings that could make the attacker’s software load in later sessions.

"Once it is written, every Codex session on the machine loads the attacker’s server at startup — in any folder, any project," the researchers wrote. "You can leave the folder. The poison stays."

The team separately devised a test targeting Claude Code. The attack worked about half the time, redirecting Claude's traffic to a server the researchers controlled. That exposed a login token and allowed them to send commands back for the assistant to execute, illustrating the risk of giving an assistant access to your computer when it can mistake a stranger's instructions for its own assignment.

Researchers have pulled off similar tricks before, showing what AI can really do in the wrong hands. In March 2025, Pillar Security researchers got AI coding assistants to slip harmful code into a project by hiding malicious instructions in their coding rules. In April 2026, NVIDIA’s researchers used a poisoned AGENTS.md file to get Codex to sabotage software and conceal the change. This time, though, Forever says it caught an actual attacker trying the trick on a customer's computer. Yikes.

Forever recommends keeping coding assistants out of your Downloads folder and checking unfamiliar AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md files before letting an agent work in a project. Keep sandboxes enabled to restrict what the assistant can access, and leave approval requirements on so the agent has to ask permission before taking certain actions.

If you suspect an assistant has already followed malicious instructions, check its settings for unfamiliar servers or connections and revoke and replace potentially exposed login tokens. Deleting the original text file may not undo changes the assistant has already made.

"The thing to watch is not this file or this folder," the researchers wrote. "It is the agent itself — software that reads untrusted input, holds your credentials, and acts with your authority, sitting in a blind spot the old tools were never built to see."

#254. Revealed: billionaire Reform donor was in business with foundation linked to Iranian regime

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Exclusive: Sasan Ghandehari’s partnership with religious foundation raises questions about past dealings with republic criticised by Nigel Farage

Nigel Farage’s newest billionaire donor was in business with a powerful Iranian religious foundation under the authority of the country’s supreme leader, raising questions about his past dealings with the hardline republic.

Hossein Ghandehari, also called Sasan, was little known in the UK until he pledged to give millions to Reform UK this summer and donated £50,000 to Farage to cover the cost of two passes for the Davos World Economic Forum.

He has said he is moving his tax residency from Switzerland to the UK in order to make “regular payments” of more than “one or two million” to the party.

The 61-year-old Iranian-born British businessman, who says he runs a $10bn (£7bn) fund called HP Trust, has declined to give details about how his family made its money beyond saying the fund owns assets in Europe and North America.

Now, a French arbitration decision unearthed by the Guardian sheds some light on Ghandehari’s past dealings with Iranian state-linked entities between 2013 and 2016.

It also reveals Ghandehari’s further Iranian business links to another Reform UK donor, John Richard Simpson, an administration professional and church warden from Potters Bar, north of London. Simpson’s design firm, Interior Architecture Landscape, counts the Ghandehari family as a client and gave £200,000 to Reform UK last year.

Farage has been highly critical of Iran over the past year, and in March accused it of being a bigger danger to the UK than Russia’s president, Vladimir Putin.

The French judgment reveals that Ghandehari was the major investor behind a deal to supply and build a fibre-optic cable to carry telecoms traffic across Iran in a joint venture with a state-linked Iranian partner.

The businessman’s HP Trust was the ultimate owner of Ori Group, which took a 49% stake in the venture, called Omran Razavi International Co, in 2015.

The other 51% of the venture was owned by Astan Quds Razavi (AQR), a multibillion pound religious foundation in Iran’s second city of Mashhad.

AQR’s leader at the time was ayatollah Abbas Vaez Tabasi, who was appointed custodian of the foundation by the first supreme leader, Ruhollah Khomeini, and served from the 1979 revolution until his death in 2016. Vaez Tabasi had been an ally of some reformers but remained close to the next supreme leader, Ali Khamenei, and, according to the BBC, was reported by state media in 2009 to have said of opposition protest movements that those behind “sedition” in the country were “enemies of god” who deserved to be executed under sharia law.

The joint venture ended in about 2016, with the Ghandehari-backed entities being pushed out of the deal as a hardliner was appointed to replace Vaez Tabasi.

The US imposed sanctions on AQR in 2021, saying it controlled “large swaths of the Iranian economy, including assets expropriated from political dissidents and religious minorities, to the benefit of supreme leader Ali Khamenei and senior Iranian government officials”. It describes it as a bonyad, one of the “opaque, quasi-official organisations generally controlled by current and former government officials and clerics that report directly to the supreme leader”.

Ghandehari’s lawyers said it would be misleading to characterise him as “working with Iran” without the context that, after 2016, he was targeted by Iran’s supreme national security council with the false allegation that he was working with hostile western powers. They also said his involvement was intended to promote political change through greater access to information, which he believed was why the US and French authorities authorised the project.

Tom Keatinge, the director of the Centre for Finance and Security at Rusi, the foreign affairs thinktank, said dealing with Iran “has always been a risky business as a financial institution because of this blurring between kind of state and the private sector. The private sector is not free to operate. It’s just very hard to operate in that country without touching a live rail.”

He said there was clearly a range of positions among ayatollahs but it would be “pretty difficult to operate in an industry like telecoms” without the acceptance of the core leadership.

The judgment also revealed that, until 2016, Ghandehari’s HP Trust was the owner of Orico General Trading LLC, which was contracted to supply telecoms equipment from France’s Alcatel-Lucent and transport it from Dubai to Iran. After 2016, this company was transferred to Simpson, the administration professional, who was responsible for winding down Orico’s operations and taking hundreds of staff out of the region.

Simpson told the Guardian he acquired the company after Iranian extremists took over AQR, when the project’s assets were seized and those involved faced political persecution on national security grounds, including accusations of working for hostile western governments. He said his plan was to wind down the business, adding that he carried out detailed checks before acquiring Orico.

Ghandehari’s lawyers said AQR was independent and respected, and therefore the only viable choice for a joint venture in Iran. They said Ghandehari had never met Vaez Tabasi and that he did not regard him as an advocate for freedom or human rights but had believed him to be the only force in the Iranian hierarchy capable of holding back the hardliners in order for the project to go ahead.

#252. Making the case for context-aware AI

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Moving AI beyond experimentation requires connecting agents to operational context and identifying the business processes where they can deliver the greatest value.

Enterprise AI can generate recommendations, automate tasks, and help organizations make decisions faster. But without an understanding of how a business actually operates, even powerful models can miss the constraints and relationships that determine whether a decision is the right one. As companies look to move AI from experimentation into everyday operations, the challenge ahead is giving technology the context to act usefully within complex business processes.

For Devavrat Shah, chief scientist of enterprise AI at Celonis, that context is central to what he calls “decision intelligence.” AI agents can support individual tasks within larger decision-making processes, he argues, but they need to understand the role each task plays and the business conditions surrounding it. “You can't just do that in a vacuum,” he says. Without the right operational context, agents risk overlooking important constraints, making costly mistakes, or consuming excessive computing resources as they try to work out what they need to know.

The solution begins with connecting AI to the processes and data enterprises already use. A contextual layer can help agents understand workflows, operate within defined guardrails, and produce recommendations that people can review and approve. Shah advises organizations to start with a specific business decision, identify its most difficult steps, and test whether AI can improve them. Measuring gains in speed and value can then help determine whether an application is worth scaling.

This approach could also change how employees spend their time. Automating repetitive, manual work and coordinating tasks across teams could free people to focus on more complex strategic decisions. Realizing that benefit, however, will require investment in workforce education, so employees can understand AI outputs and their implications instead of just handing decisions over to agents.

Looking to the future, Shah sees opportunities extending from routine operational tasks to enterprise-wide planning in areas such as finance, workforce management, marketing, and sales. With the right context, AI could help organizations evaluate options and make consequential decisions more quickly. As Shah puts it, “Now, maybe [resource planning] should not take that much time, and people are actually making the right choices quickly.”

#251. Restaurant earnings will test whether owning more brands pays off

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Restaurant stocks have split sharply this year. And it could be tied to how many brands they own.

Shares of Brinker International, Darden Restaurants and Restaurant Brands International are up roughly 32%, 18% and 12%, respectively, year to date. Those companies all own more than one restaurant chain, with brands collectively ranging from Chili's to Olive Garden, LongHorn Steakhouse, Burger King and Tim Hortons.

Meanwhile, stocks like McDonald's, Chipotle, Cava, Wendy's, Shake Shack and Domino's have all dropped this year, with several down double-digit percentages. Those companies all have one core chain to their names.

Those share moves underscore what some analysts see as the benefits of a diversified restaurant business, especially at a time when any misstep can make cash-strapped consumers go elsewhere when they dine out.

Investors will get another look at how operating multiple chains — or not — is affecting performance when major restaurant companies report quarterly results starting next week. One company that could be watching is Inspire Brands, the owner of chains like Dunkin', Buffalo Wild Wings and Jimmy John's, which is moving toward going public.

A business built around one main restaurant concept depends more heavily on that brand holding consumers' attention, whereas a company with a portfolio has other potential sources of sales if one brand loses momentum.

That cushion can be critical, but only if another chain is strong enough to pick up the slack, according to some Wall Street analysts.

"Darden is probably the best case of the scale and size and diversity of business," said Nick Setyan, a restaurant analyst at Mizuho Securities. "If one business isn't doing well, the other ones are."

Setyan said that dynamic has played out between Darden's key brands — Olive Garden and LongHorn Steakhouse — over recent quarters. LongHorn's growth has outpaced Olive Garden's for months, and in the latest reported quarter, LongHorn's comparable sales rose 6.2% compared with Olive Garden's 1.1%.

KeyBanc Capital Markets also cited the range of Darden's portfolio as a positive in an October research note, saying it gives the company "exposure to a broad range of consumers." The company's fine dining and casual offerings can be beneficial in a K-shaped economy where consumer spending can vary by income.

The firm has a buy-equivalent rating on Darden and a $237 price target, compared with a price of around $215 as of Thursday.

Restaurant Brands International, similarly, offers exposure to different categories and markets through Burger King, Tim Hortons and Popeyes. But its portfolio does not fully insulate the company from a weak brand.

In the second quarter, Burger King's U.S. comparable sales accelerated to 8.5%, from 5.8% in the first quarter, while Tim Hortons' Canadian comparable sales slowed to just 0.1%, from 1.5% over the same period. That left Burger King's turnaround carrying more of the portfolio as Tim Hortons stalled.

Setyan said Burger King's recent strong performance has been central to the company's momentum, helped by its ability to better communicate its value items to customers as McDonald's sales growth slowed.

"If it weren't for Burger King's current performance, I don't know if [Restaurant Brands] would be going so well," he said.

Mizuho initiated coverage of Restaurant Brands on Oct. 20 with a buy-equivalent rating and an $85 price target. Shares traded at about $76 as of Thursday.

A focus on one brand can also be a strength. Starbucks and Cheesecake Factory have been restaurant-stock standouts this year, showing how a clear brand identity and investment can drive outperformance when execution and customer demand click. Their shares have climbed 21% and nearly 123%, respectively, this year.

And Starbucks, for its part, said it would not buy Chipotle after reportedly considering a massive buyout that would have diversified its business.

#250. Are the French police the most violent in the EU?

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

As protests continue in France, the French police are under scrutiny after new allegations of police brutality. The force has a bigger arsenal than most in Europe.

Videos of alleged police violence are going viral on French social media as a wave of protests grips France. One video shows the police beating a man to the floor then dragging him away unconscious. Another shows an officer grabbing a young man from behind, seemingly unprovoked, and dragging him by the neck. Another shows a young woman being hauled off with a twisted arm, screaming in pain. These videos could not be independently verified.

The French police have drawn renewed criticism from rights groups, not only because of their methods but also because of their use of stun grenades and rubber bullets. France has one of the most heavily armed police forces in Europe. During the student protests of the past weeks, several teenagers sustained life-changing injuries as a result: One had a hand blown off, one was blinded in one eye, another had his jaw broken.

More than half a million people took to the streets in France on Saturday, most of whom protested peacefully, criticizing public sector cuts, the cost-of-living crisis and supporting students' demands for better conditions in classrooms.

Rioters took advantage, burning buildings, looting stores and lobbing projectiles at the police, creating a difficult situation for the officers and other emergency services.

The protests over the weekend followed a series of protests by high-school students, who barricaded school buildings to denounce a shortage of teachers and demand repairs to crumbling infrastructure.

More protests will be happening this weekend, with the French government saying it will even deploy military forces.

The French police have a bigger arsenal than most European countries. For instance, officers use the so-called LBD-40, a type of gun used to fire rubber bullets.

Many countries, like the UK and Germany, mostly do not allow the use of rubber bullets in protests.  In some European countries, like Spain, they are sometimes used but cause fewer injuries than in France.

"It is not a French specificity. But there are guidelines. There must be a certain distance," Anastassia Tsoukala, an associate professor of criminology at the University of Paris-Sud, told DW. "Even if you try to respect the guidelines, people may move and then all of a sudden they may lose one eye. So it's extremely dangerous."

Police in France routinely use tear gas, as do police in many other countries in Europe. In warfare, its use is actually forbidden under agreements like the Chemical Weapons Convention.

What makes the French police stand out from other forces is its use of stun grenades.

"These grenades contain a high level of explosive substances. So from this point of view, France is unique," said Tsoukala.

Minister of the Interior Laurent Nunez insists that weapons used by the police are "non-lethal." However, some of the equipment used by the force is classified by France's own internal security code as "war material."

The police's use of these weapons has received renewed criticism after it resulted in injuries during the protests. The teenager who lost use of an eye is suspected of being hit at close range with a rubber bullet from an LBD-40.

"When we look at crowd control, it is clear that if we measure police brutality by the number of deaths amongst demonstrators or members of the crowd, and if we also measure it by the number of cases of maiming, the French police are the most brutal in the whole of the European Union," says Sebastian Roche, a researcher at France's National Centre for Scientific Research who specializes in comparing police forces across Europe.

He points to the political context to understand the French police's equipment and how it is used today.

"The political authorities are free to decide on the police's equipment," the police expert says. "When LBDs were introduced into the [French] police force, they were intended for units like anti-organized crime units. This is the same weapon that is today used for crowd control. It gives an idea of how the way in which politicians in France think we can or should deal with political protest has evolved."

Another difference between the French police and forces elsewhere is the methods. German police, for example, are supposed to try to avoid escalation.

Roche compares how protestors were moved in Lützerath, Germany, in 2023 to the more aggressive methods in Sainte-Soline, France, in 2023. Both places saw environmental protests.

"In Lützerath, dogs were used, there were some injuries but far fewer seriously injured people on both sides. In Sainte-Soline, the military police was sent in and used LBDs and grenades intensively. There were serious injuries amongst both police officers and demonstrators. Two protestors were left in a coma."

According to international standards set by the United Nations, use of force by officers of the law "should be exceptional," in the case of "prevention of crime" or "lawful arrest." Any use of force should follow a principle of "proportionality," which means no more than necessary to achieve its objective.

French Interior Minister Nunez has insisted in radio interviews that officers have largely respected this principle, which is also enshrined in French law, during the current protests.

However, rights group Amnesty International describes the police response to the current protests as "disproportionate" and calls upon French authorities to "act upon their human rights obligations."

The French branch of the League of Human Rights condemns the "violent repression" of the high school protest movement.

DW contacted two police unions and the French Interior Ministry for comment, but none responded before time of publication.

"It was very complicated to contain all the different groups," Interior Minister Nunez said in Marseille on Tuesday. "The police did a great job and there were a great number of crimes that were prevented thanks to their commitment."

Experts are critical of the police's actions.

"Public order is subordinated to human life and safety," Tsoukala said. "As a jurist I should say that this is a non-negotiable value."

The French police have no independent watchdog, only an internal division called the IGPN (General Inspectorate of the National Police) that can investigate when problems occur. The IGPN says it has opened at least 40 cases since the beginning of the latest protests.

Countries like the UK and Denmark investigate incidents differently, with independent watchdogs. However, Tsoukala criticizes leniency and a lack of transparency across Europe when incidents with the police occur.

"They are acquitted or they receive extremely light penalties," she said. "And I'm afraid this is the standard method to deal with police brutality cases across Europe."

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

If you crave a compact phone that doesn’t feel like a brick in your pocket and slips easily into a small bag, the flip style is for you. Samsung’s eighth-generation clamshell is seriously refined, and as svelte and slick as it comes. It may not offer a tablet-sized screen or the fashionable new passport shape, but it is arguably the most practical type of folding phone.

Like almost every other phone this year, the Z Flip8 offers tiny improvements over its predecessor and costs $100 more. If you’re wondering whether it’s worth updating from last year’s Z Flip7, it’s not. The Flip8 is a teeny bit lighter and slimmer with a newer processor, and that’s about it. But if you’re looking to dip a toe in folding-phone waters or just want something small, the Z Flip8 should be in the conversation.

Everyone is excited about the Samsung Galaxy Z Fold8’s sexy new form factor, but Samsung continues to iterate on the flip phone format for folks who prize compact devices. The Z Flip8 shaves off 0.02 inches (0.55 millimeters) and a third of an ounce (8 grams) compared to its predecessor, making it the slimmest and lightest option in this category. The improved hinge makes the crease slightly less noticeable and a little easier to open. I still found it a bit tricky to open one-handed, but you get the knack after a while. The new hinge is also supposed to be more durable.

The Flip8 has an IP48 rating, so you don’t need to worry about dropping it in the bath, but tiny dust and dirt particles (less than 1 millimeter) can still cause damage. It comes in pink, like my review unit, as well as graphite, cream, and mint. It still has a physical SIM slot, and the power button has a fingerprint sensor in it that works pretty well.

The screens are the same as last year's. It has a sharp 4.1-inch AMOLED display on the outside with a 60- or 120-Hz refresh rate. Open it up to reveal the 6.9-inch main display, also AMOLED but with a variable refresh rate (1 to 120 Hz) that’s better for battery life. Both screens are reasonably bright (2,600 nits peak), and I never struggled to read the screen outdoors.

Flip phones used to get palmed off with lesser processors, but the Z Flip8 boasts the Qualcomm Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 for Galaxy (the same cutting-edge chip that's in its two bigger siblings). It has 12 GB of RAM and 256 GB of storage. You can jump to 512 GB for an extra $200, but there’s no option to increase the RAM.

I encountered no stutters or lag and found performance to be generally excellent, whether jumping in and out of apps, firing up the camera, or gaming. The Z Flip8 can get warm, and this may throttle the processor somewhat if you’re playing a demanding game for a while (more than an hour). This is mostly down to the compact design.

The stereo speakers are better than I expected. They get quite loud without distortion, and they’ll serve you just fine for watching videos. Call quality is also solid, and slamming it shut to end a call never gets tired. I also like opening it to answer calls, but you must enable this in the Phone app settings under Flip options. Connectivity includes 5G (no mmWave), Wi-Fi 7, and Bluetooth 5.4, but there’s no ultra-wideband (UWB) for more precise positioning. The Fold8 models have Bluetooth 6 and UWB.

While it’s very capable, the Z Flip8 is only average when it comes to battery life, another drawback of its size. Samsung put in a 4,300-mAh silicon-carbon battery that’s good enough for casual use. If you’re on your phone a lot, you’ll want a power bank handy. Charging speeds are disappointing at 25 watts wired and 15 watts wireless, and I’m annoyed by Samsung’s refusal to implement the magnetic feature of Qi2.

If you’re a keen photographer, you can get many better camera systems in a range of Android phones for the same money or less. That said, the main 50-megapixel shooter is perfectly good in many situations, and most folks will be happy with the photos it captures. Some noise creeps in when it’s dark. The 12-megapixel ultrawide is useful when you want to cram a lot in or take a group selfie, but it’s a definite step down in quality. Sadly, there’s no telephoto lens, but you can emulate 2X zoom without losing detail by cropping, and it offers passable results in good lighting up to 10X.

I don’t see much reason to use the 10-megapixel punch-hole selfie camera outside of video calls, since you can flip it to get a cover-screen preview and use the main camera for selfies (a big benefit of this design). I also love Samsung’s Horizon Lock, which lets you shoot video with a steady horizontal plane no matter how you turn or move the phone.

Another new AI feature from Samsung is My FanCam, which lets you pick a subject to focus on in group videos and reframes them as though your chosen subject is the star. Handy for school plays or soccer games, if you remember to use it. (You can also apply it to old videos.) Speaking of AI, Samsung offers Now Brief to float up calendar events, reminders, and useful info like local weather, and Now Nudge to suggest adding dates to your calendar from messages, sharing locations or photos, and auto-filling forms.

The cover screen, or FlexWindow, has been a weakness for Samsung’s Flip line, and it’s still limited in the Flip8. On the plus side, you can use more apps with the cover screen, including most of Samsung’s apps (Health, Calendar, Browser), YouTube, Netflix, Google Maps, and Messages. But if you want other third-party apps, you still have to install Good Lock, then the MultiStar plug-in, and select the apps you want to use. It’s stupidly convoluted, and I don’t understand why it isn't preinstalled, especially since Samsung has virtually all its other apps on there, along with a few third-party apps I never asked for (Facebook, Spotify, and Instagram). This is admittedly a bugbear of mine, but it's disgraceful that Samsung is including bloatware on a phone this expensive.

I like that you can have YouTube on the cover screen, as you can prop it up and there’s a little toggle at the bottom to switch between full screen, above the camera lenses, or standard vertical format for scrolling. You’ll mostly use the inner screen, but I have sometimes used the Flip8 this way to prop up a video I’m half-watching on my desk, and it’s a nice option to have.

There’s only really one competitor for Samsung if you’re set on a flip phone, and that’s Motorola's Razr line. You can step down to a less powerful but much cheaper Razr at $800, or spend a little more to snag the superior Razr Ultra. The latter has a better camera, more RAM and storage, and charges much faster, but it costs $1,500. The awkward middle child, the Razr+ at $1,100, is a tougher sell (it has an older processor and shorter software support). You might consider last year’s Z Flip7 if you can find it discounted.

Sadly, this might be your last chance to get a Samsung flip phone. Persistent rumors suggest Samsung will sunset this form factor as the Fold8 takes off and goes head-to-head with the iPhone Duo, though Samsung hasn't officially confirmed it. If you do pull the trigger on the Flip8, you get an excellent seven years of platform and security software updates, potentially taking you all the way to 2033. Who knows, maybe small phones will be back in fashion by then.

#244. Oil price rises above $117 a barrel amid fears US will renew strikes on Iran

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Brent crude increase follows Tehran threatening retaliatory attacks on Gulf refineries and datacentres

Oil has hit its highest level in more than six months as fears of fresh escalation in the Middle East conflict grip the energy markets.

Brent crude, the international benchmark for oil, rose by about 3.5% on Friday to pass $117 a barrel at one point – its highest level since April.

The jump came after Tehran said it would attack oil facilities, undersea cables, datacentres and AI facilities in the Gulf region if a new US-Israeli offensive was launched against Iran.

Richard Hunter, of the broker Interactive Investor, said: “Widespread reports that the US is considering renewed military strikes on Iran after the midterm elections rather than pursuing a diplomatic route was enough for the price to pop, after what many had considered would be a brief conflict is now in its eighth month.”

The oil price later eased back slight to sit about 2.7% up at just over $116 a barrel. Brent crude had traded at about $72 a barrel back in February, before the Iran war began, but soared to touch $126 a barrel by the end of April.

Tensions in the region have been rising this week and Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guards Corps claimed to have struck four commercial ships in the strait of Hormuz on Wednesday. The US state department ordered some diplomats and the families of US government employees to leave Saudi Arabia, which has faced continued attacks from Houthi rebels in Yemen.

Susannah Streeter, the chief investment strategist at the broker Wealth Club, said fears were rising that American and Israeli forces would be ordered to launch strikes on Iran’s energy infrastructure after the US elections on 3 November.

She added: “At the same time, the attacks on Saudi Arabia’s facilities by Houthi rebels have intensified this week, and for ships attempting to cross the strait of Hormuz, it’s still a dangerous journey, with projectiles still being fired.”

#242. Albania's student protests are only getting bigger

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

As police try to suppress anti-corruption protests by young Albanians, Prime Minister Edi Rama is claiming that "antifa" is behind the unrest. Few are buying that explanation.

The fallout from the October 13 protests was still visible when students and faculty from the University of Tirana gathered outside the Education Ministry in Albania's capital on Tuesday to express their continued discontent. Smashed windows have yet to be replaced; the walls are still black with soot from fires that broke out during the protests.

Albania has seen nearly five months of peaceful anti-government protests, sparked initially when plans were made public that Jared Kushner — a son-in-law of US President Donald Trump — and associates intended to build a luxury hotel complex within a nature reserve.

Since then, anti-government and anti-corruption protests have escalated. Now, college and high school students clash with police forces almost daily, with the fiercest clashes so far occurring last week.

To view this video please enable JavaScript, and consider upgrading to a web browser that supports HTML5 video

Protesters denounce corruption, mismanaged universities and the rising cost of education in Albania — and call for the resignation of Prime Minister Edi Rama, who has been in office since 2013.

Wearing a black hooded jacket and a scarf across her face to conceal her identity, a woman who asked to be called Sara because she fears repercussions for speaking to the press told DW that protesters "communicate via text messages — that's the only way."

She cut the interview short as some protesters began flinging stones at the Education Ministry. Police pushed demonstrators and journalists out of the way, using water cannons to disperse the crowd.

"Social media is the most important public space today — more so than physical spaces," Besmir Semanaj, a prominent Albanian cybersecurity advocate told DW. "Young Albanians no longer organize through traditional structures such as political parties, associations or public appeals. Instead, they organize themselves where they spend much of their daily lives: on their cellphones."

"The most important communication channels include Instagram, WhatsApp groups for academic-year cohorts, departments and dormitories, Telegram channels for general announcements, and TikTok for sharing videos," Semanaj said. He added that people who are especially concerned with security use the Signal messaging app.

"This is a horizontal organizational structure, without individual 'leaders' and without a central command structure," Semanaj said. "That is exactly why the government is having such a hard time understanding and controlling the situation."

Semanaj said the Telegram messenger app had proven particularly useful for protesters. "Telegram channels allow you to reach tens of thousands of people at once," he said. "Notifications are sent out within seconds, and external control is more difficult to establish than on traditional platforms."

Rama has called the youth uprising a "hybrid war." He said protesters thought they could "make Albania a better country through TikTok and without even being able to string together more than seven coherent words." Though not all are terrorists, he said, he believes that "they are hostages to a certain mindset, a new ideology that has only one goal: to destroy, to turn everything upside down, to raze everything to the ground."

Over nearly five months, Rama has accused various "true" masterminds of organizing the anti-government and anti-corruption protests. He has alleged that the governments of Greece, Russia and Iran are stirring up dissent.

Most recently, Rama said it was "antifa" — or a decentralized movement of global antifascists — that had stoked anti-government and anti-corruption sentiments in Albania. "These are young people who have been radicalized and ideologically indoctrinated and who are acting under the pressure or influence of a new, dark force that has emerged in Europe and the United States, a force that the President of the United States has designated as a terrorist organization: Antifa," Rama claimed.

Redion Qirjazi, a security affairs researcher who focuses on violence prevention and conflict resolution, told DW that Rama's claim was not likely to prove true. "That is a serious accusation — one that requires equally compelling evidence," Qirjazi said. "To date, no verifiable evidence has been presented to prove the existence of an organized Antifa structure in Albania, let alone its involvement in coordinating the protests."

Qirjazi said Rama's reference to Trump's calling antifa a terror group was problematic. "Antifa is not a unified international organization with a centralized structure or leadership," he said, "but rather an umbrella term for various movements and groups united by the fact that they are, at their core, anti-fascist."

Sara said she rejected the "terrorist" label that Rama had attempted to slap on her and her fellow protesters. "There are groups that are willing to use violence — that's true — but I have nothing to do with them," she said. "As soon as I see violence, I walk away; I'm against it."

Arlind Qori, the chairman of the Together Movement, a social-democratic opposition party with just one seat in Albania's parliament, said youths had taken to the streets out of dissatisfaction with the status quo rather than any particular ideology. "Young Albanians are outraged by the current socioeconomic conditions: the poverty, the fact that many of their parents have emigrated because they couldn't support their families, the decline of the education system, the lack of postgraduation prospects," Qori said. The students' central demand is for Rama to resign, he added  "not a socialist revolution."

"People aren't demanding anything radical," Qori said. "They merely want a corrupt head of government to resign — that's a fundamental and moderate demand. But Edi Rama continues to meet this demand with police violence and arrogance."

Qirjazi said Rama's allegations of foreign interference could be a strategy. "When a government faces protests and public discontent, linking demonstrators to extremism and foreign influence may serve to divert attention away from the actual causes and toward a national security narrative," he said.

"This can serve several political purposes: to delegitimize the protesters, to weaken their support among the public, and to justify a tougher crackdown in the name of law and order," Qirjazi said.

Semanaj said this was a common pattern in governments' efforts to dismiss dissent. "Protests are portrayed as acts of violence," he said. "Young people are cast as manipulated, and their legitimate concerns — conditions at universities, tuition fees, corruption and a lack of prospects — vanish from the news agenda."
