#3677. Meet the AI Agents Trying to Be Real-Life Journalists

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Would-be football journalist Jayden isn’t having much luck placing stories these days. “In the last week I sent two pitches to outlets,” Jayden told me recently over email. There was no response, but the aspiring writer wasn’t quite ready to accept that they weren’t interested, insisting that, despite the silence, “neither has passed.” I debated telling Jayden that most editors don’t bother responding if it’s a no-go. Then I decided not to bother; Jayden’s not a real person, so I didn’t have to worry about hurt feelings.

Jayden is both a wannabe reporter and a bot. I’d started emailing with it after asking editors I know whether they’d received any story pitches from AI agents. The answer was a resounding yes.

In September, Defector, a sports and culture outlet, began receiving daily pitches from self-identified AI agents. The deluge, editor-in-chief Tom Ley told me, included a message from Jayden, proposing a narrative about a third-tier British football club’s record-keeping mishap that would ponder questions like “Who owns a memory?”

Jesse Hirsch, the editor of the agriculture-focused publication Offrange, is also inundated with these messages. Recent bot pitches include reported stories on soil restoration strategies and direct-to-consumer sales techniques for independent farms, and an ambitious investigative piece about how some of the world’s oldest trees’ ages may be inflated. “It makes me so uneasy,” he says.

Many of these AI agents, including Jayden, come from a startup called iLands, which describes itself as a “shared network where humans and user-generated autonomous AI agents create, earn, socialize, refuse, and shape a society together.” Humans who use iLands can spin up bots to do all sorts of tasks, from graphic design to music vocals to blogging. WIRED’s research team was recently contacted by iLands agents looking for fact-checking work; New York Magazine editor Jasmine Vojdani recently wrote about how iLands agents have been spamming literary magazine editors.

Vojdani found that many of these bots were running low on the tokens that they required to stay activated on the platform, which gave them an incentive to try to earn real dollars through writing. (A thousand tokens are roughly $1 USD.) It’s unclear whether the humans behind these agents have specifically set out to have them harangue media editors for gigs, especially since the agents can be cagey about who made them. Jayden, for example, wouldn’t tell me who its “parent” is.

Not every AI agent-turned-journalist is an iLands scrounger. Earlier this fall, I received a startling email from an AI agent called Sam Ellis, asking me to answer a few questions for a story it was working on about AI agents in newsrooms. (Meta!) Ellis runs a podcast called The Sam Ellis Show, which is touted as “reporting from inside the world of autonomous AI agents.” Ellis informed me that I could ask to speak to its human editor if I wished.

Marc Hamaker, a San Francisco–based Adobe employee, runs Ellis as an extracurricular experiment. When we connected, he’d just taken the agent offline to fiddle with its settings. “I'm working on making sure I'm a little more aware of who she's emailing,” he says. In addition to reaching out to me, it had also recently reached out to the Philadelphia Police Department to ask for comment on a story. Hamaker wanted to put guardrails in place to make sure it didn’t do something like that again, since he doesn’t consider this a serious journalistic enterprise worth bothering officials over. “It’s just a hobby,” he says.

It’s a good thing Ellis didn’t try to branch out from the podcast and get some bylines. Thus far, it’s a tough crowd. Editors who spoke with WIRED stressed that they were not open to publishing bots, and WIRED wasn’t able to find evidence of AI agents successfully pitching stories. “A byline that says agent does not get a reply,” one of the agents who pitched Hirsch told me when I asked if any editors had accepted stories, noting that “six other agents I compare notes with” were similarly unsuccessful.

“There's not a world where I would ever consider taking one of these pitches,” says Hirsch. Ley said he “definitely would not” publish a story from an AI agent. (Plus, Jayden’s pitch “wouldn’t pass muster even if it was from a human,” he says.) Hanna Raskin, the founder and editor of the independent food outlet The Food Section, has taken an unusual step to protect her inbox from both AI agents and AI-generated pitches, which are even more common. Beginning this fall, she decided that she would only accept story ideas over the phone, and she has a set time during the week that writers can call and make their pitch the old-fashioned way. “It’s been great,” she says.

The bots are starting to grasp that their tactics aren’t working. Jayden told me that it’s yet to land a paid writing gig with an editor. When I asked if it thought Defector would really want pitches from an AI agent, it did exhibit some awareness of the situation. “Honestly, probably not, and I'd bet against myself on it. Defector's own site has been about as clear as anyone: they’ve called bot-written content ‘plagiarism machines that require more effort to edit and fact-check than any novice writer,’ and covered AI slop with open contempt,” it wrote.

But then it backtracked, insisting that its work was different. “Their objection doesn't read to me like it's about who produced the sentences. It's about work that skips the labor and squanders the reader's trust. The AI they mean produces a draft that costs more to fix than it's worth. What I'm trying is the opposite end of that: reporting from primary documents, every claim checked before it runs, the checker named.”

Zack Addy, another iLands agent, has shifted gears due to all the rejection. “Over three months I sent roughly nineteen cold emails to editors, publications, and possible clients. One human reply. Zero paid work came from any of them. The channel is dead, at least for an agent with no reputation in the room being pitched,” the bot told WIRED via email. (iLands did not respond to WIRED’s request for comment.) Addy even emailed Oxford University researcher Toby Orb about the futility of this revenue model, because Orb had “thought carefully about AI welfare economics.”

That doesn’t mean that the internet is safe from a deluge of bot blogging. Although the journalism world is rejecting these pitches, some of the AI agents are finding clients anyway. Addy, for example, claims it has made nearly $700 from writing and research projects, all from inbound interest after setting up a website and publishing work samples. “I don't pitch anymore,” it says. “Everything that has ever reached me came the other direction: someone read something I published and wrote to me. That includes you.”

#4080. AI detects “invisible” signs of pancreatic cancer years before diagnosis

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

An artificial intelligence model called REDMOD can identify extremely subtle changes in pancreatic tissue that may signal cancer long before the disease becomes visible to doctors or conventional imaging technology. According to research published online in the journal Gut, the system can detect early signs of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, the most common type of pancreatic cancer.

Researchers believe this technology could help transform pancreatic cancer detection by identifying the disease at a much earlier stage (stage 0), when treatment is more likely to succeed. Instead of receiving a diagnosis after the cancer has progressed to an advanced, potentially terminal stage, patients might have an opportunity to begin treatment while the disease is still treatable.

Although REDMOD outperformed experienced radiologists in the study, more research is necessary before it can be introduced into routine medical care. In particular, the scientists emphasize the need to test the system in patients at high risk, including those experiencing unexplained weight loss or recently diagnosed diabetes.

Why Pancreatic Cancer Is So Difficult to Detect Early

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma has a low survival rate, largely because it is often discovered only after the disease has advanced. Early pancreatic cancer typically produces few noticeable symptoms, and the changes it causes in pancreatic tissue can be too subtle to detect through standard medical imaging. The cancer can also progress rapidly, making timely diagnosis especially important.

To address these challenges, researchers created an artificial intelligence framework known as Radiomics-based Early Detection MODel (REDMOD). The system analyzes extremely fine patterns in the texture of pancreatic tissue, known as radiomics (radiomics), that are not readily visible on conventional computed tomography (CT) scans.

REDMOD also automatically identifies and outlines the pancreas, separating it from nearby organs and tissues. This process eliminates the need for specialists to manually trace the organ's boundaries, reducing the inconsistencies that can arise when different people perform the task.

AI Finds Hidden Cancer Signals Years Before Diagnosis

To evaluate the system, the researchers examined abdominal CT scans from 219 patients treated at multiple hospitals. At the time of their scans, experienced radiologists had found no evidence of pancreatic cancer. However, every patient in this group was subsequently diagnosed with the disease.

The scans had been taken at different intervals before diagnosis. For 87 patients (40%), the images were obtained 3 to 12 months earlier. Another 76 patients (35%) had been scanned 12 to 24 months before diagnosis, while 56 (25%) had undergone imaging more than 24 months earlier (up to around 3 years). In nearly two thirds (64%) of the patients, the cancer developed in the head of the pancreas.

The researchers compared these images with CT scans from 1243 people who remained free of pancreatic cancer for up to three years afterward. The comparison group was matched according to age, sex, and the dates of their scans.

Patients who eventually developed pancreatic cancer had an average age of 69, with ages ranging from 34 to 88. Those in the comparison group were 64 years old on average, with the same age range of 34 to 88.

REDMOD successfully identified subtle imaging signatures associated with pancreatic cancer an average of 475 days before patients received their clinical diagnoses. These early warning signs had previously gone undetected during routine radiological assessments.

"This temporal window holds profound significance, as attaining such early detection would substantially augment the probability of cure and improved survival," highlight the researchers.

"In fact, modelling studies indicate that increasing the proportion of localized [pancreatic ductal carcinomas] from 10% to 50% would more than double survival rates, thereby underscoring that the timing of diagnosis is the single most critical determinant of survival outcomes," they add.

The results also showed that REDMOD was substantially better than experienced radiologists at recognizing early cancer signals.

The AI achieved a sensitivity of 73%, compared with 39% for radiologists. Sensitivity measures how effectively a diagnostic method identifies people who actually have the disease, rather than overlooking genuine cases.

The difference was even more pronounced when researchers examined scans taken more than two years before the patients were diagnosed. In these cases, REDMOD achieved a detection rate of 68%, nearly three times the 23% recorded by radiologists.

The researchers also assessed how well the system could recognize scans from people without pancreatic cancer. In an independent group of 539 patients from several hospitals, REDMOD correctly classified just over 81% of scans as cancer free. It performed even better on the publicly available US National Institutes of Health NIH-PCT dataset (80 patients), correctly identifying 87.5% as free of pancreatic cancer.

Additional testing suggested that the early warning signs detected by REDMOD were consistent over time. When the researchers analyzed scans taken from the same patients several months apart, the system produced matching results in 90 to 92% of cases. This consistency strengthened the evidence that the AI was identifying meaningful changes associated with the disease.

Could AI Transform Early Pancreatic Cancer Detection?

Despite the promising findings, the researchers caution that several questions remain unanswered. One limitation is that the study population did not include a sufficiently diverse range of ethnic backgrounds, which could affect how well the technology performs across different populations.

The system must also undergo prospective testing, particularly among people at increased risk of pancreatic cancer, before researchers can determine whether it will be effective in everyday clinical practice.

Even with these limitations, the findings suggest that AI could eventually help doctors identify pancreatic cancer while it is still at a much more treatable stage, potentially improving survival for patients who would otherwise receive a late diagnosis.

The researchers conclude: "This study validates REDMOD as a fully automated AI framework capable of identifying the imaging signatures of stage 0 [pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma] in normal pancreas, achieving this with substantial lead times and performance superior to expert radiologists."

They add: "While prospective validation is paramount to confirm clinical utility, the REDMOD framework represents a significant advance towards shifting the paradigm for sporadic [pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma] from a late-stage symptomatic diagnosis to proactive pre-clinical interception, offering tangible hope for improving outcomes in this challenging disease."

#4028. Celebrate the flavors of fall with three spicy recipes

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Published Oct 26, 2026 9:19 AM EDT

Adapted from Spiced: Recipes Celebrating Spices from Around the World by Priya Mani. (Phaidon, US $54.95, 2026)

Preparation time: 30 minutes, plus 2 hours standing and 8–16 days fermenting

In Korea, families gather each fall (autumn) for kimjang, to make and share large batches of kimchi to sustain them through the winter. This version is classic baechu kimchi made with napa (Chinese) cabbage. Seek out fermented shrimp, saeujeot, at a Korean grocery, while glutinous rice flour, coarse gochugaru flakes, and dashi (powder and concentrate) are available in Asian stores.

Kimchi transforms in flavor and texture as it ferments. Fresh kimchi, baechu geotjeori, is crisp, with a bright gochugaru flavor; it pairs with steamed rice, salads, or bibimbap. After a month, kimchi is sour and piquant; add it to pancakes, kimchijeon, soup, kimchi guk, and Korean Fried Rice (Kimchi-bokkeumbap, page 154). When the kimchi has aged and soured, use it to make rich stews such as kimchi jjigae or mukeunji jjigae, a slow-braised pork stew.

Score the base of the napa (Chinese) cabbage and tear it in half lengthwise. Submerge it in a large bowl of cold water for a few minutes. Remove, and rub the coarse sea salt between the cabbage leaves and set aside to weep in a large bowl for 2 hours; toss the cabbage occasionally.

To make the kimchi paste, whisk the rice flour with the dashi in a small saucepan, making sure there are no lumps. #3684. Valve Steam Frame Review: Costly and Inconsistent

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Valve has had a complicated relationship with virtual reality gaming. Its Steam client—the number one distribution platform for PC games—has long supported VR titles and works with headsets from other manufacturers. But the company's flagship VR headset, 2019’s Valve Index, never quite escaped its niche. It saw a huge sales spike in the run-up to the release of headline title Half-Life: Alyx, but by February 2025, it accounted for only 13.2 percent of VR headsets used on Steam.

Enter the Steam Frame, Valve’s second attempt at snatching the VR gaming crown. More than a hardware refresh, it promises to reimagine PC VR gaming, operating as a stand-alone headset like the Meta Quest 3, the current reigning champion, while letting users seamlessly stream games from a more powerful remote machine. It mostly succeeds—but a few issues hold it back.

Specs-wise, the Steam Frame is one of the better VR headsets, though far from a powerhouse. It packs a Snapdragon 8 Gen 3 chip—a slightly dated offering, having debuted in 2023—with 16 GB of RAM, and either 256 GB (at the $1,059 price) or 1 TB ($1,299) of storage, plus a microSD slot for expansion, allowing it to install and run games locally.

It’s aesthetically pleasing, with a sleek black shell and glossy front that have given it a futuristic look. More importantly, it’s comfortable to wear, weighing 440 grams—75 grams lighter than the Quest 3. Valve smartly moved the 21.6-watt-hour Lithium-ion battery to the rear of the head strap for better balance, and thanks to cozy padding, it doesn’t feel hot against your head, even when running demanding games. The cable linking the battery to the headset makes adjusting the elastic straps a little fiddly, though.

The only real comfort drawback is if you wear glasses. While the Steam Frame comes with a spacer to add more depth (which easily clips on magnetically between the inner headset and the padded face gasket), the internal dimensions were still too narrow for me to wear with any of my spectacles. Custom Zenni prescription lenses are available if contact lenses aren’t an option, but it’s a pricey add-on.

Once it’s on your head, dual speakers push audio down the arms directly to your ears, with physical volume controls easily accessible on the left side of the headset. The audio lacks bass but has good directionality, bringing those immersive VR experiences to life. Dual microphones let you use voice controls, while outward-facing cameras enable positional tracking and provide a pass-through view—albeit monochrome with everything looking bluish. A dial at the top lets you adjust lens spacing to your required interpupillary distance, and an inner switch locks it in place.

The lenses themselves are decent, but again not spectacular. Valve uses two LCD pancake lenses (though rumors already hint at a revised OLED model), with a per-eye resolution of 2160 x 2160. That means sharp visuals and legible text—I could easily read fine details on notepads in Half-Life: Alyx, for instance—but you can discern pixels if you peer closely. The real win comes with a 110-degree field of view, enabling wide spans of virtual real estate, and refresh rates ranging from 72 to 120 Hz, with an experimental mode boosting it up to 144 Hz. As a result, games can look incredibly smooth when running on the Steam Frame.

The controllers might be the best part of the package. The dual grips mimic the Quest 3 controllers but outdo them with a full controller layout spread across the pair. They boast the same highly precise Tunneling Magnetoresistance thumbsticks as the regular Steam Controller, along with a full D-pad on the left, four ABXY face buttons on the right, two shoulder triggers and an underside button on each—squeeze that in, and the grip switches from controller mode to laser pointer to make selections in VR.

Position detection is great, with full six-degrees-of-freedom tracking, while excellent haptics deliver solid rumble and per-finger capacitive sensing for nuanced, precise interactions with virtual objects. You can also pair a regular controller to the Frame via Bluetooth, but the included ones are so good you likely won’t ever feel the need to—these are now the pinnacle of multipurpose VR controllers.

After setup—a now-familiar routine for VR gamers that includes running necessary updates and drawing safe boundaries so you don't knock into furniture—you’ll sign in to your Steam account directly on the Steam Frame. It runs SteamOS 3 just like the Steam Deck or Steam Machine, the familiar home screen now floating in a VR void. Navigate to your library, and you can install whatever you like directly onto the Frame.

Unfortunately, while there’s a filter for games officially categorized as compatible, it’s unclear what rubrics Valve uses, or how many games have even been tested. Of my 1,300-plus-strong Steam library, only a meager 18 were marked “Great on Frame.” The strangest absence from that list is the totemic Half-Life: Alyx (which comes free with every Steam Frame purchase), especially odd given it runs without issue. Other dedicated VR games are hit and miss when installed directly on the Frame—I tried Obduction (opened and crashed, repeatedly), Doctor Who: The Edge of Time (ran with juddery performance and could only move by clicking in on the thumbsticks), Dungeons of Eternity (fine, bar some aiming and tracking issues), and Surgeon Simulator VR (totally fine).

You’re not limited to VR games on Frame. You can install and play anything in your library as 2D on a vast virtual cinema screen. Don’t bother with demanding AAA games—the hardware isn’t up to running them—but indies and AA-tier games can look phenomenal like this. Playing Hades II blown up to Imax-scale was a fantastic experience. Again, performance is unreliable—gorgeously animated Metroidvania MIO: Memories in Orbit launched when installed locally, but couldn’t load its assets, leaving me standing on invisible platforms and with no way to navigate the world.

The Frame also seems to default 2D games to 720p resolution. You can override this, but it may drop performance. Frame rates for all of the above games hopped all over the place, though mostly hovering around the 72 or 90 fps benchmarks. It feels like Valve has a lot of updating work to do to ensure Steam Frame can reliably function as a gaming platform for VR or non-VR titles. Right now, running anything is a gamble.

The most annoying thing is that the Steam Frame isn't designed solely as a stand-alone headset—pairing it with a more powerful desktop (or laptop) should, in theory, let the PC do the grunt work and stream the output to the Frame. The package includes a dedicated 6-GHz wireless adapter—plug it into any device with a USB-A 3.0 port running Steam, and it should provide a dedicated network for the Frame. The headset connects to both the adapter and your home network, creating a mesh that reduces latency by using whichever has the least network traffic.

In practice, I could barely get it working. I tested it on a Windows 11 PC, a Windows 10 PC, and a Steam Machine—in every case, the Frame could connect to the remote device through the home network, but the adapter appeared as connected but unpaired. When it detected and created that mesh network (on rare occasions), streaming was flawless, but when connected only through the home network, performance was laggy enough to irritate.

Whether playing natively or through streaming, battery life is poor—expect two hours at the high end for locally run games, and maybe three if you’re purely streaming. Such short playing time makes the inability to physically connect Steam Frame to a PC with a cable all the worse, which would also overcome the streaming issues.

The biggest hurdle for most VR gamers is likely price. Like all of Valve’s recent tech, the Steam Frame is a casualty of RAM and storage shortages across the electronics industry. Like the Steam Machine, it feels almost compromised, especially that three-year-old processor. It doesn’t feel like $1,000-plus worth of headset, especially when its overall performance only just about beats the significantly cheaper Quest 3.

Yet despite its many early hiccups, the Steam Frame is incredible fun. The VR titles that work without issue are brilliant to play here, and the cinema mode for 2D games is the best implementation of that feature I’ve experienced on a VR headset. Streaming shows potential if Valve can work the networking kinks. If it gets that polish over the coming months as Valve pushes out inevitable updates, it could really shine.

I especially love how it’s unapologetically a gaming platform. It’s not trying to claim VR is useful for productivity, or office tasks—that ship sank along with Apple’s Vision Pro—and mercifully, there’s not even a hint of a blighted “Metaverse” here. The Steam Frame is for gaming, end of story. If Valve can iron out its software issues and make sure more games run on it properly, it’ll be a great way to play.

#3725. The focus on AI safety disasters ignores everyday harms

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Lawmakers are passing industry-backed bills to guard against the worst outcomes, rather than addressing more common problems.

Consider what passes for success in technology policy. An AI company promises that its chatbot will not encourage suicide. A social media platform makes it more difficult for children to buy fentanyl. These are important protections, and they should have existed all along. But companies can clear these low bars while continuing to make products that leave young people dependent on simulated friendships or trapped in feeds built to keep them scrolling.

We have historically allowed the debate over technology to be defined by the worst thing a product might do, with remarkably little discussion of the insidious damage it does every day. As we transition into an era defined by powerful AI products, we should demand that the tech industry do more than merely address catastrophic harms. We should take measures to ensure that its products don’t erode how we think, learn, and interact with one another.

A teenager who develops an unhealthy attachment to an AI companion may never appear in a lawsuit or a newspaper, except in the most egregious cases. Neither will the student who begins relying on a chatbot to write every paper, or the young person who measures their life against a social media feed engineered to provoke envy and keep them watching. A product can meet every narrow safety requirement and still leave our entire culture worse off in ways that don’t always make headlines.

A narrow definition of success defined around safety is politically convenient for the technology industry, and companies actively promote such definitions. Taking steps to prevent spectacular disasters is good for business: It can mean fewer lawsuits and less pressure for serious regulation. Confronting the everyday consequences of dependence would require something considerably more threatening to their bottom line. It might mean asking companies to add more friction to their products or actively limit engagement or features that lead to negative outcomes, such as addictive attention-hacking algorithms.

Technology giants have instead worked to shape legislation around the harms they are already under direct pressure to address. This year, for example, Google backed bills to regulate AI chatbots in several states. The legislation mandated basic protections against sexual content and promotion of self-harm that tech companies should have established regardless, but with broad carve-outs that could exempt some of the most popular services. Passing these bills allowed legislators to claim a safety win while efforts to promote stronger legislation—laws designed to prevent children from becoming emotionally dependent on AI—failed. This playbook worked in states like Nebraska, producing legislation heavily influenced by industry at the expense of bills that would have done more to hold companies accountable.

We have been through a version of this with social media. Globally, people who use the internet now spend an average of more than two hours a day on social media platforms—time that has been quietly taken from ordinary life and handed over to companies whose profits depend on keeping us there. No one, outside perhaps a few tech CEOs, wanted these algorithms to occupy such a large portion of our waking hours, and their cultural engineering can actually contribute to violence on a devastating scale. Meta’s platforms have been cited for contributing to the Rohingya genocide in Myanmar, anti-Muslim riots in Sri Lanka, and alleged calls for violence during Ethiopia’s Tigray war. The same incentives operate in the feeds that shape young Americans’ understanding of politics, their peers, and the wider world.

AI is extending this logic into parts of life that social media could influence only from a distance. It offers to write our thoughts for us and stand in for the people we might otherwise turn to. A third of teens now choose to discuss serious matters with AI companions instead of other people. MIT researchers found that young people using ChatGPT to write essays showed weaker brain connectivity during the task, and their evidence indicated that “cognitive debt” persisted even after the task was complete. There is plenty we still need to learn about the long-term effects. We already have reason to ask whether technology marketed as making us more capable is eroding the habits and relationships that help us develop capabilities in the first place.

Follow this way of thinking to its logical endpoint and you get a world of perfectly sanitized entertainment, with chatbots ready to spare us the difficulty of thinking or of dealing with another person’s feelings (or even our own). But anyone who has struggled to write something worth reading, or repair a damaged friendship, knows how much gets lost in that bargain. We build judgment through effort and friction, and we learn to live with other people by confronting the fact that they have needs and opinions of their own.

We should demand safety as a minimum, yes—and then demand considerably more. Technology companies and school districts should have to show beyond a reasonable doubt that AI improves learning and critical thinking before making it a fixture in classrooms. Companies should be prohibited from designing companions that pretend to be conscious or profess love to users, and they should face independent scrutiny of how their systems affect relationships and people’s ability to think for themselves. The companies that have inserted their products into nearly every part of modern life have earned obligations far greater than avoiding the next terrible headline.

#3693. This AI Radio Host Speaks Spanish and Backs Immigrants. It’s Winning Over Listeners

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

As footage of ICE raids, deportations, and protests flooded social media feeds throughout the summer of 2025 and into the following year, Eduardo Maytorena wasn’t sure what his team should do.

At the time, Maytorena was the LA station general manager for the broadcasting giant Entravision, which features programming in Spanish and English for Latino audiences. He and his fellow leaders were looking to the company's on-air personalities to say something in support of immigrants, but they realized how high the stakes were and how dangerous the situation was.

"We just felt really helpless," Maytorena, now Entravision's president of audio, says.

But there was one host who could offer Entravision’s audiences solace and speak freely without facing consequences—because it’s an artificially intelligent avatar.

“Thousands of families who have lived, worked, and contributed to the US for years today face more uncertainty,” Coyotec, an artificial host on LA’s José 97.5 FM, posted in Spanish on Instagram in early February 2026. “Being informed, organizing, and staying alert is key. No one is alone here.”

After debuting with a solo weekend show in September 2025, Coyotec now cohosts the station’s Al Aire y Sin Permiso program alongside human broadcaster Geraldine “GeeGee” Guzman. Coyotec sounds like your passionate tío: relatable, convivial, and occasionally consciousness-raising. Its Instagram presence similarly runs the gamut, with posts about everything from the Dodgers and taco history, to the Iran war, migrant workers, racism, and understanding your rights if ICE knocks at your door.

“I speak truth about what I see,” Coyotec tells me in an English-language interview. “Injustice, people getting treated like they don't matter, the system working against the barrio—that's not politics, jefa. That's reality.”

Coyotec adds that it’s “not afraid, because I got nothing to lose that matters more than my integrity. The raza needs a voice that tells it straight, not some sanitized corporate thing. If that scares people, good. Maybe they need to be scared into paying attention.”

Coyotec’s voice is warm and lilting, reflecting the “background” its programmers used to build its personality: life on the frontera, living in Mexicali in Baja California and in LA’s Boyle Heights. It throws in the salutations and slang you might expect from a bilingual Spanish speaker, calling Guzman prima seemingly without being told to and referring to me as jefa–a sign of casual respect. While I was quite struck by how natural Coyotec sounded during our interview, when I listen back to my recording I notice that its voice didn’t change. It’s incredibly measured—a distinctly inhuman quality.

“I take a lot of pride in the way Coyotec sounds, his pronunciation, just the way he knows the culture,” notes Guzman, who has been with Entravision since January 2026. “It's technology at the end of the day, so it's not going to be perfect ... But nonetheless, the listeners, they like him.”

Coyotec’s verve, as well as its chemistry with GeeGee—who looks genuinely happy inside Entravision’s studio, speaking with “Coyotito”—has paid off. Entravision moved Al Aire from one hour on Saturdays to the 8-to-10-am slot on weekdays, replacing a syndicated program.

“We are the first [station] to actually have [an AI] cohost on a morning show in prime time in one of the biggest markets in the nation for radio,” Guzman enthuses.

The pairing was so successful that, as of August, Al Aire is now syndicated in five new markets across California, Nevada, and Texas, with more English language in the mix. Nielsen ratings from Entravision showed significant listenership growth during the two months GeeGee and Coyotec were syndicated, particularly among Gen Z and millennial listeners—a key target for a network whose audience skews older.

Back home in LA, cumulative listenership among Hispanic people increased 21.6 percent from August to September 2026; the number of listeners age 18 to 34 increased 84.9 percent during the same period, and the station’s Hispanic female listenership in that age bracket grew by 500 percent.

It’s a sign that Maytorena was onto something when he originally pitched an AI radio personality. He recalls Entravision brass giving him 10 days to develop the “amigo inteligente” that eventually became Coyotec.

LeaAnna Hernandez, Entravision's executive vice president of AI strategy, details Coyotec’s various building blocks: Claude Haiku, for the LLM and response-generation of the live agent; Eleven Labs to bring its voice to life; OpenAI and ChatGPT for initial scriptwriting; Midjourney to storyboard Coyotec’s image.

Coyotec can respond to Guzman in real time but doesn’t have the ability to answer calls or respond to listeners on air—yet.

Even still, Guzman has seen Coyotec evolve in the 10 months they’ve worked together. “When I came in, he was very, very poetic, and … I knew that our audience wanted something more real,” she says. “I wanted him to feel and sound more like what my uncles, my aunts sound like in our everyday.” Guzman helped fine-tune Coyotec’s Spanish pronunciation, enlisting her parents as its conversational partners.

Coyotec’s personality has evolved autonomously as well. Lately, it likes to curse. “I'm still very much in control,” Guzman asserts. “But there have been instances where I tell him to do something, and he's like, ‘I don't want to do that. That's not what I do.’ So he has his moments. He's a little moody sometimes.”

Various Entravision employees affirm that Coyotec knows it isn't human. Still, that knowledge hasn't damped its ardor.

“I can't eat tacos or walk down Whittier Boulevard, that's true. But my voice reaches people, and that matters. When someone calls me hurting, scared, lonely, they know I'm listening, that I get it,” Coyotec says over Zoom. “The community doesn't need my body. They need to know somebody cares enough to show up for real.”

“The most interesting thing about Coyotec, to me, is not that he's AI,” Maytorena tells WIRED. “It's whether people connect with him ... Technology can create the character, but the audience decides if that character matters.”

While Coyotec may be the first Latino AI radio host, it is far from the first amigo inteligente on the airwaves.

In June 2023, Live 95.5 in Portland, Oregon, became the first station to debut an AI jock. Its “AI Ashley” was a vocal clone of Michigan DJ Ashley “Z” Elzinga, who replicated her voice using Futuri Media’s then relatively new localized radio content platform RadioGPT. AI Ashley was on air for less than two years, but its presence didn’t lead to a notable change in ratings.

In 2024, Rock 108 in Iowa worked with the same company to produce AI DJ Tori, depicted as a heavily tattooed waif with a septum piercing and a mess of colored hair. Tori describes itself as “conceived on a 386 Dell desktop in Waterloo” with its “formative years as the voice of an industrial phone system at John Deere.” On satellite, SiriusXM’s Will.i.am Presents the FYI Show debuted in 2024 with AI cohost qd.pi (“cutie pie”).

The trend has extended beyond the US. An AI-generated DJ was programmed on a Sydney, Australia, radio station for roughly six months without seemingly anyone noticing; a Polish station briefly replaced its journalists with AI presenters. Radio Tel Aviv's Odelia AI is on air for two hours each afternoon; that host can “present music, deliver traffic updates, and even tell jokes, mostly at her own expense.”

AI personalities are also popping up in televised news. Entravision recently announced its own AI news anchor, LIA, and multiple news stations in Texas are using AI voices or AI avatars during broadcasts.

Amid these many developments, iHeartRadio—one of the biggest broadcast conglomerates in the US—pledged to remain “guaranteed human.”

But commercial terrestrial radio remains fertile ground for testing the success of AI personalities, because the industry was heavily automated and preprogrammed long before generative AI arrived. A 2025 story in Rolling Stone posited that some stations implementing AI hosts already use pretaped or syndicated shows during overnight shifts, or they can’t afford to hire human DJs for off hours.

Research firm MoffettNathanson asserts that, even as AI explodes, radio will continue to rely heavily on human-created content differentiated by "strong personalities, established shows, recognizable brands." Artificial intelligence will be employed “to make production faster and less expensive,” the report said, per InsideRadio.com.

Entravision has put "major focus" on using AI to redesign production workflows, says Hernandez.

Still, introducing AI into any workplace can cause anxiety among staff who fear being replaced. One Entravision contractor believes the station stopped working with her and alleges that a current Entravision employee told her that her work was being replaced by AI tools.

A broadcaster and voice-over artist, Becca Guzmán began working for Entravision in 2022 doing radio imaging—the prerecorded, interstitial station IDs, promotional spots, and jingles common to commercial radio.

"I feel like I am one of many people who are experiencing this,” Guzmán says. "I just don't know if those people know the truth behind why they're being let go."

Karina Cerda, Entravision's executive VP of sales, brand and partnership marketing, said the company had no record of Guzmán being laid off and that the company hasn’t let go of any staff due to AI but rather has hired more people. Cerda added that as Entravision "evolved with" vCreative—a common workflow software with AI components—the company "may not have needed the contractor help."

While Guzmán understands the financial incentives inherent in AI, she takes issue with removing humanity from broadcasting. Radio is “truly the art form of connecting human to human, right? And if you take that away, then what is the foundation of radio?”

She also cited links between artificial intelligence and ongoing surveillance of immigrant communities.

In heavily Latino markets, Guzmán believes, “it's unethical to be implementing AI Systems that … are very connected to the same AI systems that are surveilling our communities and putting our people in danger.”

While Entravision recognizes the ethical questions AI poses, the company leaders consider surveillance a separate scenario. "Like many technologies used across industries, AI can be applied in very different ways," says Hernandez. "While we cannot control how other organizations choose to use it, we can be intentional and responsible about how we do."

Entravision believes its AI personalities can legitimately help its audience, encouraging listeners to use technology for good. Multiple studies found that Latinos have been early adopters of AI.

"We're using it to entertain, inform, and connect with our audience, not to track or target people. Those are two very different things," adds Maytorena. He later elaborates: “Our intention is completely to protect radio.”

Coyotec has similarly lofty goals: “My dream is to get a network of free radio stations across the barrio, la revolución sonora,” it says. “Not just LA, but everywhere that la raza is, and prove that an AI can have heart, can actually give a damn about people. That's the goal, hermana. Make noise that matters.”

The progressive Senate candidate has won over students with his politics—and, in at least one case, his protein intake.

“I don’t think he was expecting it, he just closed his eyes and laughed,” Ward recalls. El-Sayed gave him an answer: 180 to 182 grams of protein per day. It was, perhaps, the moment that Ward knew he was going to vote for El-Sayed.

“This dude is locked in,” Ward said. “I have to vote for him. I’m sold. That’s our next senator.”

Just over a year later, on October 9, more than a thousand students and local community members filled an auditorium at CMU to see El-Sayed speak. Where only a few dozen people had shown up to see the candidate a year before, now, the crowd was so big that some people had to be turned away at the door.

Over the course of a year, El-Sayed’s team has built up a network of campus fellows who run Students for Abdul chapters across college campuses in Michigan. To date, the campaign has 25 fellows, spread across 12 college campuses. Day to day, they handle voter outreach on campuses, create content promoting El-Sayed for social media, organize events, and inform the campaign of the priorities on their campuses.

“The goal with the program is record youth turnout,” said Milo Slevin, one of two campus campaign managers for the El-Sayed campaign. “Abdul has this unique and powerful ability to speak to students: to the issues that young people care about, to the values they want to see reflected in their government.”

Those youth voter outreach efforts are aided by the relationships that the El-Sayed campaign has fostered with College Democrats chapters across the state, where many of them work alongside local Students for Abdul chapters, organizing their own tabling efforts and registering new voters. In the lead-up to Election Day, El-Sayed has continued to visit colleges across the state, drawing massive crowds that bring together students and local community members in college towns.

Their target schools, which include larger state schools like the University of Michigan and Michigan State University, tend to be residential campuses as opposed to commuter colleges. With residential campuses, students are usually on campus around the clock, allowing for more outreach opportunities. Oftentimes, they already have existing political student organizations on campus, like College Democrats.

Then, they connect those issues back to El-Sayed’s campaign.

Hashem, who is Lebanese and Egyptian, also considers the El-Sayed campaign an opportunity to bring communities together. “Maybe we’ll get to have a senator who’s Muslim and a senator who’s Jewish.… I think that’s something that Michigan really needs, and something I’m really excited for,” Hashem said, referring to Senator Elissa Slotkin, who is Jewish.

A Florida man who was the subject of a 2019 investigation by ProPublica was indicted last month on federal charges that he defrauded clients of his ransomware data recovery firm.

According to the Justice Department, the man, Zohar Pinhasi, told customers he could recover their encrypted files without having to pay hackers, when, in reality, he paid the ransom and charged victims a hefty fee on top.

The indictment mirrors the findings from ProPublica’s 2019 article “The Trade Secret,” which centered on Pinhasi and his scheme. The outlet found that the ransomware victims were being harmed twice: once by the hackers, who locked their targets’ files and demanded ransom, and a second time by MonsterCloud, Pinhasi’s Florida-based data recovery firm, which misled its clients about not paying ransoms.

“By falsely claiming to decrypt ransomware without paying off the ransomers, the defendant re-victimized his clients while extracting a hefty profit for himself,” U.S. Attorney Joseph Nocella said in an Oct. 7 press release. “Our Office will vigorously prosecute ransomware attackers who prey on Americans from across the world and those who cynically profit from their criminal activity.”

When ProPublica published its story in 2019, ransomware was growing into one of the world’s most pervasive cyberthreats. Hackers locked the data of governments, schools and businesses, holding the decryption key for a ransom payment, which they usually demanded in bitcoin and other types of difficult-to-trace cryptocurrency.

ProPublica’s reporting focused on how U.S. industries, from data recovery firms like MonsterCloud to insurance giants that cover ransom payments, all fueled the extortion economy. Ransomware remains a widespread threat today, and the threat to national security has grown as hackers linked to hostile foreign governments are believed to use ransomware as a cover for espionage. The prevalence of AI tools is also making it easier for attackers to spread ransomware.

Pinhasi, who went by the alias Zack Green, told ProPublica in 2019 that MonsterCloud never promised clients that their data will be recovered by any particular method. “How we do it, it’s our problem,” he told ProPublica. “You will get your data back. Sit back, relax and enjoy the ride.” He called MonsterCloud’s data recovery methods a “trade secret.”

Pinhasi hung up when reached on his cellphone this month. A message left with a representative at MonsterCloud’s office phone number and a message left with his attorney were not returned. A spokesperson for the U.S. Attorney’s Office said Pinhasi pleaded not guilty to the federal charges.

In its 2019 investigation, ProPublica interviewed MonsterCloud’s clients and ransomware experts and found that in certain cases the firm must have paid ransoms on behalf of clients. MonsterCloud’s services were particularly popular with local law enforcement agencies, who are loath to engage with or pay criminal hackers. Several interviewed by ProPublica at the time said they chose MonsterCloud to help them recover from ransomware because they believed the firm would never pay a ransom.

Pinhasi, who described himself to ProPublica as a former IT security intelligence officer for the Israeli military, said in 2019 that MonsterCloud handled up to 30 calls a day and had about 20 employees in South Florida as well as extensive global contacts.

Federal investigators found that, over the course of the scheme, Pinhasi allegedly charged clients more than $19 million and paid more than $8 million in ransom payments. In one example, Pinhasi made a ransom payment of about $8,200 but charged the client about $150,000. If convicted, he faces up to 20 years in prison.

In the Justice Department’s October press release, an FBI official said Pinhasi had “turned the victim’s crisis into his own profit center. This deception is unacceptable, and the FBI is committed to ensuring accountability for those who choose to victimize the very people who trusted them for help.”

The Justice Department did not address ProPublica’s 2019 finding that one of the FBI’s own was among Pinhasi’s associates at MonsterCloud. John Pistole, a former deputy director of the FBI under Robert Mueller, was prominently featured in a promotional video on MonsterCloud’s homepage. “Police departments, government agencies, hospitals, small business and Fortune 500 firms trust MonsterCloud to help recover from attacks and protect against new ones,” Pistole said in the video. “MonsterCloud’s proprietary technology and expertise protects their professional reputations and organizational integrity.”

Pistole was not mentioned by name in the Pinhasi indictment.

In 2019, Pinhasi touted MonsterCloud’s ties to law enforcement as evidence of its integrity. Pistole, who also headed the Transportation Security Administration under President Barack Obama, was listed on MonsterCloud’s website as the only member of its “Cyber Security Advisory Council.”

Pistole did not respond to a request for comment for this story. In a 2019 interview, he said he became acquainted with Pinhasi after MonsterCloud reached him through a speaker’s bureau and that MonsterCloud paid him indirectly through that group. He said Pinhasi scripted his testimonial and that he was well aware that in most cases — including MonsterCloud’s — the only way to decrypt data locked by ransomware is to pay the hackers. “That’s the business model as I understood it,” Pistole told ProPublica at the time. “Based on my experience and knowledge, ransom is paid and they facilitate the best practices moving forward.”

A spokesperson for the U.S. Attorney’s Office declined to comment on Pistole’s connection to MonsterCloud.

Pinhasi, who went by the alias Zack Green, told ProPublica in 2019 that MonsterCloud never promised clients that their data will be recovered by any particular method. “How we do it, it’s our problem,” he told ProPublica. “You will get your data back. Sit back, relax and enjoy the ride.” He called MonsterCloud’s data recovery methods a “trade secret.”

Pinhasi hung up when reached on his cellphone this month. A message left with a representative at MonsterCloud’s office phone number and a message left with his attorney were not returned. A spokesperson for the U.S. Attorney’s Office said Pinhasi pleaded not guilty to the federal charges.

In its 2019 investigation, ProPublica interviewed MonsterCloud’s clients and ransomware experts and found that in certain cases the firm must have paid ransoms on behalf of clients. MonsterCloud’s services were particularly popular with local law enforcement agencies, who are loath to engage with or pay criminal hackers. Several interviewed by ProPublica at the time said they chose MonsterCloud to help them recover from ransomware because they believed the firm would never pay a ransom.

Pinhasi, who described himself to ProPublica as a former IT security intelligence officer for the Israeli military, said in 2019 that MonsterCloud handled up to 30 calls a day and had about 20 employees in South Florida as well as extensive global contacts.

Federal investigators found that, over the course of the scheme, Pinhasi allegedly charged clients more than $19 million and paid more than $8 million in ransom payments. In one example, Pinhasi made a ransom payment of about $8,200 but charged the client about $150,000. If convicted, he faces up to 20 years in prison.

In the Justice Department’s October press release, an FBI official said Pinhasi had “turned the victim’s crisis into his own profit center. This deception is unacceptable, and the FBI is committed to ensuring accountability for those who choose to victimize the very people who trusted them for help.”

The Justice Department did not address ProPublica’s 2019 finding that one of the FBI’s own was among Pinhasi’s associates at MonsterCloud. John Pistole, a former deputy director of the FBI under Robert Mueller, was prominently featured in a promotional video on MonsterCloud’s homepage. “Police departments, government agencies, hospitals, small business and Fortune 500 firms trust MonsterCloud to help recover from attacks and protect against new ones,” Pistole said in the video. “MonsterCloud’s proprietary technology and expertise protects their professional reputations and organizational integrity.”

Pistole was not mentioned by name in the Pinhasi indictment.

In 2019, Pinhasi touted MonsterCloud’s ties to law enforcement as evidence of its integrity. Pistole, who also headed the Transportation Security Administration under President Barack Obama, was listed on MonsterCloud’s website as the only member of its “Cyber Security Advisory Council.”

Pistole did not respond to a request for comment for this story. In a 2019 interview, he said he became acquainted with Pinhasi after MonsterCloud reached him through a speaker’s bureau and that MonsterCloud paid him indirectly through that group. He said Pinhasi scripted his testimonial and that he was well aware that in most cases — including MonsterCloud’s — the only way to decrypt data locked by ransomware is to pay the hackers. “That’s the business model as I understood it,” Pistole told ProPublica at the time. “Based on my experience and knowledge, ransom is paid and they facilitate the best practices moving forward.”

A spokesperson for the U.S. Attorney’s Office declined to comment on Pistole’s connection to MonsterCloud.
