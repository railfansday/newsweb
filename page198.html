#4118. Bat mothers’ hearts beat faster when they hear their pups

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Published Oct 23, 2026 12:00 AM EDT

A single bat colony contains hundreds or even thousands of the winged mammals. But with less-than-ideal eyesight and so many neighbors, how do mothers identify their pups amid the extremely crowded conditions? Writing in a new study published today in the journal Frontiers in Ethology, researchers say it often comes down to the subtle, unique vocalizations emitted by their individual offspring. To assess their responses, the team relied on an even more delicate biological signal—the mother bat’s own heartbeat.

The investigation began when biologists at Japan’s Doshisha University noticed a surprising—but consistent—behavior among the Japanese house bats (Alionoctula abramus) in their laboratory colony. Whenever a pup wandered too far from its roosting area, only its mother raced to the rescue.

“We knew that pups emit isolation calls—acoustic distress signals—when separated. But seeing this strict behavioral selectivity made us wonder: what is happening inside the mother when she hears these calls?” recalled study co-author and Doshisha University biologist Kazuki Yoshino-Hashizawa.

To learn more, Yoshino-Hashizawa and his colleagues monitored four mother bats who each recently birthed a litter of three pups. Some of the litters only contained females, so they decided to exclude the young males from the study to avoid sex-based variations. At two days old, the team recorded clips of the female pups’ tiny isolation calls. These function like the cries from a human baby, and each vocalization has its own unique tones.

However, Japanese house bats grow quickly. The new generation started making echolocation calls at about eight days old, and they ceased isolation calls after around 12 days. After roughly one month, they began flying like adults and set off on their own within the colony. The researchers then recorded their adult echolocation calls, as well.

Around 40 days after the pups’ birth, the team isolated each mother and played them the various audio clips while monitoring their heart rates using an ECG machine. As a control, they also played the recording of a random adult male bat. The study’s authors quickly saw that a mother bat’s heart rate sped up when they heard cries of their own pups, but not the pups that belong to other bat moms. Surprisingly, the mother bats’ heart rates boosted the most from recordings of the older, independent pups. Researchers theorize this might relate to the bats’ memory of more recent calls, but that idea requires more study to confirm.

Still, don’t get overly sentimental. The team also recorded strong heart rate reactions when the mothers heard calls from the random adult male. It’s possible the bats interpreted the sounds as a threat, but the team stopped short of categorizing the reactions as good or bad.

“‘[Heart rate measurement] cannot distinguish between positive emotions, such as maternal motivation, and negative ones, such as stress or alarm,” Yoshino-Hashizawa added.

Instead, the study authors suggest heart rates may be a solid starting point when trying to analyze bat behaviors and responses to their everyday environment. At the same time, a mother bat’s heart certainly seems to patter a bit more for her pups.

“[This] suggests that these vocalizations instantly physiologically prime the mother to engage in maternal behavior,” explained Yoshino-Hashizawa.

#4229. Why Does Anything Exist at All? Physicists Think a Humble ‘Ghost Particle’ May Hold the Answer

📅 22 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

An overlooked particle may have briefly ruled the early cosmos, changing how matter survived.

The universe owes its existence to a tiny mismatch, but one of the greatest consequences imaginable. In its earliest moments, matter and antimatter annihilated on contact, converting their mass into energy. Equal amounts of matter and antimatter are predicted to have formed in the split-seconds following the Big Bang. Yet for roughly every billion pairs, an extra particle of matter survived.

That thin residue of matter that escaped annihilation eventually became galaxies, planets, and us.

What gave matter its edge over antimatter? In a new study published in Physical Review Letters, physicists Rishav Roshan of the University of Southampton and Sudipta Show of the Indian Institute of Technology Kanpur suggest that an often-overlooked relative of the neutrino may have played an unexpected role. Neutrinos are sometimes affectionately called ‘ghost particles’ because they pass through ordinary matter almost without interacting. They have no electric charge, almost no mass, and feel only the weak nuclear force, so an average neutrino can fly through the entire Earth — or a wall of lead light-years thick — and emerge on the other side unchanged. About 100 trillion neutrinos pass through your body each second.

Physicists have long considered a theoretical explanation for their tiny masses called the seesaw mechanism, involving three far heavier neutrino relatives. Two can explain the known neutrino masses and contribute to a matter excess. The third, so weakly connected to other particles that it seems inconsequential, is often set aside.

But, ironically, it’s this last neutrino relative, which particle physicists have largely written off, that may explain why anything exists at all.

Its apparent weakness may have been its greatest strength. Roshan and Show’s calculations suggest this forgotten particle could have survived long enough to briefly dominate the infant universe, changing how fast it expanded and, crucially, how much matter survived to form everything we see today.

“This decoupled heavy neutrino has long been dismissed as an irrelevant background detail in particle physics,” Roshan said.

Roshan and Show focused on the neglected heavy neutrino, known as N3. In one of their calculations, this hypothetical particle was about 100 trillion times heavier than a proton. Yet it interacted so weakly with other particles that it could survive for an unusually long time. The researchers assumed that the extremely hot early universe had initially produced an enormous number of these particles.

At first, the heavy neutrinos moved at nearly the speed of light, behaving much like radiation. But as the universe expanded and cooled, they slowed down and began behaving like matter.

Physicists had previously established that as the universe expands, radiation loses energy faster than matter does. So, while the energy stored in radiation rapidly dwindled, the heavy neutrinos lost energy more slowly. Eventually, they accounted for most of the universe’s energy. For a brief period, these exotic particles effectively ruled the cosmos, making it expand faster than it would have if radiation had remained dominant.

Eventually, the heavy neutrinos decayed, releasing their energy back into the surrounding sea of particles. Radiation once again took over. But the temporary change in cosmic expansion had important long-term consequences.

But why would any of this affect the balance between matter and antimatter?

In the early universe, other heavy neutrinos could decay into particles of matter and antimatter. Crucially, these decays could produce slightly more of one than the other, creating the beginnings of the imbalance that eventually allowed ordinary matter to survive.

But producing an imbalance wasn’t enough. The young universe was a crowded, chaotic place, filled with reactions that could alter or erase the tiny advantage in matter over antimatter.

Some of these reactions involved the Higgs field (an invisible energy field that permeates the entire universe and gives mass to fundamental particles) and tau leptons, heavier cousins of electrons. Whether those reactions were active determined how the matter-antimatter imbalance evolved and how much of it could survive.

By changing the universe’s expansion rate, the neglected neutrino delayed the point at which these reactions became effective. In one calculation, they began influencing the outcome only after the universe had cooled to a temperature seven times lower than usual.

The eventual decay of N3 also released energy that diluted it. When the researchers accounted for both effects, they found that, under certain conditions, the universe could end up with precisely the tiny excess of matter we observe today.

In other words, while N3 didn’t create the imbalance between matter and antimatter directly, it may have helped determine how much of that imbalance survived.

“The fact that we are here at all proves that an asymmetry between matter and antimatter occurred,” Roshan said.

“The decoupled neutrino shifted the temperature in the early universe at which fundamental particles – like electrons and other leptons – interacted with their anti-particles with the help of Standard Model Higgs boson.

“This temperature acts like a switch that shapes how the matter-antimatter imbalance was built up.”

The proposal builds on leptogenesis, an idea developed in 1986 by physicists Masataka Fukugita and Tsutomu Yanagida. In this picture, decays of heavy neutrinos produce slightly different amounts of leptons and antileptons. Other processes in the young universe convert some of that difference into an excess of the particles that make up ordinary matter.

The overlooked neutrino does not create that excess. Instead, by changing how fast the universe expands and when lepton interactions become effective, it changes how much of the excess can survive.

With selected parameters, the researchers’ calculations matched today’s observed matter excess. Their results show the excess growing, then shrinking as the long-lived particles decay. That demonstrates the scenario can work, not that it happened — all of this was done in a theoretical framework, without any experiment or observation to back it up.

In a 2024 study, Roshan and colleagues showed that a prolonged period of reheating after cosmic inflation could also shift the timing of lepton interactions. The new work identifies a different possible cause: the humble and neglected neutrino N3.

The theory developed by the researchers also makes a striking prediction: the lightest of the three known neutrinos should be almost completely massless, weighing just a trillionth to a hundred-trillionth of an electronvolt. That could eventually help physicists test the idea.

Measuring something so extraordinarily light is a formidable challenge. Germany’s KATRIN experiment, one of the world’s most sensitive neutrino experiments, has so far established only that a combined measure of neutrino mass is below 0.45 electronvolts. It cannot directly confirm the much smaller mass predicted by the new theory.

Another possible clue could lie in primordial gravitational waves, ripples in space-time whose patterns might preserve evidence of an early matter-dominated era.

“These gravitational wave signatures open up new avenues for us to unravel the evolution of the early universe after the Big Bang,” Show said.

But searches have yet to detect an early-universe gravitational-wave background.

There is also a crucial assumption that, if untrue, would unravel the whole theory like a house of cards: the weakly interacting heavy neutrinos had to be produced in the first place. The authors suggest ways this might have happened, but none is established.

For now, the study does not establish why matter won (and why you and I exist). But particle long ignored might have changed the rules of the contest, and that’s one cool physics story.

#4360. Burnham to reveal plans for AI unit to tackle wildfires after scorching summer

📅 22 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

PM will also announce funding for specialised protective gear and the deployment of military aircraft

The UK government is to create an AI Cobra unit to spot wildfires after this year’s record-breaking summer saw devastating blazes across the country.

The country experienced its hottest-ever summer this year, with prolonged dry periods and extreme heat fuelling wildfires that triggered an emergency alert to mobile phones in England and Wales. Figures released on 8 October showed a clear upward trend in the number of fires recorded each year.

The Met Office found that the climate crisis made this extreme heat about 130 times more likely.

At the government’s first wildfire resilience summit on Friday, Andy Burnham will announce plans for a new wildfire AI unit in the National Situation Centre, which is part of the Cobra briefing rooms.

Businesses including Google, Airbus and VodafoneThree are expected to attend the summit to set out how satellite monitoring and early warning systems can help fight fires. The government said its new AI unit would enable it to “spot and fight fires faster”.

The prime minister will also announce £45m for fire crews to buy wildfire-specific personal protective equipment (PPE).

This comes after the Guardian revealed that more than half of the 29 fire services that responded to requests for information had no wildfire PPE.

They included the Suffolk fire and rescue service, which had to deal with an “apocalyptic” wildfire earlier this summer that burned close to the Sizewell B nuclear power station and destroyed vast areas of a protected nature reserve.

Standard particle-filtering respirators do not protect against dangerous gases such as carbon monoxide, which are heavily present in wildfire smoke.

Standard PPE can also be too hot and heavy for wildfire conditions. This can cause crews to overheat, or remove their PPE altogether, exposing them to greater danger from heat, smoke and radiation.

The prime minister will also announce that military planes and helicopters will be made available to fight wildfires on remote moorland, steep hillsides and other hard-to-reach terrain.

The defence secretary, Wes Streeting, said: “As the threat of wildfires in the UK grows, defence will step up to provide greater support to our emergency services and protect military training areas across the country.”

The government will also look at tougher local restrictions or product bans to stop people starting fires when the risk is high. In August, Burnham announced a temporary ban on sales of disposable barbecues. He has also been criticised for failing to address the climate emergency in his wildfire response.

Farmers will also be given funding to make landscapes more resilient to fires. This announcement comes after the government quietly scrapped plans announced under the Conservative government to prepare the countryside in England for wildfires.

This revelation led Alistair Carmichael, a Scottish Liberal Democrat MP and chair of the Commons environment, food and rural affairs committee, to raise “serious questions about whether this summer’s wildfires could have looked different with more resources on the ground”.

The government also said it would allocate £25m to restore dried-out peatlands in England so they hold more water and act as natural firebreaks, as well as provide financial support for farmers to fireproof their land.

This new grant scheme is expected to run from April 2027 to April 2029.

Environmental campaigner and author Guy Shrubsole said: “For decades, wealthy grouse moor owners have drained our peat bogs, turning them into a tinderbox. This grant will help reverse that destruction, but must be followed by a new round of landscape recovery funding, and duties on landowners to repair the damage done. It’s time to make England wet again.”

The prime minister said: “I stood in Stourbridge and watched families lose everything. I will never forget it. Our firefighters were heroes that day, but heroes shouldn’t have to fight with one hand tied behind their backs.

“Crews will now have the kit they need to stay safe and military aircraft will stand ready to help tackle wildfires from the sky. We cannot put people through another summer like this one.”

#4356. The 17 Best Cocktail Bars in Milan, Where Aperitivo Is Just the Beginning

📅 22 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

One common misconception about cocktail bars in Milan is that the city’s drinking culture begins and ends with the aperitivo. Yes, the pre-dinner ritual still defines much of the city’s social culture, but the bar scene in Milan has evolved significantly over the past few years, thanks to a brigade of hospitality-minded, boundary-pushing talent.

While the Spritz and the Negroni Sbagliato aren’t going anywhere anytime soon, a new wave of mixologists has elaborated on the customary red-orange palette, pouring an array of jewel-toned beverages layered with technique, seasonality and innovation. Some of these cocktail bars in Milan are part of larger operations with restaurants; others distinguish themselves independently. From the classic to the new, here are 18 of the best cocktail bars in Milan where even teetotalers will drink exceptionally well. Reservations are recommended whenever possible.

Like its equally multi-faceted sibling Moebius (named after the French cartoonist), Lubna claims a namesake: a radical Italian comic book heroine of the 1970s. It arrived on the scene in December 2024 inside a former tram depot in the district dubbed SouPra (“South of Prada”—the Foundation, that is). The vast, faintly industrial open warehouse incorporates concrete and wood surfaces, and glass walls frame views of the al fresco crowd in the courtyard. The layout splits between a restaurant and a cocktail bar, with a listening lounge tucked into the back corner, whose walls come alive with flickering anime cartoons. The cocktails echo executive chef Enrico Croatti’s flame-driven cooking, incorporating ingredients grilled or charred over open fire. The Strawberry and Roasted Capsicum is a smoky riff on a Negroni, built from mezcal and diluted juice from a roasted pepper, while a spin on the tequila-based Toreador adds yoghurt and house-made “black oil” infused with fiery charcoal ember.

Rita, Edoardo Nono and Chiara Buzzi’s modern classic opened in 2002 along the Naviglio Grande—well before the banks of the canal-crossed district evolved into the bar-loaded hotbed it is today. Nono and his team pioneered practices now considered commonplace, such as house-made infusions and juices and buffet-free quality aperitivo eats served at the table. Sit at the U-shaped Larchwood bar backed by a bottle rack displaying over 500 premium labels that stretch to the ceiling, or plop yourself on one of the red banquettes edging the walls. Among the ever-changing, inventive cocktails is one retro throwback: the Gin Zen, a 90s-style hodgepodge of crushed ice, gin, muddled fresh ginger, lime, and soda that Nono’s been mixing since day one. Just across the street is Rita’s Tiki Room, which opened in 2019.

This epic institution evokes the charm of a bygone era while still setting the benchmark for cool. Avail yourself of a Negroni Sbagliato, or the “Mistaken Negroni” swapping prosecco for gin, created in 1972 by late owner Mirko Stocchetto. Better yet, ask for “uno Sbagliato grande” which swims among clinking ice cubes in a glass goblet bigger than your head. If you’re not in the mood for bitter, Bar Basso’s plentiful cocktail list runs 500 long and counting. The clientele represents all walks of life: young and old, regulars and newbies, locals and travellers. Stocchetto’s son Maurizio, the current owner, marked the 50th anniversary with a light refurbishment but one that kept the vintage backbone intact, from the brocades and mirrors to the marble floor and dark wood panels. The crowds can spill onto the sidewalk any given evening (especially during Design Week), so make sure to book in advance for prime-time imbibing.

Bob The Other Side is the newest venture from Luca and Michele Hu, the twin brothers behind the Chinese Box Group and longtime stalwarts of Milan’s mixology scene. The name is literal: the bar is just beside Bob Milano, their cult Isola cocktail spot, hidden behind an unassuming teal door that recalls the entryways of old Milanese tenements. Step inside, though, and the mood shifts entirely. The design plays with geometry, reflection and depth—think warm wood panelling, bronzed mirrors, walnut tables, brass accents and luminous geometric details, including a striking hexagonal counter. Cesar Araujo, who also leads the cocktail program at Bob, crafts the drinks, which include the Hidden Love with gin, tomato–pomegranate cordial, and Timut pepper; Ray of Illusions, blending pisco, sherry, black sesame and apple-flavoured matcha; and Holy Water, a lush mix of Woodford Bourbon, spiced honey, citrus and coconut milk infused with Lapsang.

Don’t be alarmed by Camparino’s proximity to the Duomo—there’s nothing tourist-trappy about this 110-year-old mainstay. At the ground-floor Bar di Passo, sip Campari classics at the counter, surrounded by an exquisite Art Deco backdrop that has captivated patrons since its opening. In the upstairs Sala Spiritello, Tommaso Cecca and his bar team push boundaries with creative cocktail pairings. The current menu, crafted in honour of Camparino’s 110th anniversary, traces Camparino’s history through 12 drinks, starting in 1915, then marking the start of each subsequent decade through 2025. Among them: Italian Blood (1935), with Campari, tomato water and aromatic herbs; Lacto Garibaldi (1975), which combines Campari with lacto-fermented orange juice; and the Breeze (1995), a vibrant blend of Campari, vodka, pomegranate juice, zallotti blossom, and pink grapefruit soda. At the intimate, reservations-only Sala Gaspare, named for Campari’s founder, the drinks reimagine the aromas and ingredients that make up his iconic bitter.

Occupying a 700-square-metre former textile warehouse, Moebius juggles multiple concepts, one of which is a cocktail bar that currently ranks seventh on the World’s 50 Best Bars list. Bar manager Giovanni Allario weaves unexpected twists into classics, like the Pesto Martini with vodka, white balsamic vinegar, and homemade pesto and a Gremolada Negroni with notes of lemon and parsley. The decor juxtaposes its iron-flecked industrial skeleton with warm and natural accents like a 700-year-old Andalusian olive tree. A backlit concrete bottle rack extends three metres high from behind a light wood counter that seats seven, while wood tables and couches and chairs in earthy grey, brown, and blue hues speckle the boundless venue.

Don’t let the name fool you – Dario Comini’s trailblazing molecular mixology mecca is about as far from a British sports pub as it gets. The elaborate, experiential drinks are presented on trays or pedestals alongside clever fixings. The French Kiss rests beside illuminated lips, clutching an aphrodisiac flower, and patrons put the finishing touches on the signature C.S.I. cocktail with syringes and test tubes. A dark wood bar salvaged from New York’s Knickerbocker Hotel anchors the room from its post in front of shelves crammed with over 1800 bottles. Dozens of eclectic objects, like stone buddhas and African masks, that Comini procured from his travels, complete the treasure trove look. No reservations, no exceptions. Ever. There’s always a line, but it’s worth the wait. If you get there around 10-15 minutes before it opens, you should be able to get seated during the first turn.

Named after a former Futurist magazine, Lacerba opened in 1999 and strikes the perfect balance between nostalgic and zany: vintage ads and abstract art adorn the walls, dozens of empty bottles dangle from the ceiling, an illuminated cutout of Milan’s Duomo twinkles beside shelves stocked with analogue clocks and travel books – an ambience that can dub as an icebreaker if you’re struggling for conversation on, say, a first date. Though, Lacerba’s always a blast with friends and loved ones. Of owner Angelo Trapasso’s 51 cocktails, five change monthly – a sure bet year round is the Discorso aritmetico alla fidanzata (Arithmetic Speech to the Girlfriend), a riff on the classic Americano. Lacerba’s famous Bloody Mary menu features ten recipes ranging from classic to playful, like the flowery Saint Mary in Love with dry sherry and elderflower liqueur.

Luca Marcellin’s 26-seat cocktail bar couples the conviviality of a neighborhood place with the intrigue of a speakeasy. You ring a bell to enter, but the mystery isn’t so much what’s inside as the windowed facade reveals a lively, chatty crowd, but rather in the curiously clever cocktails, like the Negroni all’avocado, which incorporates their own avocado oil distillate or the Sazerac Fire, mixed with Cognac, cocoa beans and chilli pepper, and hazelnut bitter. Gin and tonic lovers can choose from five versions. Drinc.different, which Marcellin co-owns with Desiree Brunet, is just five minutes from its sibling. The narrow, 18-seat nook feels equally inviting but more discreet with low tables and chairs and dim lighting—perfect for date night. The equally curious drinks include N’Doritos with Doritos-infused Pisco, red vermouth, and chocolate bitter, and the refreshing Fra’Paloma with tequila, cinnamon, citric, grapefruit soda, and a house-infused strawberry and vanilla mezcal.

Niccolò Caramiello and Stefano Rollo’s bar in Lambrate evokes “Old Milan” with its sleek green Guatemalan marble bar top and custom-made furnishings procured from recycled materials, lived-in walls, and dark wood floors. Absinthe is the star of the glass here, with 11 Italian, Swiss, and French labels on offer, appearing in reworked classics like the Absinthe Marini, Absinthe Rita, Absinthe Mule, and Absinthe Julep. For an alternative to the anise-flavoured liqueur, try the Million Dollar Hotel made with vodka, port, mastiha, beets, and a hint of wasabi. Provisions include tinned Spanish and Portuguese fish and French oysters—it’s not unusual for the latter to appear in a martini. Earlier this year, the team opened Cactus Joe, a cocktail bar focused on agave distillates. Who’s Norah? Accounts may vary, but the one consistency is that she’s a figment of the owners’ imagination.

An anagram for “Casual Ristorante Cocktails”, Carico also means “powered” in Italian, a fitting pun to a concept packing punchiness and prowess. Domenico Carella oversees a selection of cutting-edge drinks, like the Capsicum Funky, with tequila, white chocolate, jalapeño, and citrus; Arantius Siesta, a mix of mezcal, sherry, orange, and sesame; and the Chipotloni, a negroni kicked up with Chipotle. Can’t decide? Opt for the mini cocktail route: four half-portions for 30€, or stick to reliable staples like a gin and tonic—with over two dozen types of gin to choose from. The kitchen delivers playful, elevated small plates, such as a croque monsieur with mortadella and Parmigiano fonduta, and pulled pork tacos, among others. Every so often, the seven-seat Negroni Room pops up for an experiential, all-sensory food-and-drink experience dedicated to its namesake legend—book your spot in advance. Carella is also one of the forces behind Ultra, a pizzeria and cocktail bar that opened last year.

The Bulgari Hotel’s swanky bar area centres around a glossy, oval black resin island that seats 20 and rests beside a glass wall looking out at the garden. Bar manager Patrick Greco and his team update the menu twice a year, and on the list today are a half-dozen evocatively named creations. The Polaris dazzles with tequila, mezcal, blackberry syrup, and a touch of citrus air foam, while the Hydra balances gin, mastiha, carrot, celery, and a bright, zesty twist of lime. For a deeper dive, the Belvedere Martini Experience presents two scaled-down Belvedere-based martinis side by side to illustrate the subtle contrasts in flavour, aroma, and texture. When the weather cooperates, book an al fresco table among the pagoda trees and boxwoods of the lush garden that feels light years away from the city.

The eighth floor of Casa Brera, a Luxury Collection Hotel, Milan, houses one of the city’s most stunning rooftop venues: Etereo, a sky bar and restaurant whose spectacular 360-degree panorama of the city encompasses the Duomo, Galleria, Castello Sforzesco and the Porta Nuova skyscrapers. The space feels both vintage and contemporary, decorated in shades of cherry red, brown sugar and desert sand, with windowed walls, cane-backed chairs and lacquered tables, all anchored around a red arabesque marble bar. The “Art of the Aperitivo” cocktail list features 13 signature drinks, each paying tribute to a different Milanese neighbourhood. The Bell’Art Martini, an ode to the Brera, combines vodka, gin and white penja and is finished with a spritz of black pepper, while the Heritage draws inspiration from Castello Sforzesco and Corso Sempione, mixing tequila, mezcal, mastiha, tonic, angostura bitters, Champagne, passion fruit, and a touch of green mandarin.

The first cocktail bar in Milan from Flavio Angiolillo’s now epic Farmily Group, MAG Cafe, opened in the heart of the Navigli in 2011. The cocktail current roster, named “World Comes to Milan”, features a selection of cleverly crafted cocktails, each named after a different country, in honour of the 2026 Winter Olympics. China combines ginseng rum, litchi liqueur, rice-and-peach cordial, sapsang souchong tea and lime, while France mixes Cognac Martell VS, prickly pear distillate, Ratatrip (a proprietary take on French ratafia), ratatouille water, wine-tomato reduction and lemon. The boho-vintage interior ups the charm factor: an Argentine pharmacy counter, dangling bicycle wheels affixed with lights resembling the spokes of a ship helm, and ever-changing paintings and ornaments. The group’s other establishments include La Pusterla, MAG’s sibling; 1930, the elusive invite-only speakeasy; Backdoor 43, the world’s smallest bar; Iter, a cocktail bar equipped with a proper kitchen; and two outposts of Tripstillery, Italy’s first cocktail bar with a distillery onsite.

In Milanese dialect, “Tusa” means “girl”, the inspiration for owner Norma Galdamez’s cocktails, all named after 20th-century female trailblazers. The Sissi combines rum, lime and chestnut honey, with notes of coffee and mint, for a tipple as intense, sweet and unpredictable as its namesake princess. #4239. Astronomers Found Something They Thought Couldn’t Exist: A Green Star

📅 22 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Astronomers had never before convincingly identified a green star.

The universe is full of red stars, blue stars, and white stars. But green? Despite billions upon billions of stars scattered across the cosmos, astronomers had never convincingly identified a green star.

Now, they’ve found one. And the explanation is stranger than you might expect.

Stars come in different colours because they have different surface temperatures. Relatively cool stars glow reddish-orange. Hotter stars appear white or bluish-white, with the hottest giving off a distinctly blue tint.

You might reasonably expect stars with intermediate temperatures to appear green.

In fact, some stars emit their greatest amount of visible light at green wavelengths. Our own Sun is a good example. Its spectrum peaks around the green part of the visible spectrum, depending on how the light is measured.

But nobody looking at the Sun from space would really describe it as green. It’s essentially white. The explanation lies in the difference between emitting green light and looking green.

Stars don’t emit just one colour. They produce a continuous spectrum of light containing many wavelengths, much like the colours of a rainbow. The proportions change with temperature, but the different colours are always mixed together.

When a star’s spectrum peaks in green, it still produces plenty of red and blue light. Our eyes combine those wavelengths and perceive the result as white. This is why you can find stars that look reddish, orange, white, or bluish, but not the vivid green of a traffic light or a laser pointer.

Please check your inbox and confirm your subscription.

Astronomers describe this behaviour using something called blackbody radiation. It’s a mathematical model that predicts how an object radiates light based on its temperature

But it turns out that one unusual class of dead stars can break the pattern.

WD J1330+6435 is a white dwarf, the dense stellar remnant left behind after a star exhausts its nuclear fuel and sheds its outer layers. Our own Sun will also eventually become one.

White dwarfs are extraordinary objects. They typically pack a mass comparable to the Sun’s into a sphere roughly the size of Earth. Their gravity is immense, and although they no longer sustain the nuclear fusion that powers ordinary stars, they remain extremely hot for billions of years as they slowly cool.

WD J1330+6435 has an estimated surface temperature of around 4,240 Kelvin, or about 3,970°C. That would ordinarily make it look reddish-orange.

But it also has an atmosphere; and that atmosphere is really unusual.

Astronomers had already studied the star because its spectrum contained unusual amounts of lithium, sodium, and calcium. These elements are clues that the white dwarf has accumulated rocky material from bodies that once orbited it. That material could have come from shattered asteroids or other remnants of its former planetary system.

The new research suggests that this chemical pollution is also responsible for its green appearance.

Basically, the star looks green because its atmosphere acts a bit like a filter.

White dwarfs can have extremely dense atmospheres. In some of the coolest ones, the gas is packed so tightly that atoms constantly bump into each other.

This changes the way the star’s atmosphere absorbs light. In WD J1330+6435, sodium absorbs a broad swath of yellow and orange light, while calcium absorbs some light at the violet end of the spectrum.

With those colours partly removed, more of the remaining light falls in the green range. As a result, the star would appear a pale yellowish-green, often described as chartreuse. Chartreuse is also a French herbal liqueur that has been made by Carthusian monks since the 18th century.

“The traces of sodium in the atmosphere of this white dwarf absorb the orange part of the spectrum, leaving behind a ghostly green glow,” explained lead author Mark Hollands of the University of Warwick in a university statement.

No astronomer has directly photographed WD J1330+6435 as a resolved green disc. The object is extremely faint and far too distant for us to see its surface directly.

Researchers have only reconstructed what the star would look like if a human observer could approach it closely enough. But they’re still pretty confident it’s green. I mean, chartreuse.

They started with stellar spectra, measurements that reveal how much light an object emits at different wavelengths. A spectrum is essentially a detailed breakdown of starlight. By examining which wavelengths are missing or diminished, astronomers can identify chemical elements in a star’s atmosphere and investigate the conditions there.

The researchers used atmospheric models to reproduce these observations. They then passed the resulting spectra through mathematical models of human colour vision, calculating how the light would appear to our eyes.

They even generated realistic-looking images of the stars, accounting for the way brightness and colour change toward the edges of a stellar disc.

There are uncertainties. The star’s measured spectrum is somewhat noisy, so its exact shade could differ from the model. However, the team is confident that the star really would have a green appearance.

And WD J1330+6435 isn’t entirely alone. The researchers identified two other, much paler greenish white dwarfs. One of them, WD J0851+1543, has a higher-quality spectrum that supports its unusual colour.

The researchers also identified white dwarfs that would appear pink, purple, yellow, or cyan, their unusual colours shaped by the chemistry of their atmospheres. Such stars are rare: just five of the 152 nearby white dwarfs studied had markedly unusual colours.

These exceptions reveal something remarkable. A star’s colour can tell us not just how hot it is, but also what it’s made of and, sometimes, what happened to its planets. In the case of WD J1330+6435, that strange greenish glow may carry the chemical traces of worlds destroyed long ago.

For decades, astronomers had good reasons to believe green stars couldn’t exist. It turns out the universe found a loophole.

The study, “Any colour you like: The diverse chromaticities of white dwarfs,” was published on 15 October 2026 in RAS Techniques and Instruments.

#4131. Scam alert: The government is not deleting your phone number

📅 22 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Published Oct 22, 2026 4:15 PM EDT

The scams are getting a bit meta. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) has issued a warning about a new scam currently making the rounds. But this time, the con artists aren’t impersonating a politician or celebrity to trick consumers.They’re claiming that they are the ones sounding the alarm at the FTC.

Real FTC officials explained the general scheme in an alert released on October 22. Scammers call a target to tell them that bad actors in another country have registered a separate phone using their name and number for nefarious purposes. The imposter may even “transfer” you to a fake police officer to file a report. Fake badge numbers and IDs are also offered to make it all sound legitimate, and skeptics may even be directed to verify everything by searching for the police department’s phone number online (also fake). Failure to comply allegedly results in the loss of your personal phone number.

Those who follow through with the instructions provide scammers with their state-issued ID and other personal info for the bogus police report, but it’s not over yet. Some time later, targets will receive another call informing them that they will soon be arrested for those nonexistent crimes committed using their phone number. The solution? Transfer money for a security deposit.

“None of this is true—it’s all a scam,” assured the FTC.

To prevent unnecessary headaches, the FTC listed a few ironclad rules. Actual FTC employees will never contact you about sending money, or transfer you to someone else requesting funds. The FTC will never threaten to disconnect your phone number, or ask you to keep your conversations secret from others. Finally, they will never text a photo of the supposed employee ID as a way to verify their identity to you.

Unfortunately, combatting scammers is a bit like playing a neverending game of Whack-a-Mole. While the FTC has a list of common ways to spot government imposters, they encourage everyone to report the latest con jobs to ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

#3599. Brazil’s Workers’ Party Is Not Stopping

With just a few days remaining before the runoff election, supporters of Lula’s party are doing everything possible to rally support behind the president.

Supporters of Brazil’s president and presidential candidate Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva of the Workers’ Party attend a campaign event in São Paulo, Brazil, on October 11, 2026, ahead of the presidential runoff election.

Rio de Janeiro—On Tuesday, October 6, at roughly 5 pm, people wearing red shirts, hats, and stickers started filling the Rio de Janeiro Bankers’ Union office in the center of the city. Still shaken by the results of the first round of Brazil’s elections that previous Sunday, Workers’ Party voters had been called to a meeting by newly elected congressman Marcelo Freixo to strategize the next steps of a national campaign to turn the elections around.

That Sunday, October 4, millions of Brazilians voted to elect seats in the chamber of deputies and the Senate, as well as state deputies, governors, and the president. Democratic socialist President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, a member of the Workers’ Party, was facing far-right candidate Flávio Bolsonaro, the son of Jair Bolsonaro, who is currently in jail for an attempted coup d’état in 2022. Contrary to the polls leading up to election day, which gave Lula a lead over the far-right candidate, Bolsonaro captured 1.87 percent of valid votes more than Lula, with 47 percent of total votes against Lula’s 45 percent. Since neither candidate reached over 50 percent of the vote, the race heads to a runoff on October 25. But even if Lula were to win this coming Sunday, Bolsonaro’s party, the Partido Liberal, swept the Senate, winning 28 of 54 available seats, giving the far right a majority in the legislative chamber on top of its control of the lower house.

A Bolsonaro win in the runoff would give unprecedented power to the far right in two of the three top branches of Brazil’s government. And in the 48 hours following Bolsonaro’s advantage in the first round, he announced plans to modify the country’s 1988 Constitution to “redemocratize the country,” proposing to abolish reelections and reform the Supreme Court, the last independent branch of government. The Supreme Court has been key in maintaining Brazilian democracy. But experts warn that a far-right majority in the Senate could be used to impeach and humiliate ministers who voted to convict Jair Bolsonaro. “It’s the end of the Lula era and we will be in power for decades,” Flávio said in a campaign event following the race.

The Lula era began in 2003, when he became president on a democratic socialist platform. He was elected again in 2006, with an approval rating of 52 percent. He won his third election in 2022, ousting far-right incumbent president Jair Bolsonaro. Lula’s governments were marked by a decline in poverty through welfare policies, expansion of investment in higher education, increased access to water and basic sanitation, the delivery of hundreds of thousands of public housing units, and the expansion of workers’ rights.

After the first round of voting, Lula and the Workers’ Party accused Meta of meddling with the 2026 elections through disinformation campaigns and the suppression of left-wing content across Meta’s social-media platforms. Hundreds protested outside of Meta’s office in São Paulo on Monday, and the company announced on Tuesday that is complying with an order from the electoral court, triggered by the Lula campaign’s accusation, “to give equal treatment in its platforms” to both candidates ahead of the runoff, reported the Associated Press.

As Lula campaign supporters race to activate voters ahead of Sunday’s runoff, legal experts remain concerned about Flávio’s intentions to change the Constitution. For Wallace Corbo, professor of constitutional law at the State University of Rio de Janeiro and the Getúlio Vargas Foundation, this proposal should be understood as a dog whistle confirming the likelihood of a return to far-right authoritarianism. “Substituting the 1988 Constitution in a context of the rise of authoritarian governments, with very low popular participation, is very problematic in terms of legitimacy, democracy, and rights for citizens and workers,” Corbo told The Nation, referencing the record number of people—33.4 million people, or 21 percent of the electorate—who did not vote in the October 4 elections. This low participation contrasts sharply with the support for the 1988 Constitution, which involved an overwhelming number of participants from the different historically marginalized groups, including Indigenous and Black people and women, becoming “the most democratic and participatory Constitution in our history,” after 21 years of a far-right military dictatorship supported by the United States, Corbo said, adding, “There’s a danger of its being effectively abolished or significantly disfigured, with the rollback of certain rights for the population.”

Abortion is largely banned in Brazil but allowed in the cases of rape, life endangerment, or when the fetus has been diagnosed with anencephaly—exceptions that have been protected by Supreme Court rulings. In the Senate, the risk is a conservative majority passing even more draconian anti-abortion legislation. “If we have a chamber of deputies, a Senate, and a president that align with Bolsonarism, we lose all the checks and balances that could, for example, lead to a veto or the overturning of a presidential veto,” added Boldrini.

With the far right running the Senate and Bolsonarista governors across Brazil acting in support of deforestation, the addition of a Flávio presidency would be devastating.

#4137. You could live in a converted Cold War missile silo for just $1.6 million

📅 22 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Published Oct 22, 2026 3:10 PM EDT

When it comes to buying a home, many people pay a premium for privacy. But one new listing Carlton, Kansas, takes the cake. For a measly $1.6 million, someone can call a converted Cold War Atlas intercontinental ballistic missile (ICBM) silo home. Though the site sits on 18.2 acres of rural grassland, nearly the entire complex is hidden underground, save a single ominous steel door. The silo trenches down 182 feet, eventually leading to a two floor launch control center retrofitted to fit the modern world.

Surrounding the entire complex are 600 tons of steel rebar and six-foot-thick epoxy concrete walls meant to withstand nuclear blasts and 500 mph winds. The listing’s website calls it quite possibly “one of the most hardened structures ever built by man.” Once the staging ground for nuclear Armageddon, the indoor launch control center has been converted into a 2,200-square-foot, two bedroom, two bath, home outfitted with generic modern appliances. To top it all off, it is caked in a fresh coat of muted millennial gray and white paint.

This particular missile silo was part of the United States Air Force’s Atlas F Intercontinental Ballistic Missile Program. The Atlas program began toward the end of World War II, and produced the country’s first operational ICBM. At its peak, from late 1962 to mid-1964, 132 of these Atlas sites were built  around the country.

According to the National Park Service, north-central  Kansas’ Atlas F was “by far the best” of the Atlas variants ever deployed. The missile stood 82 feet tall and carried a 4.5 megaton warhead, roughly 300 times as powerful as the 15-kiloton bomb dropped on Hiroshima. Aside from the Kansas location, there were 72 Atlas missile sites stationed on bases in Nebraska, Oklahoma, Texas, New Mexico, and New York.

Though advanced for its time, the Atlas was soon eclipsed by the even stronger Titan and then Minuteman systems.The majority of the Atlas systems were decommissioned in 1979, when President Richard Nixon signed the SALT (The Strategic Arms Limitation Talks) treaties. That meant dozens of the massive structures were left dormant. Many of those relics have taken on new lives as luxury apocalypse bunkers or gimmicky rentals.

A buyer interested in viewing the North Star Missile Silo in Kansas would really have to know what they’re looking for. Getting there requires driving through a seemingly nondescript stretch of grassland before eventually coming up on a large gated fence with the iconic yellow “Fallout Shelter” signage on its side. Once inside, a surreal-looking steel door jutting out of the quiet countryside offers a clue to the shelter. It looks like a portal to some eerie, distant world, which, in some sense, it really is.

Once through the door, visitors descend a metal staircase that leads to a 40-foot tunnel. Photos show a narrow, claustrophobic walkway encased in a ring of white metal beams. At the end of the walkway stands a pair of imposing 7,000-pound blast doors. Home sweet home!

But the weirdest part about the retrofitted missile silo house is how remarkably normal much of it looks on the inside. The living quarters feature a floor plan with two bedrooms, two kitchens, and two bathrooms spread across two floors. Stainless steel appliances and a washer and dryer sit alongside standard wooden cabinets, none of which would look out of place in any hastily built rental unit across the country. The central area is laid out loft-style, showing off the control center’s massive support column. Visually, everything seems out of context. It evokes a slightly off not quite right AI-generated version of a house, or a modern millennial apartment that a visiting alien might describe to fellow interstellar travelers.

It’s an undeniably weird place to live full time, but not a half-bad survival bunker.  The listing notes that it features two separate sewage systems (one above ground and one underground) humidity control, a cellphone phone signal repeater and a solar power powered seven foot chain link gate. It also has ample room for solar or wind energy installation, though the buyer would have to install those. It’s also not necessarily limited just to survival or end time doomers. Realtor.com notes that the silo was previously used as a rental, though he also said there was interest in using it as an off-the grid conference site or even a wedding venue.

Many Cold War relics have taken on new lives as quirky rental and luxury condominiums. Airbnb shows a number of silos available for short term stays in New Mexico, Kansas, and southern Texas. Some of these explicitly court “prepper” audiences with fat wallets. Possibly the best known of those is Survival Condo, located about 200 miles outside of Kansas City, That sprawling Atlas Missile Silo was converted into a 15 floor luxury condominium outfitted with a pet park, climbing walls, swimming pool (with a slide) library and a movie theater. The starting asking price for one of the units? Around $1 million in 2020 dollars.

And while Cold War bunkers are relics of the past, new interpretations of the structures are still being built. In the U.S. and abroad (New Zealand attracts a particular amount of attention) a subset of the world’s wealthiest elites are reportedly constructing their own apocalyptic lairs. Some of the best known examples of those billionaire bunker builders are in the tech industry with some racing to build advanced AI models that are increasingly putting them at odds with public opinion. Aside from escaping the pitchforks, the Kansas listing also notes one more potentially enticing incentive.

“Underground structures not taxable by [the] County.”
