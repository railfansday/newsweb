#4214. NASA, Partners Accelerate Lander Development for 2028 Moon Missions

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

As NASA returns humans to the lunar surface and establishes a long-term presence on our closest celestial neighbor, the agency and commercial human landing system providers Blue Origin and SpaceX have streamlined the designs and changed both concepts of operations for its future crewed landers.

Since NASA established initial contracts with Blue Origin and SpaceX for crewed missions to the Moon, the agency has redefined Artemis III to demonstrate rendezvous and docking capabilities between test versions of landers from each provider and the agency’s Orion spacecraft in low Earth orbit next year. In parallel, the companies are also developing crew and cargo versions of their landers for near-term Artemis missions.

“We are executing President Trump’s National Space Policy to complete the long-awaited return of American astronauts to the lunar surface in 2028 and establish a Moon Base,” said NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman. “We have a proven foundation in SLS and Orion, along with two lunar landers in development with Blue Origin and SpaceX. But NASA has no intention of sitting idly by as all the pieces come together. That is not how Apollo succeeded, and it is not how the near-impossible is achieved. NASA is energized, our core capabilities are being rebuilt, our machine shops are running 24/7, and our experts are in the field attacking the critical path. We are building a Super Bowl-caliber team to safely achieve these objectives and everything that comes next.”

For the initial crewed missions to the Moon, Artemis astronauts will launch in the agency’s Orion spacecraft on top of the SLS (Space Launch System) rocket from NASA’s Kennedy Space Center in Florida. During each Artemis mission, a commercial human landings system will link up with Orion before taking two crew members to the lunar surface while two remain aboard Orion. To accelerate development toward a 2028 landing, the Artemis landers and Orion will no longer rendezvous in a near-rectilinear halo orbit around the Moon and instead meet up in an orbit that differs for each partner, informed by their systems.

Both companies are required to perform an uncrewed demonstration mission with their crewed landers prior to the near-term Artemis crewed missions to the Moon. NASA will certify lander systems prior to the crewed missions to ensure safety and mission success.

SpaceX continues to plan for an in-space propellant aggregation approach to transfer super cold, or cryogenic, liquid propellant in space. The company will launch multiple versions of Starship to low Earth orbit prior to the launch of the Starship Human Landing System (HLS) from Florida. Similar to a gas station on Earth, the series of propellant “tanker” Starships in low Earth orbit will transfer propellant to the waiting “depot” Starship. The depot Starship will ultimately fuel the Starship lander for its journey to the Moon.

Under the revised operations, Starship HLS will await Orion and its crew in Earth orbit. The lander and Orion will rendezvous and dock in Earth orbit, then the Starship lander will perform a trans-lunar injection burn to send the combined lander and Orion stack to low lunar orbit. NASA is working to modify Orion to enable Starship to push it to the Moon. The lander will bring two crew members to the lunar surface for several days before delivering them back to Orion for the ride back to Earth.

Propellant aggregation in low Earth orbit allows for flexibility in the crew launch timing on SLS, while a trans-lunar injection burn performed by the Starship lander will enable direct access to low lunar orbit. This new concept of operations using low lunar orbit will significantly lower the amount of propellant Starship needs to aggregate in space while reducing the amount of time it can take for astronauts leaving the Moon’s surface in Starship to link back up with Orion. SpaceX will demonstrate the propellant aggregation approach during the uncrewed demonstration mission.

For its accelerated 2028 crewed lunar landing mission, Blue Origin will defer propellant transfer and use three transfer stages to send the Blue Moon Mark 2 Alpha Lander to the Moon. Based on existing Mark 1 and Mark 2 hardware, the stages operate like a multistage rocket. They launch into low Earth orbit, followed by the Mark 2 lander. Once docked, the stack departs for an elliptical polar orbit around the Moon, with each transfer stage separating as its propellant depletes. Compared with the previously planned near-rectilinear halo orbit, the elliptical polar orbit offers faster returns from the lunar surface to Orion, reduces Blue Moon lander propellant requirements, and maximizes the performance available by SLS.

This architecture provides multiple opportunities for SLS and Orion to launch the crew to elliptical polar orbit and dock with the Blue Moon Mark 2 lander. Two crew members then transfer to the lander and descend to the Moon’s South Pole. After surface operations, the lander returns to Orion, where the crew transfers for their journey back to Earth.

Blue Origin will incorporate lessons from earlier robotic Mark 1 missions supporting the agency’s Moon Base Program and demonstrate the full mission profile in an uncrewed lander transfer stage demonstration mission.

Ultimately, based on lander readiness, NASA will determine which company will be first selected to safely deliver astronauts to the Moon’s surface and back to lunar orbit in 2028. A second crewed landing opportunity will follow.

NASA is targeting two, approximately two-hour long lunar surface Moonwalks during initial surface missions. Astronauts will use the Moonwalks to collection lunar samples and test capabilities of the spacesuit provided by Axiom Space.

Earlier this year, NASA updated its contracts with Blue Origin and SpaceX for human landing systems and large cargo deliveries to the Moon. The updates align the agency’s human landing system requirements with acceleration efforts announced earlier this year to streamline Artemis mission architecture, expedite a lunar return, and support an increased mission cadence to the Moon.

Under the original HLS contracts, SpaceX’s Starship human landing system was assigned to the Artemis III and IV missions, while Blue Origin would use its Blue Moon Mark 2 human landing system for Artemis V. Under the contract realignments, both providers are participating in the Artemis III mission in low Earth orbit and neither lander provider has been assigned to a specific Artemis lunar landing mission.

“NASA and our lunar lander providers Blue Origin and SpaceX have been working hand in hand to ensure we can return American astronauts to the Moon by 2028,” said Jeremy Parsons, NASA’s Artemis program manager.“While work to realign with our partners in support of architecture updates announced at Ignition Day in March has been underway since then, updating the contracts ensures NASA and its partners are on the same page with requirements and other milestones to ensure continued insight into lander development to support mission success and the safety of our Moon crews.”

Both Blue Origin and SpaceX have added development milestones to provide NASA with more insight into lander development efforts, reducing risk and enhancing astronaut safety in preparation for crewed Moon landings. SpaceX is adding two demonstrations of its crew cabin, called Cabin 1, for its Starship lander. Blue Origin is adding new human-in-the-loop testing and hardware readiness milestones to streamline training development as well as ground and flight operations.

As part of a Golden Age of innovation and exploration, NASA will send Artemis astronauts on increasingly difficult missions near the lunar South Pole to explore more of the Moon than ever before. In addition to providing economic benefits and advancing scientific discovery though its Moon missions, NASA will establish an enduring human presence on the lunar surface and build on the agency’s foundation for the first crewed mission to Mars.

#4220. NASA’s Artemis III Mission to Test Future Moon Lander Operations

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Before astronauts return to the lunar surface, NASA will first conduct one of the most ambitious and complex test missions in the agency’s history.

Launching in 2027, Artemis III will send NASA astronauts Randy Bresnik, Frank Rubio, Andre Douglas, and ESA (European Space Agency) astronaut Luca Parmitano aboard the Orion spacecraft into low Earth orbit to perform a series of demonstrations with test versions, or test articles, of human landing systems being developed by industry partners Blue Origin and SpaceX. During the planned 14-day mission, crews and flight controllers will practice the rendezvous, docking, habitation, and operational procedures that will be essential when Artemis astronauts begin landing near the Moon’s South Pole in 2028. The mission is designed to reduce risk, validate hardware, and gain operational experience before NASA attempts a crewed lunar landing.

“Every piece of this mission in Earth orbit is designed to reduce risk before we put astronauts back on the Moon,” said Jeremy Parsons, NASA’s Artemis program manager. “We’re launching three spacecraft on three different rockets and testing how they operate together — something NASA has never attempted. Every lesson we learn here gets us closer to landing on the Moon in 2028.”

First stop: Blue Origin’s lunar lander test article

Earlier Artemis missions used only NASA’s SLS (Space Launch System) rocket and Orion spacecraft. Artemis III will involve three separate spacecraft launched on three different heavy-lift rockets within a relatively short period of time.

The mission begins with Blue Origin launching a test version of its Blue Moon Mark 2 lander into a circular low Earth orbit approximately 273 miles (237 nautical miles) above Earth. The spacecraft can remain on orbit for up to 30 days providing schedule flexibility. Artemis III is designed as a tightly choreographed campaign, with NASA planning to launch Orion on top of SLS from the agency’s Kennedy Space Center in Florida only days after the Blue Moon test article launch and confirmation the vehicle is healthy and ready for joint operations.

Following launch, Orion will initially enter an elliptical Earth orbit of 18 x 1122 miles (16 x 975 nautical miles). It will use its service module to perform a series of maneuvers to transition to a circular orbit at about 345 miles (300 nautical miles) for thermal stabilization before then gradually setting up its rendezvous with the waiting Blue Moon lander test article. Because each spacecraft operates in slightly different orbital regimes optimized for its mission requirements, Artemis III requires a carefully choreographed sequence of orbital maneuvers and rendezvous operations throughout the flight.

During the first two days of flight, the crew will check out Orion’s systems, prepare for proximity operations, and gradually position themselves for the mission’s first major objective: rendezvous and docking with the Blue Moon test vehicle.

The operation represents NASA’s first opportunity to evaluate how Orion and a commercial lunar lander work together in space. Engineers will assess how software aboard both spacecraft, guidance systems, communications systems, and operational procedures perform in real-world conditions, while flight controllers gain experience coordinating activities across multiple teams and organizations.

Once docked, astronauts will spend approximately two days conducting evaluations aboard the Blue Moon test article. Crew members will open the hatch and enter the spacecraft, allowing them to assess the cabin environment, controls, and life-support systems firsthand. Teams will verify the spacecraft’s ability to maintain the proper atmosphere and pressure.

The Blue Moon test article incorporates many of the avionics, software, flight-control systems, crew-cabin technologies, and life-support functions planned for future lunar missions. Testing those systems in orbit will provide the first opportunity to evaluate how many of the lander’s flight systems perform in space as an integrated vehicle.

By the time Orion undocks, NASA will have completed its first evaluation of a commercial lunar lander with crew, gathering data on spacecraft systems, crew operations, and spacecraft interoperability not possible on the ground.

After completing operations with Blue Origin’s lander, Orion will undock and reposition to support the next phase of the mission: a second rendezvous operation.

SpaceX will launch a Starship Version 3 test article from Florida. Because Starship is designed for a much shorter on-orbit lifetime than Blue Origin’s lander, launch occurs later in the mission, allowing NASA to efficiently sequence the two docking demonstrations. Starship will enter a lower orbit at 206 nautical miles, optimized for communications coverage, and will carry an added docking system mounted at the forward end.

Orion will rendezvous and dock with the spacecraft during a test expected to last up to two hours. Crew will not enter Starship during this test. The demonstration is focused on validating how Orion and Starship interact as a docked system in space. NASA and SpaceX plan to evaluate spacecraft controllability, communications, integrated software performance, and the dynamics associated with docking and undocking operations.

The docking itself also presents a unique test, using different docking geometries. Orion will connect to a port beside Blue Moon’s crew cabin, then later align its forward docking system with the one at Starship’s nose. Testing both approaches during a single mission gives NASA an opportunity to assess different operational concepts while gathering valuable engineering data.

Together, the two docking campaigns will demonstrate how Orion interfaces with two very different commercial lander architectures during a single mission.

Even after docking activities are complete, Artemis III will continue generating data that supports future missions.

Once the Starship demonstration is complete, Orion and its crew will have up to five additional days available in orbit before beginning preparations for the journey home. NASA plans to use this time to complete a variety of test objectives that support future Artemis missions, including additional evaluations of the Orion Crew Survival System suits, docking sensor tests, acoustic and thermal measurements, human-factors assessments, and a range of science and technology demonstrations.

Several investigations led by NASA will build on studies conducted during Artemis II, collecting additional data that will help inform future deep-space missions. The crew also will conduct a proof-of-concept study examining how astronauts could perform health-related assessments when they are unable to communicate directly with mission control, an important capability for future lunar surface expeditions and other missions farther from Earth.

NASA also plans to fly a cellular biology experiment during the mission while researchers use the flight to test concepts for returning lunar samples to Earth. Teams will evaluate how future samples could be transported, received, and moved into curation facilities after landing, while also conducting assessments of Orion’s environment to better understand potential contamination considerations for future lunar missions.

The mission also will include Earth and solar observations that help astronauts continue developing critical observation skills while taking advantage of the spacecraft’s unique orbital vantage point. Plant seeds will fly aboard Orion as well, providing opportunities for scientific study and educational engagement following the mission.

Mission planners have also built flexibility into the flight timeline, providing additional opportunities to complete test objectives depending on how operations unfold in real time.

When ready, Orion will begin phasing for return, and the crew will configure the cabin for reentry and splashdown, which will occur in the Pacific Ocean, off the coast of California. Orion and its crew will be recovered by NASA personnel and the United States Navy.

Every activity performed during Artemis III supports a larger goal: reducing risk before astronauts return to the lunar surface.

The mission will provide NASA with experience operating alongside multiple commercial partners while managing complex rendezvous and docking operations involving three different spacecraft. Engineers will gain insight into spacecraft interoperability, mission-control coordination, communications architecture, crew operations, and hardware performance.

By the time Artemis III concludes with Orion’s splashdown on Earth, NASA will have demonstrated technologies and operational concepts that future lunar crews will rely on as they travel to and from the Moon.

Most importantly, Artemis III will provide the operational experience needed to transform plans for future lunar landings into proven flight procedures. The lessons learned throughout the mission will help refine future exploration plans, strengthen partnerships with industry, and ensure astronauts are prepared for humanity’s next giant leap at the Moon.

In addition to providing economic benefits and advancing scientific discovery through its Moon missions, NASA will establish an enduring human presence on the lunar surface and build on the agency’s foundation for the first crewed mission to Mars.

#4164. Could GTA VI Sick Leave Really Cost the US Economy Over $1 Billion?

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Millions are rearranging their work days for one video game.

November 19 sounds like pretty much any other day. But on November 19, something unusual might happen to America’s workforce. Offices could be emptier, sick days could mysteriously multiply, and some employees working from home might find themselves considerably more interested in stealing virtual cars than answering emails.

There’s a new bug going around. The GTA bug.

We’re talking, of course, about Grand Theft Auto VI, a video game so anticipated that some estimates suggest 1.5 million Americans could skip work to play it, potentially costing the US economy $1 billion in a single day.

It sounds ridiculous. But after 13 years of waiting for a new mainline Grand Theft Auto game, fans are making vacation plans around its release.

For anyone who hasn’t played Grand Theft Auto, the premise is straightforward: imagine being dropped into an enormous, detailed version of an American city and given permission to cause almost unlimited chaos. You can steal cars, pull off elaborate heists, flee the police, or simply drive around listening to the radio. The games are part crime drama, part satire of American culture, and part digital playground.

The upcoming GTA VI takes players back to Vice City, Rockstar’s fictional version of Miami, and the surrounding state of Leonida, inspired by Florida. The story follows two criminals, Jason and Lucia, caught up in a conspiracy.

It’s difficult to overstate the anticipation. The previous installment, GTA V, came out in September 2013, when the PlayStation 4 hadn’t even launched. According to publisher Take-Two Interactive, the game generated more than $1 billion in worldwide sales in just three days. Now, after multiple delays, Rockstar has scheduled GTA VI for Thursday, November 19, 2026.

And Thursday, unfortunately for employers, is usually a working day.

A September report from Money found that requests for paid time off on November 19 were already running 9% to 17% above historical averages for the equivalent Thursday, according to data provided by human resources platform BambooHR. Of course, it’s hard to prove GTA is to blame, but the timing is suspicious.

One California automotive parts company, Burger Motorsports, even announced a tongue-in-cheek operational pause around the launch, warning customers that employees might be unavailable because they’re busy exploring Vice City. Take-Two CEO Strauss Zelnick also half jokingly predicted that plenty of people would call in sick on November 19.

The latest survey suggests he might have a point.

On October 20, gaming and media company GameSquare released research involving 1,000 full-time employed American gamers.

The results were striking. Ten percent said they planned to skip work on launch day, while another 30% were considering taking time off. Among those considering it, 36% said they might call in sick. Meanwhile, 42% expected the game’s arrival could make them less productive at work.

Of course, these weren’t percentages of all American workers. They came from a sample of people who already play video games, surveyed by a company with a commercial interest in gaming culture. And saying you’ll take a day off is not the same as actually doing it.

But a lot of things are saying November 19 won’t exactly be the most productive day in American history.

The claim appears in a July 2025 report by Spanish broadcaster Cadena SER, which discussed the game’s potential economic consequences with José García Montalvo, an economics professor at Pompeu Fabra University in Barcelona.

The broadcaster suggested that absenteeism could cost the American economy around $1 billion on the game’s first day. Montalvo discussed why younger workers, particularly men aged 18 to 30, and people working remotely in technology might be especially tempted to stay home or spend less time working.

As it so often happens, the claim got spread massively. But it’s not exactly a careful calculation; it’s more like a specialist’s estimate.

One widely circulated explanation assumes 1.5 million absent workers, each producing about $70,000 of economic output annually on average.

But divide that $70,000 across approximately 250 working days, and each worker produces about $280 a day. Multiply that by 1.5 million people, and you get roughly $420 million, not $1 billion.

So, it’s reasonable to expect significant losses on account of GTA being launched, but it’s hard to come up with an accurate prediction.

But this is where the economics gets even more interesting.

According to the Entertainment Software Association’s 2026 report, approximately 212 million Americans play video games at least an hour a week. The average player is 37 years old. And video games really can change how people decide to spend their time.

In a study published in the Journal of Political Economy, economists Mark Aguiar, Mark Bils, Kerwin Kofi Charles and Erik Hurst examined changes in leisure activities and working hours among young American men.

Using time-use surveys and economic modelling, they investigated a simple question: what happens when entertainment becomes significantly more appealing?

Their findings suggested that improvements in recreational computing, including video gaming, had reduced working hours among men aged 21 to 30 by around 2% over the period examined.

But that figure is often misunderstood or misquoted

The researchers weren’t saying that a new game (say, GTA) would cut national productivity by 2%. They were studying longer-term changes in how a particular group of people divided their time between paid work and leisure.

When entertainment becomes better, free time becomes more valuable. A person might prefer an extra evening gaming with friends over working additional hours, just as someone else might choose more time with family or a shorter working week.

GTA VI is so big and so intensely expected that it adds the sense of a shared cultural moment. GTA is a cultural phenomenon. And there’s something faintly absurd about describing a day spent doing something enjoyable and cultural as a national economic catastrophe.

GDP, the standard measure of economic output, tells us how much an economy produces. It doesn’t tell us whether people enjoyed producing it, or whether they would have preferred to do something else.

The OECD explicitly recognises that GDP doesn’t capture everything that matters to people’s lives, including work-life balance and wellbeing. I’m not saying productivity is irrelevant or that employers shouldn’t care about staff shortages. It’s just that the economic value of work and the personal value of free time are different things.

There are plenty of perfectly ordinary reasons to take a vacation day: attend a wedding, visit family, travel somewhere new, or simply rest. Spending the day playing a video game is just another choice, and one that will likely make many people happy.

Perhaps the most remarkable thing about GTA VI isn’t how much money it might cost employers. It’s that it’s convinced millions of people how they’d want to spend their Thursday.

#4227. NASA’s Swift Mission Ends, Leaves Behind Scientific Bonanza

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

NASA’s Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory has ended its science mission now that the spacecraft is orbiting less than 171 miles (275 kilometers) above Earth’s surface. The greater atmospheric drag at this height prevents the satellite’s telescopes from steadily gazing at their astronomical targets.

Swift conducted its final observation on Oct. 20. It is expected to re-enter the atmosphere in the next few weeks.

“Swift delivered an incredible, lasting return on investment for the nation, designed for a two-year primary mission but productive for more than 10 times as long,” said Shawn Domagal-Goldman, director, Astrophysics Division at NASA Headquarters in Washington. “In that time, Swift played a major role in advancing our understanding of the changing universe and was part of a revolution in astrophysics that showed how critical rapid-response observations are to the entire community. To support rapid response to cosmic events in a post-Swift world, we’re exploring our ability to use and augment NASA’s fleet of existing space telescopes, from fast-to-orbit commercial capabilities to efficient, innovative new missions. And Swift leaves us with an extraordinary archive of multiwavelength observations that will fuel new discoveries for decades more.”

In its storied career, Swift has observed more than 2,000 gamma-ray bursts, the most powerful explosions in the cosmos. It has studied more than 1,400 supernova explosions, witnessed stars shredded by black holes, monitored active galaxies, conducted an all-sky X-ray survey, studied asteroids and comets, and more. Swift data has contributed to about 7,000 published scientific papers.

“Swift was designed as a satellite dedicated to studying gamma-ray bursts, outbursts that came and went so quickly that no one could target them with large telescopes to learn more,” said S. Bradley Cenko, the mission’s principal investigator at NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center in Greenbelt, Maryland. “Swift needed to detect and locate gamma-ray bursts, communicate their position to other observatories, and then swing its telescopes around to image the burst locations before their afterglows faded out. This typically took Swift about a minute to do.”

Led by its first principal investigator, Neil Gehrels, Swift was born as a multiwavelength observatory, with co-aligned Optical/Ultraviolet and X-ray telescopes that could quickly image and refine a burst’s position. The mission launched into orbit on Nov. 20, 2004, and went to work the following month. In 2018, NASA renamed the mission to honor Gehrels following his death.

Speed was especially important for an elusive population called short gamma-ray bursts, which reach peak brightness in less than two seconds. Until Swift, it had been impossible to locate these bursts rapidly and precisely enough to observe their afterglows. In May 2005, Swift achieved this milestone with GRB 050509B, which lasted just 0.03 seconds. Swift turned to the burst fast enough to detect 11 X-ray photons, making this the first short burst with a detected afterglow. These observations validated long-standing theoretical models, showing that short gamma-ray bursts likely come from mergers of two neutron stars — objects with the mass of the Sun that have been crushed to the size of a city.

Another factor led to Swift’s success as a kind of astronomical multitool. Its flexible planning system enabled astronomers to request "target-of-opportunity" observations that could be commanded from the ground in as little as a few minutes, or to set up monitoring programs for observing specific sources lasting from as little as a few minutes to months. The system could schedule up to 75 independent targets a day.

"The unique speed of response of the Swift hardware, the extraordinary success of the rapid mission operations, and the closely knit teamwork of the Swift Flight and Science Operations teams placed Swift at the crossroads of burgeoning areas of research," said John Nousek, the mission’s operations director and professor of astronomy and astrophysics in the Eberly College of Science at Penn State in University Park, Pennsylvania.

The Swift archive contains nearly 22 years of brightness measurements of all kinds of cosmic outbursts, multiwavelength spectra, more than 1.5 million images from its Ultraviolet/Optical Telescope, and much more. The full dataset, publicly available as part of NASA’s commitment to open, gold standard science, spans approximately 12 terabytes, the storage equivalent of about 600 high-definition movies.

As part of the agency’s previous planning for Swift’s end of mission, NASA will continue to prioritize finding new options to react rapidly to cosmic events, using current missions to help fill the gap in the meantime.

#4173. Scientists Identify a New Type of Depression That May Feel Like Permanent Jet Lag

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

A new study suggests nearly one in four depression patients may experience chronic body clock disruption.

For some people with depression, every day feels like waking up with jet lag but without the flight. They sleep longer, wake exhausted, and find themselves most alert when the rest of the world is winding down. Their problem may not simply be how much they sleep or how poorly, but when their internal clock tells them to.

In an analysis of 11,380 Australians with a history of depression, researchers identified 2,604 people, or 23 percent, whose sleep habits and symptoms fit a proposed new subtype of depression called circadian depression. The patients developed depression younger, experienced more severe symptoms, and found common antidepressants less helpful.

In other words, a large portion of the population may experience depression due to their body’s internal clock, which perhaps is not in sync with the tune of modern life, rather than due to anxiety.

Our internal timekeeping systems, or circadian clocks, respond to light and help regulate sleep, appetite, alertness and mood.

“When it’s off, you feel jet-lagged all the time,” Ian Hickie, a University of Sydney psychiatrist and senior author of the study, told ScienceAlert.

Depression can look strikingly different from one patient to another. Some cannot sleep; others sleep excessively. Some lose their appetite; others gain weight. Yet psychiatrists often begin treatment with similar medications, sometimes trying several before finding one that helps.

Only about 30% of patients experience full relief from their very first antidepressant medication. Around one-third of people do not respond to standard antidepressant drugs overall.

Researchers at the University of Sydney wanted to investigate whether a subset of patients might share a particular vulnerability owed to disruption of their internal clocks.

The investigation goes well beyond poor sleep making people miserable. Someone whose internal clock runs late may feel wide awake at midnight but exhausted at 8 a.m. When school or work demands an earlier schedule, the mismatch can resemble living in a different time zone without ever traveling.

To investigate, the team examined questionnaires and genetic data from the Australian Genetics of Depression Study. The 11,380 participants predominantly had severe or recurring depression.

Researchers looked for six clues: a strong preference for evenings, unusually late sleep timing, sleeping more than usual during depression, feeling very tired on waking, seasonal changes in mood and behavior, and social jet lag.

The latter refers to the discrepancy between biological time, determined by our internal body clock, and social times, mainly dictated by social obligations such as school or work. Someone might force themselves awake at 7 a.m. during the week but naturally sleep until 11 a.m. on weekends.

Participants who met at least three of the six criteria entered the circadian group. The most common combination involved excessive sleep during depression, pronounced morning exhaustion and an evening preference.

Within this proposed subset, depression had begun at an average age of 18.7 in the circadian group, versus 23.2 among the others.

More than half reported at least ten depressive episodes, compared with just over a third of other patients with more typical depression. They also reported greater psychological distress, more suicidal thinking and more experiences resembling mania or psychosis.

This may be mostly related to genes. Participants in the circadian group had somewhat higher inherited risk scores for ADHD, bipolar disorder and depression, alongside traits associated with inflammation and insulin resistance.

Some symptoms overlapped with atypical depression, a recognized subset that can involve excessive sleeping and weight gain. But more than 1,400 participants with atypical depression did not meet the circadian criteria.

That suggests the two groups are not interchangeable, despite the overlap sometimes.

People in the circadian group rated two common antidepressant classes, SSRIs and SNRIs, as somewhat less effective and reported more side effects.

Previously, research linked evening chronotypes to poorer antidepressant responses. Separately, clinical trials have suggested that bright-light therapy can help some people with depression. Another review of 58 studies found evidence linking improvements in mood to better circadian alignment, particularly among people with delayed internal clocks.

Co-author Jacob Crouse argues that clock-focused treatments, including light therapy and melatonin, deserve consideration earlier in care.

“Because circadian depression is linked to people’s internal body clock, treatments targeting the circadian rhythm, such as light therapy and melatonin, may be much more relevant, and clinicians should reach for these much earlier in people with this presentation,” he said. “That means these genetic associations do not independently establish a distinct biological subtype of depression,” Ronan Lordan of the Royal College of Surgeons in Ireland told the Science Media Centre.

#4178. What Did Denisovans Look Like? Scientists Have Their Best Answer Yet

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Ancient proteins are helping scientists put a face to a long-lost branch of humanity.

For years, we’ve mostly known this mysterious human lineage from fossil bits and pieces. Scientists have found fossilized bones, skull parts, they’ve even found traces of their genes in people alive today. But what did the Denisovans look like?

Now, a new study is finally bringing their appearance into focus. By analyzing ancient proteins preserved in fossils from China, researchers have identified more Denisovan remains and pieced together a remarkably detailed picture of their features including massive brow ridges, sloping foreheads, and broad, relatively flat faces.

The findings also suggest something surprising: several ancient human groups that scientists thought were distinct may have been Denisovans all along.

The Denisovan story began in a cave in Siberia. In 2010, scientists analyzed DNA recovered from a tiny finger bone and discovered that it belonged to a previously unknown group of ancient humans. They named them after Denisova Cave, where the bone was found.

Denisovans were close relatives of Neanderthals. They had also interbred with our species, leaving traces of their ancestry in people alive today.

Unlike Neanderthals, whose skeletons have been found at numerous sites, Denisovans were initially known from only a handful of fragments. Scientists could read their genetic history more easily than they could picture their faces.

That began to change in 2025, when researchers showed that a nearly complete skull from Harbin, northeastern China, belonged to a Denisovan. The fossil, nicknamed “Dragon Man” and previously described as Homo longi, offered a rare look at the shape of a Denisovan head.

The latest discovery adds another crucial piece.

At Xujiayao in northern China, archaeologists excavated 20 human fossil specimens between 1976 and 1979. The roughly 199,000–138,000-year-old remains included skull and jaw fragments whose place on the human family tree remained disputed.

The DNA didn’t survive. But the new team, led by researchers in China, turned to something else: proteins preserved inside bone.

They recovered 18 ancient proteins from two fossils. In both specimens, they found a distinctive version of a collagen protein previously identified in confirmed Denisovans, but not in Neanderthals or modern humans. A separate analysis of the protein sequences also grouped the Xujiayao fossils with Denisovans.

Taken together, the Xujiayao remains and the Harbin skull suggest a few traits. Denisovans had long, low skulls, heavy brow ridges and foreheads that sloped backward. Their faces were relatively broad and flat through the cheeks, with little forward projection around the mouth. Their nasal openings were broad, and their skull bones thick.

So, they weren’t just Neanderthals living farther east. They shared some features with Neanderthals but differed in the back of the skull and parts of the jaw. Some features also resembled the much older Homo erectus, while others looked surprisingly modern.

Of course, these facial reconstructions can never be perfect; there’s a lot of information you can’t derive from bones, DNA, and proteins. For example, we can only infer their skin tone, hairstyle or exact facial appearance. Any lifelike image necessarily involves artistic interpretation. But it’s the first time we have a more complete idea of what Denisovans looked like.

“We are finally starting to put a face to the Denisovan,” says Sally Wasef, a palaeogeneticist at the Queensland University of Technology in Brisbane, Australia, for Nature.

The bones now reveal far more than scientists could have inferred from the original Siberian finger bone. And they may explain another longstanding mystery.

For decades, researchers encountered ancient human fossils across China that seemed to fit nowhere neatly. Some were classified as early modern humans. Others were treated as Neanderthal relatives or members of separate species.

The Xujiayao fossils themselves were recently placed in a proposed species called Homo juluensis, partly because of their enormous estimated braincase capacity of about 1,700 cubic centimeters. Meanwhile, the Harbin skull had been assigned to Homo longi.

The new study argues that differences such as skull size may not justify dividing these fossils into separate evolutionary branches. After comparing the bones from these regions, the scientists suggest we’re simply dealing with a varied Denisovan lineage.

This is still far from a settled issue, and the conclusion is stronger for the specimens identified through proteins or DNA than for those linked only by resemblance. But this would explain why scientists just weren’t finding any Denisovan remains; they were, but they didn’t realize they’re Denisovans.

The accumulating discoveries also suggest that Denisovans were remarkably adaptable.

Fossils and molecular evidence now place them across a huge swath of Asia, from Siberia to the Tibetan Plateau, northeastern China and as far south as the Taiwan Strait. The authors suggest they may have occupied parts of East Asia over hundreds of thousands of years, across radically different environments.

They weren’t a small group confined to a Siberian cave. They were a widespread part of humanity’s history.

And their story didn’t end entirely with extinction. Ancient interbreeding left Denisovan DNA in modern humans, particularly among populations in Oceania and parts of Asia. One inherited Denisovan-related genetic variant even helps some Tibetans cope with oxygen-poor air at high altitudes.

We still can’t know exactly what Denisovans were like. But scientists are no longer working with a faceless genome.

A population once revealed by a tiny finger bone is gradually emerging as a group of real people, with distinctive faces, varied lives and a place in our own ancestry. Real people who are now a part of our own genetic heritage.

#4190. Scientists Turned Shiitake Fungi Into Experimental Computer Memory

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Your next computer might grow in a compost heap.

You can grow a computer component on your kitchen counter. Well, sort of. That’s the radical implication of 2025 research from The Ohio State University, where scientists used shiitake fungi to make experimental electronic devices that can remember information.

In their study, research scientist in psychiatry John LaRocco and colleagues describe a fungus-based memory system intended for brain-inspired computing.

“Being able to develop microchips that mimic actual neural activity means you don’t need a lot of power for standby or when the machine isn’t being used,” said LaRocco in an October 2025 university release. “That’s something that can be a huge potential computational and economic advantage.”

Memristors — short for “memory resistors” — are electronic components whose resistance depends on their electrical history. This makes them useful candidates for neuromorphic computing, which takes inspiration from the brain. Traditional versions are made of silicon or metal oxides, using specialized fabrication processes. The Ohio State team decided to explore whether fungi could offer an alternative. Because why not?

Specifically, they turned to shiitake mushrooms (Lentinula edodes), known for their resilience and peculiar sensitivity to electrical stimuli. So, the researchers grew the fungi in Petri dishes filled with farro, wheat germ, and hay until the mycelium formed a dense white mat. Then they dried the samples in sunlight, rehydrated them just enough to restore conductivity, and connected them to an Arduino-powered circuit. What they got was a memristor — a sort of tiny bioelectronic brain cell — that could remember and respond to electrical patterns.

“We would connect electrical wires and probes at different points on the mushrooms because distinct parts of it have different electrical properties,” said LaRocco. “Depending on the voltage and connectivity, we were seeing different performances.”

The team reported roughly 90% accuracy in memory tests at up to 5,850 cycles per second. A separate test found a 95% match to ideal memristor behavior at 10 cycles per second and 1 volt. These percentages measure different things; neither establishes parity with commercial computer memory.

This isn’t the first time scientists have tried to merge mushrooms and machines. Mycologists and engineers have long been fascinated by mycelium, the network of filaments that makes up much of a fungus. Researchers are exploring how its electrical responses might serve both sensing and computing.

Here, “memory” refers to the material’s electrical behavior, rather than evidence that the mushrooms learned a task or thought like a brain.

But Ohio State’s work goes further: the researchers didn’t just observe electrical chatter — they trained it.

The paper describes a setup where each fungal disk acted as a node in a circuit, capable of volatile memory. Dehydration turned out to be key: drying the mushrooms made them durable while preserving their electronic traits. “We obtained experimental validation that dehydration can preserve the observed properties in a previously ‘programmed’ sample,” the team wrote in their study.

Space applications remain speculative. The experiment did not test these devices under radiation, and the paper acknowledges limited evidence directly connecting lentinan, a shiitake compound, to radiation resistance in the fungus.

And shiitake fungi come with some remarkable extras. They’re radiation-resistant, thanks in part to a compound called lentinan, which helps them endure oxidative stress. So, that opens the door to applications beyond Earth. “Shiitake has exhibited radiation resistance, suggesting its viability for aerospace applications,” the authors noted.

Beyond that, the environmental case for fungal electronics is equally compelling. Traditional semiconductor fabrication devours energy and produces electronic waste laced with heavy metals. Mycelium, by contrast, grows at room temperature and decomposes naturally.

“Society has become increasingly aware of the need to protect our environment and ensure that we preserve it for future generations,” said Qudsia Tahmina, a co-author and associate professor of electrical and computer engineering at Ohio State. “So that could be one of the driving factors behind new bio-friendly ideas like these.”

He reported memory behavior in a device with protective packaging that survived impact tests. But the work has not been peer reviewed, lacked a nonliving comparison system and covered only a week. It is a related experiment, not confirmation that shiitake computers are ready for everyday use.

For now, the prospect is a new way to make components, with plenty of engineering still to do. “The future of computing could be fungal,” the authors wrote.

Banks are warning against giving too much access to AI agents.

Whatever you do with AI, don’t give AI bots your banking information.

Agents like Meta’s Muse, OpenClaw and OpenAI’s dots are all the rage in the AI world these days. They promise to go beyond a chatbot that gives you information, becoming a tool where you can tell it to do something for you and it goes and does it.

When you first sign up for an AI agent, you may be asked to grant permission for it to access your apps, browsers and data so it can work with them. That might put your money at risk in unexpected ways.

“The intention might be something simple, like managing a calendar. But that access can give the agent the ability to do much more than the person intended,” said John Strand, the owner of the cybersecurity firm Black Hills Information Security. While you may be giving the agent access to simple smart home routines or getting help planning a trip, your banking information may also be accessible.

It might be tempting to think you can automate your financial chores and have more time for other things, but handing over your account credentials to an agent opens you up to many different things that can go wrong.

For one, AI agents might not be as secure as you think. This technology is very new — these kinds of always-on, persistent agents have really only been available for less than a year, and big companies like Meta and OpenAI have just put out their own versions in the last couple of months. These aren’t polished products that have had all the kinks worked out.

One of those challenges is that they don’t always behave in the way you expect or do what you want them to. Recent news stories about AI agents hacking other websites — including the AI platform Hugging Face and Australian government sites — have involved agents acting in unexpected ways to achieve an assigned goal. You don’t want AI to act like that with your checking account.

There are also plenty of reasons to be worried about the privacy of data shared with agents. Your agent might not meddle in unwanted ways with your money, but it could compromise your login info, letting someone nefarious meddle with your money.

When agents use your login info, “a bank cannot tell an authorized agent from an attacker using the exact same login,” said Ted Miracco, CEO of the mobile security firm Approov. “That is more of an identity failure than an AI failure. Until agents carry verifiable identities, scoped permissions and an audit trail, blocking them is the best defensible control a bank has.”

Many banks are aware of the risks and are already cracking down on unauthorized use of agentic agents. Charles Schwab’s CEO Rick Wurster told Bloomberg that bots are taking the liberty to move clients’ money.

“I get a report on how many times we’ve blocked clients from using one of these agents in a week, and it’s in the tens and twenties — per week — that the big agents are being blocked,” Wurster said in a Bloomberg TV interview. “What that tells me is that while people love AI, they’re not willing to turn their financial accounts over to them just yet.”

But that number is likely growing, and with bots going mainstream, it could grow rapidly. More banks are taking action against bots to protect you. A spokesperson from Wells Fargo said in an email to CNET that Wells Fargo the bank has already taken steps to block unauthorized agents from accessing customer accounts, too.

Banks aren’t completely against AI, though. Some just want you to use their features and tools instead. Wurster added in his interview that he recommends for Schwab customers to use Charley, Charles Schwab’s proprietary AI tool, to gather market insights, ask about personal financial information like balances and manage everyday financial tasks like transfers and payments. These measures make it much safer, but in the meantime, it may be best to keep your banking business to yourself.

If you want to check to see if bots have been meddling in your banking, look at your login history and account activity. Strand suggests looking for activity that doesn’t match your pattern — like multiple logins in a short period or access happening at the same time every day. That may not mean it’s your AI agent, but it’s worth keeping track of. If you notice anything suspicious, contact your bank immediately for assistance.

Strand also recommends enabling two-factor authentication now as a preventative step to safeguard your financial data, but it’s also a good time to reevaluate the access you’re giving AI. Strand also recommends restricting which applications and sites AI agents can access.

“Check the permissions. Make sure they’re limited to what it needs to do the job you gave it, and remove anything beyond that,” he added.

Shang Saavedra, founder of personal finance platform Save My Cents, suggested you regularly revisit your AI agent’s access to apps and data and require it to ask for permission before accessing new data, apps and features.

If you decide to use AI for personal finance purposes, set limitations. “I’ve tested AI with some basic questions and basic calculations, and the information it provides is good for a beginner plan that gives you some pointers as to where to go,” said Saavedra. “I also like using AI to search for book and resource suggestions.”

But the actual handling of money? Maybe do that yourself.

Since elementary school, we’ve all been taught the “5-Second Rule.” The idea is simple: Food dropped on the floor is still perfectly edible if retrieved in under five seconds. If you snatch the food up quick enough, they won’t have time to get it “dirty.”

Even as kids, we were skeptical but admit it–you still roll the dice on it every now and again. It’s like shouting “shotgun” before getting into the passenger seat even if it’s only you and the driver. It’s instinctual. But is it true?

“Let’s go with ‘No,’ there is no truth to the ‘5-second rule’,” says Gillson Sciences Director of Operations Erin Thane. “But I would argue that specifics matter.”

As Thane explains, variables such as comparative wetness of said food and type of flooring can swing things one way or another. “Food dropped on a carpet will likely pick up less contamination than food dropped on a smooth surface with the same microbial loads. Sticky or wet foods will likely pick up more contamination than dry foods dropped on the same contaminated surfaces.”

And that’s not all. “There are actually a lot of nuances to this question,” says Thane. “Is it the floor of Windsor Castle or a public bathroom? Was sticky gum dropped and then put back in the mouth, or was it a goldfish cracker? Am I a healthy adult eating something off the ground or an immunocompromised individual?”

Perhaps more surprising, according to Thane, is that the floors in your home and floors in public spaces actually don’t vary in cleanliness as much as you’d like to think. As Thane puts it, “We all wear the same shoes–or socks hopefully in the home–across the floors we encounter daily, like the kitchen and bathroom, to other areas or spaces. We are constantly picking up hitchhiking germs and depositing them from one floor to the next.”

Specifically skin-inhabiting bacteria such as Staphylococcus (which puts the “Staph” in “Staph Infection”) and Corynebacterium (which can cause diphtheria) to gut bacteria such as Bacteroides. Shoes, clothing, and bags can also carry Bacillus or mold or yeast that can cause nausea and vomiting.

While there are mitigating factors, it’s safe to say that in terms of grossness, the “5-Second Rule” is pretty high up there.

“I would certainly stay away from any wet, moist or sticky foods which act like a sponge and will immediately cling to any and all dirt, debris and contamination that it comes into contact with,” advises Thane. “However, after everything we have discussed so far, I refer back to the best practice or rule of thumb which is to just not eat any food off the floor.”

#4097. Chart: A record two-thirds of car sales in China are now electric vehicles

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

For the first time, new-energy vehicles (NEVs) made up 67% of new car sales in China in September, according to the latest data from the China Passenger Car Association (CPCA).

The figures show that NEVs, which include battery electric cars and plug-in hybrids, captured a record share of sales, even though fewer were sold than last year.

This is because sales of combustion-engine cars are falling steeply, a trend that the CPCA has attributed to rising petrol prices following the start of the Hormuz crisis.

Indeed, sales of fuel-only cars were down 31% year-on-year in the first nine months of 2026, with plug-in hybrid sales down 26% – whereas battery EV sales were only down 4%.

One analyst tells Carbon Brief that China’s rapidly growing EV fleet has offset demand for 143m barrels of oil since the start of the Hormuz crisis.

Another says that the crisis is “reinforcing China’s electrification of road transport”.

Meanwhile, amid slowing domestic sales, Chinese carmakers’ exports of NEVs more than doubled [in 2026 to date / in September] from a year earlier.

A record two out of every three cars sold in September in China were NEVs, according to CPCA data, as shown in the figure below.

(NEVs predominantly include battery electric vehicles – BEVs – and plug-in hybrids  – PHEVs. BEVs only run on electricity, whereas PHEVs also have a combustion engine.)

This marks a rapid rise for NEVs from just 6% of new car sales in 2020 up to 58% in 2026 to date – and a record 67% in the month of September.

This year, NEVs’ share of sales has also bounced back from a low of 39% in January, after the full purchase tax exemption that had been in place for more than a decade came to an end in December 2025.

The gain in NEV share in 2026 has come amid a slowdown across the wider domestic passenger-car market, which has hit sales of combustion-engine vehicles particularly hard.

This September, passenger-car retail sales across all categories declined, with overall sales volumes falling 28% year-on-year.

The contraction was led by combustion-engine vehicles, which saw sales fall 43% year-on-year in September and 31% in 2026 to date.

However, sales of PHEVs also fell by 36% year-on-year in September and by 26% in 2026 to date. By comparison, BEVs performed better, although sales still declined 8.5% from a year earlier in September and 4% in 2026 to date.

As a result, NEV sales overall fell by 17% in September and 13% in 2026 to date.

While the market changes in 2026 to date were particularly dramatic, they reflect a broad continuation of trends seen over the past decade.

Indeed, sales of combustion-engine cars in China peaked in 2017 and they have been declining ever since, shown in the figure below.

Part of the reason for the advance of NEVs in 2026, said Chinese technology news outlet OFweek, was petrol cars “giving up market share” as they were outperformed by electric alternatives.

Cui Dongshu, secretary-general of the CPCA, cited higher petrol prices as an explanation for September’s weak combustion-car sales, in comments to financial news outlet National Business Daily.

Imports of oil into China in January-September have fallen 15% compared to 2025, equivalent to 470m barrels, as ongoing conflict tightened supply and drove up prices.

China’s passenger-car NEV fleet has displaced 143m barrels of oil so far during the strait of Hormuz crisis, Euan Graham, senior energy analyst at thinktank Ember, tells Carbon Brief.

EVs “accounted for 12% of the decrease in China’s oil use post-Hormuz”, according to an estimate provided to thinktank the Brookings Institute by an unnamed analyst.

Erica Downs, a senior research scholar at Columbia University’s Center on Global Energy Policy, tells Carbon Brief that the strait of Hormuz crisis and higher oil prices are “reinforcing China’s electrification of road transport”.

She says the “world’s largest oil supply disruption” vindicates China’s early bet on EVs, which was partly driven by concerns about oil-supply security.

Nevertheless, NEV sales have fallen year-on-year, albeit at a slower rate than overall car sales. The growth in NEV market share has also been decelerating.

Zhang Yichao, a partner at consulting firm AlixPartners, tells Carbon Brief that NEVs benefit from government trade-in policies and a “strong pipeline” of new models, particularly those involving innovation.

He says these factors help explain why NEVs sales have fallen at a slower rate to their combustion-car counterparts.

Cui told the state-run China National Radio that the fall for NEVs and for cars overall in September 2026, is also due to a high baseline of sales from the same month in 2025, as well as changes to trade-in policies and disruptions from the holiday period.

Other factors, says consulting firm Wood Mackenzie, are the weakened demand due to “demand pull-forward” in 2025 caused by the end of subsidies, as well as “tighter financing conditions”.

Zhang tells Carbon Brief he does not “see much more room ahead” for further growth in NEVs’ market share.

He adds that, in his view, their share in China is unlikely to exceed 80%, as some premium-market segments and specific use cases will continue to require combustion-engine vehicles.

Meanwhile, while domestic NEV retail sales fell 17% in September 2026, exports more than doubled, increasing 164% year-on-year, according to the CPCA.

Exports are becoming increasingly important to Chinese carmakers, in the face of weaker domestic demand and cut-throat industrial competition.

The CPCA says exports have become “a core priority” for some carmakers.

However, the surge in exports from China has created concern in the EU, which has placed tariffs on imports of EVs from China and recently agreed plans to cut imports of hybrids.

Countries including Mexico and Turkey have also placed tariffs on Chinese EV imports.

At the same time, the Chinese government is putting policies in place that might be expected to disincentivise exports, such as cutting VAT export rebates.

Analysts differ on what impact this policy shift will have, with some saying it will be limited, while others believe it could cause the price of exported cars to rise.
