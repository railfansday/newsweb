#3744. Hyve Smart Package Locker Review: Protecting Your Holiday Deliveries

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

The onslaught of tech gear being delivered for WIRED Reviews, combined with the samples I receive constantly for my side hustle at Drinkhacker, means that delivery trucks are a constant occurrence on my suburban street. UPS and FedEx both make drops at my house nearly every day, and that’s not to mention occasional deliveries from the likes of DHL and, of course, Amazon and its ilk. But it’s usually not a problem. I work from home, so picking up packages left on the doorstep is just part of the scenery.

What is a problem is that I can’t be home 24 hours a day, and even though I have a very kind neighbor across the way who keeps an eye out for boxes on my front steps when I’m traveling, he isn’t always at home either. Package hold requests aren’t consistently honored by either of the major carriers—and earlier this year, FedEx seemingly discontinued its vacation hold option without bothering to tell anyone, the modern epidemic of porch piracy be damned. (A rep has since confirmed this with me, though no formal announcement has ever been made.)

Having packages held at shipping centers or access points usually costs money, typically about $5 per package and often more depending on the length of the hold, and that involves a trip after I get home to collect what’s arrived. If I’m gone for a week, that can mean fetching a dozen boxes or more.

All of that is a time-consuming pain, for which a startup called Hyve has come along to offer an assuagement: A smart lockbox that lets delivery drivers drop off packages in a secure location, notifying the homeowner whenever it’s been accessed. While the growing pains were significant, after weeks of testing, I found it a useful device that—just maybe—may earn a permanent home on my front porch.

Parcel lockboxes aren’t exactly a new concept. They operate basically like a standard post office mailbox, with a cantilevered hatch up top that allows deliveries to drop into a secure storage area below, where they can only be accessed through a keyed door. Delivery folks can put packages in through the hatch, but only you can get them out.

The Hyve Smart Package Locker is a play on this idea, forgoing the two-chamber design in favor of an electronic hatch accessed via 4-digit PINs. The box itself measures 32 inches high, 20 inches wide, and 20 inches deep, with interior dimensions about 4 inches shorter in each direction. That makes for a total of 7.4 cubic feet, which Hyve says will fit 80 percent of Amazon package sizes (though obviously this depends on the specifics of your ordering habits). For me, that math worked out: Deliveries of three-pack bottles of wine fit fine, but anything larger was typically left out by the courier.

It’s built from a combination of plastic and steel, and while the side panels seem a little flimsy, there’s enough reinforcement behind them that I never worried that the unit would be easily damaged by a would-be package thief without power tools. Units are available in gray or white; I requested the latter to match my home’s color, but alas received a gray one.

Assembly is required—and be warned, it’s no cakewalk. The locker comes packed flat, notably with no printed instructions on how to put all the pieces together. A QR code in the box takes you to a web page featuring a walkthrough video and a PDF guide, which are my two least favorite ways to be guided through an installation process on a cell phone. The PDF is very difficult to read on a small screen, and this contributed to making what was already a cumbersome construction process even more difficult. I consider myself quite handy, but I ultimately spent nearly an hour putting the locker together due to a couple of easy-to-overlook goofs. Real, printed instructions—especially considering they’re all of five pages long—would go a long way toward streamlining the process. Or, better yet, why not just ship the thing preassembled?

A security bracket and cable tether are included so you can attach the Hyve to an immovable object on your property to prevent pirates from simply running off with the entire thing. (Unloaded, the device weighs 40 pounds. While awkward, it would be fairly easy to abscond with if it isn’t physically locked down.) Though oddly, the installation of these components is covered only in the video, not in the written instructions.

The unit is powered by four AA batteries and features a solar panel on top for trickle charging a backup battery. There’s also a USB-C port that can be used to supply emergency power if all else fails. Hyve says a fresh set of cells should last about five or six months, though strangely, my first quartet of AAs died inside of three weeks and without warning. There’s fortunately a USB-C port that can be used to supply emergency power if all else fails (which I needed when my cells died).

The Hyve Locker requires its own Wi-Fi bridge, which you must plug into an inside outlet near where you’ll be placing the locker. I found this to be a bit of a tall order, as I don’t have any power outlets in my entry hall, and an outlet only 15 feet away from the locker turned out to be out of range. (Exterior walls will do that to a wireless signal.) I ended up finding another outlet, forgotten in the guest room, that was able to reach the Hyve, but if that hadn’t worked, I would have been forced to relocate the locker elsewhere. Fortunately, the signal has held strong over several weeks of testing.

Hyve’s app is deceptively simple. At its most basic, the homeowner sets a master PIN, which everyone uses for access to the locker to both make deliveries and to retrieve them. It’s a simple idea, but it obviously presents a bit of a security risk, since anyone with the four-digit code can pop the lid open and rifle through what’s inside. Whether this is a legitimate concern or not is perhaps a matter of personal risk tolerance. Due to the physical design of the device, there’s nothing preventing one courier from removing a package that another has left inside the Hyve, but then again, there’s nothing preventing them from taking an unattended package off your doorstep, either. Presumably for Hyve, and I would agree, random passers-by are the bigger concern for most homeowners, so the monolithic PIN mode is probably good enough for many users.

If you want more fine-grained security than the mono-PIN, you’ll need a subscription plan. Hyve’s Essentials subscription plan runs $5 per month or $50 per year, the main feature being that you can set separate PINs for each delivery service or carrier (including major shippers as well as services including DoorDash, Instacart, Best Buy, and more). With this setup, the app notifies you each time the lid is opened, and it tells you who opened it (or at least who employs them), logging it in the system. One month of Essentials is included with purchase.

What do you do with all these PINs? Alas, you’ll have to go it alone, though Hyve provides detailed advice on how to enter the PIN and information about the lockbox into each carrier’s delivery instructions page on their website. You’ll need an account with each carrier, tied to your address, and I found that some of these instructions are more useful than others. Both FedEx and UPS, for example, provide fields for entering gate codes, but neither lets you specify specifically that packages should be left in a lockbox. (Both used to offer free-form delivery instruction fields, but these have recently changed into more simplified drop-down systems, and “lockbox” is not a choice for a delivery location.)

If you don’t have the Hyve Returns subscription but do have Essentials, you can still use the Hyve for on-demand returns by scheduling a pickup through Uber Courier, with no additional charges due to Hyve. The trick with this scheme is that while you avoid the $15 subscription fee, you have to pay Uber’s service charge for the pickup. Those fees vary based on the distance to your nearest UPS, FedEx, or Amazon drop-off location. For me, the Uber Courier charge for dropping off an Amazon return typically ranged from $8–18, making the $15 twice-monthly return plan far more enticing than the ad hoc option. (But alas, unavailable to me.)

I did test the alternate method by using Uber Courier to create a return, and found that Hyve didn’t add much to the equation. The only difference with Hyve is that instead of grabbing the item from the doorstep, the driver fetched it from the bin with a PIN. More secure? Yes, but given that I only had to wait 20 minutes for the driver to show up, the risk of an exposed package was minimal compared to receiving a delivery when I wasn’t home for days.

While the lockbox is rather obtrusive on my front porch, the hardware works well. The weatherproofing is fine, the door opens and closes with a satisfying, locking click, and the box sounds an alarm if it’s left open for too long. Push notifications from the Hyve mobile app come quickly and accurately, letting you know whether it was FedEx or Amazon—or you—that opened the hatch.

There is, unfortunately, a catch, and at first I found it to be an awfully big one. The problem was that no delivery driver seemed to want to use the Hyve locker, no matter how much I tried to get them to.

After getting everything set up on day one, I waited with anticipation when FedEx arrived with a box, and my phone lit up with a text from the company that the delivery had been completed. Alas, it was not in the Hyve locker but sitting next to it, nestled against the exterior. I was crestfallen, yet undeterred, and kept trying to get someone to open that hatch.

The next day, I encountered the FedEx driver in person, only because he rang the bell because he had a delivery with a signature required. I asked if he had seen the delivery instructions on my FedEx account and, with a puzzled look, said that he “thought he heard something” when pulling up to my house. It turns out that FedEx’s driver devices provide verbal instructions on where to leave packages (in addition to rather garbled written ones, which he showed me), but that he had the volume on his device turned down. Clad in my bathrobe, I demonstrated the Hyve to him personally and got his buy-in.

The following day, I was thrilled to get a push notification from the Hyve app that FedEx had successfully left a box for me inside. The driver even rang the bell again, only to show me his success with a smile. I tell you, opening that hatch and seeing my package inside was like Christmas morning for both of us.

Alas, on the third day, a fresh FedEx delivery appeared on the porch again. My driver had moved to another route.

I was about to give up on the whole shebang when, all of a sudden, something clicked. FedEx, UPS, and Amazon all started using the Hyve locker, and regularly. I soon became accustomed to getting notifications multiple times a day that the box had received a delivery, with my estimation of its usage rate approaching 75 percent. No longer was my doorstep darkened with cardboard, as trips to empty out the Hyve became an undertaking necessary to complete several times a day.

In fact, on some days, the usage level revealed an entirely new problem: A single UPS delivery of a few boxes could fill the locker, leaving no room for anything else. My hopes of leaving for a week and not having to worry about packages were dashed; there’s just not enough space for more than a day or two at most in the bin, given the pace of deliveries at my house.

Early on in my testing, I spoke to Hyve about the usage issues I was experiencing at the time, and its CEO freely admitted that this is, in no uncertain terms, a problem for many homeowners. To combat it, the company provides an adhesive sign that you can use to instruct carriers to use the box, and while this was likely quite helpful at improving usage rates, it’s not a panacea (nor is it particularly attractive).

And over a Zoom call, the CEO showed me another innovation that she said was having even better results: Attaching a placard that reads “Snack Stop” to the top of the lockbox, and stocking a collection of treats like chips and meat sticks inside for hungry drivers, encouraging them to open it up to grab a bite when making their rounds. What delivery driver could possibly say no to a free Slim Jim?

FIRST ON FOX — The Pentagon is preparing U.S. military bases to keep operating for at least 90 days without outside support as it prepares for the possibility of a major war that could cripple power grids, disrupt supply chains and threaten critical infrastructure on American soil.

The new requirement is part of FORTRESS, a broader military initiative aimed at ensuring American forces can continue fighting even if the civilian systems they depend on are disrupted, according to documents reviewed by Fox News Digital.

AMERICA'S NEXT WAR COULD REACH US SOIL, TOP ARMY GENERAL WARNS

"We don't want to rely on standard civilian municipal infrastructure to run our national defense. We just simply can't. It's an intolerable risk," Pentagon chief spokesperson Sean Parnell told Fox News Digital.

Army launching new initiative to build nuclear micro reactors on bases. (Tony Gutierrez/AP:Getty Images)

The Pentagon is also laying the groundwork for civilian factories to shift to defense production during a crisis, while preparing alternative supply chains and strategic reserves to keep weapons, fuel and other essential materials flowing.

"Power is combat now. Without electricity, you lose communications, water, logistics, assembly lines and just basic military command structures," Parnell, who led the FORTRESS initiative, said.

Under FORTRESS, every major military installation in the continental United States would be expected to sustain essential operations independently for at least 90 days, including access to electricity, water, fuel and communications.

TRUMP PENTAGON LAUNCHES COMPETITION FOR AMERICA'S NEXT GREAT MILITARY BASE

The requirement reflects a broader shift in how the Pentagon is preparing for threats to the American homeland, including the possibility that a major conflict could disrupt the infrastructure supporting military operations inside the United States.

"I realized that a lot of the way we think about homeland defense was built around the antiquated assumption that two oceans would protect us," Parnell said. "President Trump understands that world is gone, and he's already paved the way for new thinking: Golden Dome, a renewed focus on the Western Hemisphere, Greenland, Panama."

A central concern is the military's reliance on civilian power grids, which support everything from communications and logistics to weapons manufacturing.

The seal of the Department of War is displayed inside the Pentagon in Washington, D.C. (elal Gunes/Anadolu via Getty Images)

FORTRESS envisions portable nuclear reactors, large-scale battery storage and microgrids capable of disconnecting military installations from the civilian electrical grid, allowing them to continue operating during prolonged power disruptions.

"Mission-critical power must be independent, hardened, and redundant," Parnell said.

The Pentagon has already announced plans to reduce major military installations' dependence on civilian power grids. The 90-day self-sufficiency standard goes further, extending the requirement beyond electricity to other essential services needed to sustain military operations.

The strategy also anticipates major disruptions to the global supply chains that provide the military with weapons, equipment and critical materials.

"The next conflict will likely be fought on multiple fronts. Look at the geopolitics behind any major war. When that happens, global supply chains will break," Parnell said.

To prepare, FORTRESS calls for strategic reserves of munitions, fuel, critical minerals, electronics, spare parts and medical supplies that could sustain military operations if international supply routes are disrupted or domestic manufacturers cannot keep pace with wartime demand.

Reserve targets would vary depending on how quickly supplies are consumed during a major conflict and how long manufacturers would need to replace them.

STARTUPS BRING INNOVATION TO DEFENSE INDUSTRIAL BASE

For the military's most critical defense dependencies, the initiative also establishes a requirement for at least two qualified supply or production pathways, reducing the risk that the loss of a single factory, supplier or industrial capability could interrupt military operations.

Another major component involves preparing America's civilian manufacturing sector to support a wartime mobilization.

Through the Civil Reserve Manufacturing Network, an existing initiative incorporated into FORTRESS, the Pentagon plans to identify commercial factories capable of shifting to defense production during a crisis.

The new details describe plans to assess manufacturers' capabilities and establish technical specifications, cybersecurity protections, contracting arrangements and conversion procedures in advance, rather than waiting until a conflict begins.

FORTRESS also calls for recurring industrial stress tests and mobilization exercises to assess whether manufacturers could maintain production through prolonged disruptions.

Beyond energy and manufacturing, the initiative includes efforts to strengthen military communications, navigation and satellite capabilities against interference, including preparations to maintain operations when GPS signals are jammed or space-based assets are disrupted.

Together, the measures are intended to reduce the military's dependence on civilian infrastructure and vulnerable supply networks during a prolonged conflict.

"You don't want to be making these decisions in the middle of a global multi-front conflict. We have to have a plan," Parnell said.

He described FORTRESS as an insurance policy for the homeland, designed to ensure American forces can continue operating even if a major war disrupts the infrastructure and supply chains they rely on.

#3753. What the US Movement Against Data Centers Can Learn From Abroad

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Paris Marx’s new book couldn’t have landed at a better time. The longtime critic of Big Tech and host of the Tech Won’t Save Us podcast has been thinking about data centers for years, and Hyperscale: The Ambition and Excess of Big Tech's Data Empires comes out right as opposition hits a fever pitch.

“I certainly didn't predict that it was all going to kind of come to a head as my book was coming out,” he says with a laugh.

While data centers are playing a role in shaping US elections—they’re one of the issues that could decide key midterm races in less than two weeks—there’s a history of struggle around the world. Marx’s book takes the reader on a tour of the tech industry’s quest for power and its opposition, from antiwar protests targeting American computers during the Vietnam War, to more recent resistance in Ireland and Chile, and an alternative vision for the future of data centers in New Zealand.

I caught up with him this week to talk about what he’s seen, and what could happen if the AI bubble pops.

This interview has been edited and condensed for clarity.

MOLLY TAFT: When did you decide to write a book about data centers?

PARIS MARX: In the Netherlands and Singapore, they had moratoriums going back to 2019. But in 2022, there was a growing recognition of the demands of data centers in Ireland, on the energy grid in particular. There were huge quantities of electricity that data centers were starting to consume over there.

The other story that really got on my radar was what happened with Google in The Dalles, Oregon. Google was really trying to hide its water use figures, to the degree that it got the city council to try to keep its water use figures secret. They kept that going for the better part of a year before the community finally got the actual numbers and found out that Google was using 29 percent of the water in the community.

ChatGPT came out at the end of 2022. It became apparent that there's going to need to be a lot more data centers for generative AI. These places were already feeling the effects of significant data center construction, and it felt like that was going to start coming to a lot more communities very soon.

I feel like a lot of Americans think data centers just came out of the blue in the US over the past year or two, just in time to be this huge midterms issue. One thing I like about your book is that it shows us how other countries and communities served as early warning signs.

Ireland was a real example of what can happen. Dublin had a moratorium passed back in 2021. It was actually the grid operator that said, “Hey, there are too many data centers. We do not have the grid capacity to connect any more of them.”

In response to that, you had the industry moving to make sure it can still build data centers in Ireland. They can't get grid connections in Dublin, so they start looking at building in other parts of Ireland. They also looked at starting to tap into the gas network so they can use generators. Eventually, they start building dedicated gas power plants to power their data centers instead of connecting to the grid.

The Irish energy grid is a public grid—only the public utility can build electricity lines. And data center companies actually lobbied for legislation that essentially allows private companies to build electricity cables. That obviously concerns a lot of people and presents questions about equity in the system.

The gas stuff looks so prescient with what’s happening in the US now, where companies are racing to build these massive private gas power plants for data centers.

[Data center operators] were getting these emission licenses from the Irish Environmental Protection Agency to be able to burn all these fossil fuels. The agency was warning that climate targets are going to be a lot harder to achieve now.

When I started to see that happen in the US, I wasn’t surprised. I’d already seen the story play out before. But the scale of it is staggering, and how quickly it’s escalated.

What happens to the physical infrastructure—from the gas plants to the data centers themselves—that's getting built out if this industry falls apart? Is there historic precedent for that?

I think we're in an AI bubble. But what does the collapse of that bubble look like? They're planning to add all these data centers and all of these gas power plants in order to power the data centers. But there are issues with getting turbines, with getting chips, with getting different kinds of pieces of the puzzle for the data center itself. Are these even going to get built out before there’s a reassessment of the demand for AI?

We often talk about the excess build-out of fiber capacity back in the dotcom boom and bust. But there was also a build-out of data centers—obviously a much earlier stage than what we're at now. A lot of data center companies went under. Companies like Equinix, which did survive, bought up a ton of data centers to be ready for when that capacity returned.

But the issue we have now is that if data centers are being planned for AI use, and we’re operating under the assumption that the chips are going to have to be cycled out within a few years. Even if the data center is there, what makes it most valuable is potentially not going to be useful by the time that demand returns.

Inevitably, even if the data centers don't come, then those gas plants will exist. They will be looking for new customers to justify their existence. I'm sure that people are not building them with the intention to just shut them down.

You ended the book with a specific example in New Zealand of a community-led effort to create a more accurate speech-recognition model of the Māori language, using local cloud providers to host the data. I really liked this alternative vision of a non-extractive way to harness the powers of AI. Have you thought about what we could do to make some changes and how this build-out is going?

I would like to see governments go really hard on actually putting firmer regulations on the development of data centers. But those should also be paired with regulations on the AI products themselves. We had a lot of promises about what generative AI was going to deliver that haven't been realized. There has also been a long list of harms that have arisen from these technologies that we were not told would come along with them. Our governments do not seem to be so willing to actually take that on. They're buying the narrative that generative AI is still what Sam Altman sold it to be in early 2023 rather than what it has become today.

I think that we do need to be thinking of how we do technology differently, especially in this moment where we're dedicating so many resources to the infrastructure to enable generative AI. We should be asking, how much computation do we really need? How many resources are actually justifiable to put into this project? What are we actually getting out of it, right?  Does what we're getting from what Silicon Valley is creating really justify the resources that we are collectively sacrificing in order to realize it? If we actually did the assessment, I think that we would very clearly find that there's no way to justify what we are doing right now—from a financial perspective, but also from a social and environmental one.

Speaking of those climate targets, it’s not just the Irish government that’s thinking about them. My colleague Joel Khalil covers how the European Union’s thirst for AI is running into European countries’ climate goals. Joel tells me he wouldn’t be surprised if data centers become a political flash point in Europe, like they are in the US, as countries go to the polls next year. “The fascinating thing about the European data center build-out is the collision of competing interests: national-level policy butts up against local priorities, ambitions for economic growth stand in conflict with climate objectives, and planning bureaucracy foils attempts by AI companies to rapidly expand their fleets,” he says. “It’s hard to see how Europe could possibly have it all.”

#4128. How the Romans Invented Apartment Living. It Was Brilliant, Brutal, and Often Deadly

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Home → Features → History and Humanities → History

Long before modern cities built upward, Rome learned how to stack human lives.

In ancient Rome, relief from rent often came only after one’s death.

On a tombstone outside the city, an ex-slave named Ancarenus Nothus left behind what later scholars called “The Tenant’s Lament.” His epitaph reads: “My body knows no longer hunger . . . now it is no longer [paying] deposit on the rent, but enjoys for free an eternal lodging.”

Ancarenus had a hard life, but he chose to put a funny twist on it. His last words to the world are, however, strikingly familiar to most of us modern urbanites.

For most people in the capital of the ancient world, Rome did not mean marble villas, garden courtyards, and reclining couches. That was reserved for the 0.1%. Their living meant crammed quarters, noise, rent due, smoke rising from below, chamber pots above, and walls that might crack while the landlord assured everyone that all was well.

The Romans built one of the first societies of mass urban housing. Their apartment blocks, known as insulae, helped make Rome and its port city Ostia dense, walkable, commercially alive, and socially mixed.

They also made everyday life precarious in ways that feel startlingly modern.

The Latin word insula literally means “island.” In the Roman city, the name made sense. These buildings often filled entire blocks, with streets flowing around them like water.

The form varied, but the basic pattern looks familiar. Shops opened onto the street at ground level. Above them rose rented rooms and apartments. Stairs led upward to reach the flats. The farther up you climbed, the cheaper and worse life usually became.

That inverted the logic of the modern penthouse. Before elevators, height meant lots of sweat, all sorts of dangers, and raw inconvenience. The rich preferred the lower floors, where access came easily and escape remained possible. The poor climbed toward the roof.

Most tenants lived in tiny single rooms called cellae, meaning “small room” but also “cellar”. Entire families often shared this cramped space. In others, especially in Ostia, apartments could be spacious, well-lit, and arranged around courtyards. Some had several rooms. Some even borrowed features from elite houses, such as reception spaces and decorative mosaics.

In other words, the insula was not just one thing. It could be a cramped tenement, a mixed-use commercial block, or an upper-middle-class apartment building. Rome had its slums. It also had its prime real estate.

The scale could be astonishing. By late antiquity, catalogues listed more than 44,000 insulae in Rome, compared with fewer than 2,000 private houses.

The villa may dominate our imagination of Roman life, but it was the mass housing apartment that ordinary Romans — as many as one million by the 2nd century CE — called home.

Rome faced a problem that modern cities know well: people kept coming.

Migrants arrived for work, food, patronage, trade, and proximity to power. The free monthly grain (Cura annonae) starting with Augustus’ reign made Rome even more attractive to migrants. The city grew to a scale Europe would not see again until London in the 19th century. But Rome had no railways, no steel frames, no elevators, no modern plumbing, and no Portland cement.

Faced with such an incoming flux of people from all corners of their vast empire, the Romans saw it only logical to build upward.

In his new book, Dwelling on Earth: The Past and Future of the Places We Call Home, architect and urban planner Sten Al quotes the Roman architect Vitruvius, who wrote: “With the present importance of the city and the unlimited numbers of its population, it is necessary to increase the number of dwelling-places indefinitely.”

“The case has made it necessary to find relief by making the buildings high,” Vitruvius wrote.

Like today, Roman concrete formed the bedrock of the insulae. Builders mixed lime with volcanic ash, especially the prized material from around Puteoli and the Bay of Naples. Seneca captured the wonder of it in a line: “The dust at Puteoli becomes stone if it touches water.”

The concrete, which wasn’t properly reinvented until 1824 when English stonemason Joseph Aspdin patented Portland cement, helped the Romans build harbors, baths, amphitheaters, domes, and multistory housing. Marcus Licinius Crassus, one of the richest men in Roman history, understood this better than most. According to Plutarch, he bought slaves trained as architects and builders, then moved in on fire-damaged properties when desperate owners sold cheaply. He acquired buildings at “a trifling price,” rebuilt them, and watched his fortune grow. “In this way the largest part of Rome came into his possession,” Plutarch noted.

The pattern sounds modern because the incentive has not changed much. Where land grows scarce and demand rises, housing becomes not only shelter, but also ripe for speculation. Huge fortunes could be amassed simply by owning a small piece of land. The same surface area could be essentially worthless just a few miles away from Rome in the countryside.

Cicero, the polished statesman, was among the patricians who drew income from rental property. However, he was better with words than with real estate. In one letter, he wrote: “Two of my shops have fallen down and the rest are cracking. Not only the tenants but the very mice have migrated.”

He did not linger in pity though, for he was extremely wealthy from his income as an elite lawyer and orator. “Other people call this a misfortune, I don’t call it even a nuisance.”

The ancient rental market offered tenants little protection. Poor residents often rented month to month, although they could sublet rooms, which helped ease some of the pressure. And legal responsibility could become a maze if something fell from an upper window and injured a passerby.

Roman writers did not romanticize apartment life.

Juvenal, the great poet of urban disgust, imagined Rome as a city barely held together: “We live in a city shored up for the most part with gimcrack stays and props: that’s how our landlords arrest the collapse of their property, papering over great cracks in the ramshackle fabric, reassuring the tenants they can sleep secure, when all the time the building is poised like a house of cards.”

The collapse of decrepit and neglected buildings was not the only danger. Fire haunted the city, always lurking like a spectre since the Eternal City was first founded in the 8th century BCE.

Cooking flames, oil lamps, wooden beams, crowded rooms, narrow streets, and virtually no fire rules made every block vulnerable. Vitruvius hated wattle and daub for that reason.

“As for ‘wattle and daub’ I could wish that it had never been invented. The more it saves in time and gains in space, the greater and the more general is the disaster that it may cause; for it is made to catch fire, like torches.”

Augustus created the vigiles, Rome’s fire brigade, partly because apartment fires had become a public crisis. Nero, after the Great Fire of 64 CE, finally imposed building reforms: wider streets, fire-resistant materials, porches from which fires could be fought, and height limits. Later emperors tightened the rules further.

Juvenal put the terror of the upper-floor tenant with poise: “I prefer to live without fires and midnight panics.”

He continued: “By the time the smoke’s got up to your third floor apartment (and you [are] still asleep), your heroic downstairs neighbour is roaring for water and shifting his bits and pieces to safety. If the alarm goes at ground level, the last to fry will be the attic tenant, way up among the nesting pigeons.”

Apartment life also meant living without privacy. Roman insulae indeed had windows, but not the kind you might imagine. They rarely had glass. Most were open, unglazed holes in the wall fitted with wooden shutters or curtains to block the weather. Glass windows were expensive and mostly limited to the wealthy

Upper-floor units lacked running water. Because ancient Rome did not use mechanical water pumps to push water up multi-story buildings, the distribution of water was limited to the ground. Residents hauled water from public fountains or paid carriers if they could afford it. Cooking at home could be difficult or dangerous, so many people bought prepared food from thermopolia, the Roman fast-food stalls that served hot meals to passersby.

Laundry went out. Bathing often took place elsewhere. Shops, taverns, shrines, baths, and apartments formed one urban ecosystem.

That density made Roman streets lively. It also made them very filthy.

Tenants used chamber pots. Some carried them downstairs. Others couldn’t care less and threw the contents out of the windows. Roman law recognized the problem because victims could claim compensation if falling waste injured them. The legal action known as the actio de deiectis vel effusis (literally, “action for things thrown or poured out”) was designed specifically to protect pedestrians and keep public streets safe.

You did not have to prove who threw the waste. Because it was nearly impossible for a victim on the street to look up and see exactly which window a liquid came from, Roman law held the head of the household (the occupier of the room) automatically responsible, even if a guest or a slave threw it.

Public latrines offered a communal alternative, but not a private one. Users sat side by side, with no partitions. Water carried waste beneath the seats. A shared sponge on a stick served for cleaning. Even Roman sanitation, so impressive for its time in aqueduct and sewer, could be deeply unpleasant.

Noise completed the urban assault. Metalworkers hammered. Bakers shouted. Carts rumbled at night because daytime traffic restrictions pushed deliveries into the dark. Children played in streets all day long. Neighbors argued through thin walls.

If Rome sounds like a nightmare, its port of Ostia was at least a bit more temperate.

At Rome’s port, many insulae survive well enough to show how sophisticated Roman apartment living could become. Brick-faced concrete walls still rise at some sites. Some buildings had names, almost like prestige apartment blocks today.

The House of Diana (named after the ancient Roman goddess of the hunt, the moon, and nature), one of Ostia’s best-known apartment buildings, shows the strength of the Roman urban model. It stood close to the forum, near the main street, and across from food shops. A resident could work, eat, worship, bathe, shop, and socialize within a short walk. Its residents, however, were probably upper class compared to those who languished in less affluent neighbourhoods.

So, not all Roman insulae were worthy of pity. The insula helped create a dense, mixed-use city long before zoning codes and transit-oriented development. Insulae put homes above shops. They supported street life. And they gave ordinary people access to the city’s opportunities, even when those opportunities came wrapped in smoke, exorbitant rent, and all sorts of risks.

However, Ostia’s better-preserved buildings may skew our view because the shoddiest structures disappeared first. Archaeology often preserves the stronger bones of the past.

Even so, Ostia shows that Rome’s apartment experiment had range.

Rome pioneered mass housing because it had no choice.

A huge population pressed against finite land. Transport moved by foot, cart, animal, river, and sea. Workers needed to live near work. Shops needed customers. The state needed to feed, police, count, and control a vast urban population.

The solution was vertical, which proved profitable, dangerous, and perhaps ahead of its time.

The Romans did not solve urban housing. No civilization has. But they saw, earlier than almost anyone, that a great city cannot survive on individual housing.

For Ancarenus Nothus, eternal lodging finally came rent-free. For the billions who now live after him, the price of the city life is paid remarkably the same: one climb, one coin, one sleepless night at a time.

Now, because it’s highly unlikely any one household will ever own more than a handful of mini Bluetooth speakers, this is one of those consumer-tech statements that rarely gets tested. So we asked the good folks at Älmhult in Ikea headquarters if they’d let us do just this. To our surprise, Ikea not only agreed, but it also upped the ante, saying it might be possible to link up to 150 without breaking the connection, so that’s exactly how many it sent us. “No one has ever tried this, though,” an Ikea spokesperson said. “We haven’t.”

That is how we ended up building a literal wall of sound at our London office, stacking 150 Kallsups carefully on top of one another to test how many would chain-play from a smartphone. Sadly, one casualty during shipping from Sweden—a single Kallsup—despite lighting up correctly and looking ready to chain, made an ominous rattling sound as if its innards weren’t entirely secure. Sure enough, it refused to play ball (or anything else), so we ended up with 149 speakers.

The chain process itself is simple. Once you have paired a single Kallsup with your music device—in our case, an iPhone 16 Pro—and it’s up and running, simply power on another cube while playing and hold down the Play button until a chime tells you it’s in “multi-speaker” mode. It should then immediately start playing whatever the Bluetooth-connected Kallsup is playing.

Thanks to this near-effortless chaining process, linking up all these Kallsups took much less time than we'd expected, about 30 minutes. We stopped at 100 to test how loud the “system” sounded at that stage before resuming the build and moving on to the maximum amount. As the music continued while we chained the speakers, it would be obvious when the collective refused to add more cubes. But this didn’t happen, and the plucky Kallsups joined forces without grumbling right up to the full 149.

It’s tempting to preface the next few paragraphs with the phrase “for a $10 speaker,” because, despite Ikea’s vast buying power, experienced research and development team, and global presence, the ability to produce a tiny speaker that doesn’t sound absolutely horrible for less than a pint of half-decent craft beer is pretty incredible.

Each Kallsup has a single 1.8-inch, full-range driver powered by a 3-watt amplifier, with Bluetooth 5.3 support. No separate tweeter or woofer, just the one tiny driver.

On its own, the Kallsup speaker sounds fine. Yes, it lacks anything resembling the deep rumbling bass people talk about in audio reviews, but did we mention it’s a $10 speaker? It is a bit tinny, and you won’t want to push the volume very hard, but as a little desktop speaker, one by the side of the bed, or to throw in a suitcase, the Kallsup does the job. It doesn’t match the performance of one of our best Bluetooth speaker recommendations, but keep the cost in mind, and it’s hard to be disappointed.

Even at low background volumes, the Kallsups worked surprisingly well together, especially playing acoustic tracks. As we paired more speakers, the sound gradually grew nicely in scale. Nothing was overpowering or dominating, just the cumulative effect of all those tiny drivers moving small amounts of air.

With 149 individual 1.8-inch drivers, the combined surface area is roughly equivalent to one enormous 22-inch driver. Each Kallsup also has its own 3-watt amplifier, giving our wall a combined rated output of 447 watts. That doesn’t mean it behaves like a giant subwoofer or a conventional 447-watt speaker, but it explains why things got so loud.

But before we turned these up, if you will, to 149, we listened at 100, and when we pushed the volume, the collective Kallsups were seriously loud. But for the record, loud like a fire alarm, not a hi-fi system. With the wall complete, we worried the collective vibrations might send the whole thing crashing down. Disappointingly for the socials, it stayed resolute. Instead, we played Trans Lunar by Twin Dimensions (WIRED director of consumer tech and culture Mike Calore’s excellent band) and slowly increased the volume to maximum.

We almost wish we hadn’t. The noise was brain-numbingly loud as we blasted, somewhat appropriately, Richard Strauss’ Also Sprach Zarathustra. We couldn’t hear each other speak, and we could feel our internal organs vibrating. Any sense of fidelity disappeared, but—all power to the Kallsups—it worked. We’d successfully linked 149 Bluetooth speakers, though sadly we’re well short of the world record of 1,500 speakers set by Majority Audio in March 2026.

At more modest, realistic volumes, everything sounded considerably more composed, even if the music lacked levels of depth and detail. The overall lack of clarity could also be due to minor timing differences between the individual drivers.

At $1,490, a wall of Kallsups is quite an investment, and not one we’re recommending, but it is extremely impressive that we managed to sync and play music wirelessly through so many small, cheap speakers.

We also tested the wall alongside WIRED’s choice for best overall party speaker, the $500 LG Xboom Stage 501. Cranked up, both reached similarly ear-splitting, frankly uncomfortable volumes. But with two 5.25-inch woofers, two 2.5-inch full-range drivers, and clever digital signal processing, the LG delivered understandably fuller, deeper bass and a considerably more musical experience, as expected.

What’s remarkable about this experiment is that so little went wrong. Only one of the 150 speakers shipped didn’t work, clearly broken in transit. We managed to get all 149 sound cubes chained easily and quickly. And when we weren’t blasting the wall of sound at max volume, instead keeping levels moderate, the speakers sounded surprisingly competent as a collective.

However, there were hints of the Kallsup chaining capacity reaching its limit, as Ikea suspected. As we got beyond 100 units linked together, the incidence of failing to chain on the first try increased. Still, no speaker refused more than twice as we got toward the end of the build.

We also would have preferred indentations on the tops of the speakers, which would have given us a bit more confidence that the whole stack wasn’t going to collapse in a heap as volume ramped up (which it didn’t). Such a simple design feature would help whether you’re dealing with just a couple placed on top of each other, let alone 149—but this is splitting hairs.

Lastly, although this setup was impressively loud, no one in their right mind would do this in their own home. Ikea has been a good sport letting us try this out, but the ultimate, unsurprising lesson is: If you want high-quality audio, spend your money on a better speaker. As for the Kallsup? Simple to use, easy to chain, with approachable sound and clean design. You really can’t ask much more for $10.

That complicates Washington's pressure campaign: The blockade may deprive Tehran of one of its biggest sources of cash without immediately starving the military and Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps of the money they need to operate.

Oil directly accounts for roughly 30% of Iran's government budget, according to Gregory Brew, a senior Iran and energy analyst at Eurasia Group, and Miad Maleki, an Iran sanctions and financial expert at the Foundation for Defense of Democracies.

Iran entered the year expecting to export about 1.7 million barrels per day at roughly $54 per barrel, Brew said. Actual exports have fallen short of that target, though higher oil prices allowed Tehran to build some financial cushion.

Maleki estimated that oil and refined petroleum products account for between 50% and 60% of regime revenue when indirect income is included.

Still, the December deadline is not a date on which Iran suddenly runs out of money.

"The December timeline is more of a cash flow kind of disruption, and not really a collapse state," Maleki told Fox News Digital.

Tehran has so far allowed inflation and the weakening rial to absorb some of the pressure rather than abruptly stopping government salaries or allowing widespread shortages to develop.

"The purchasing power of Iranians incrementally declines," Maleki said.

Losing a revenue stream that covers roughly 30% of the government budget would nevertheless force increasingly difficult choices. Tehran can cut spending, borrow more or run larger deficits. Deeper cuts could hit subsidies, pensions and other government programs, while deficit spending risks fueling already high inflation.

"There's already expectations that government spending next year is going to be greatly reduced," Brew said . "A bigger chunk of the budget is likely going to be met through borrowing and deficit spending, which is of course going to add to inflation."

Military spending, however, could prove more resistant.

"There is a broad expectation that non-military spending is going to be slashed significantly, while military spending is going to be maintained and might even increase because of the overall war footing that the Iranian regime has been put on," Brew said.

Iran is already spending resources to rebuild missile production facilities and other military infrastructure, expand underground facilities and repair damage to its nuclear program, Brew said.

Iran's armed forces also have a direct connection to the country's oil trade.

The Treasury Department said last year that Tehran was allocating roughly 200,000 barrels of crude per day to its armed forces, with the value of planned allocations expected to exceed $10 billion annually. Military organizations can sell allocated oil abroad and use the proceeds themselves.

Maleki said those sales have helped finance equipment, chemicals and weapons components and support Iran-backed groups including Hezbollah and Hamas.

The IRGC also draws revenue from smuggling, black-market commerce and sanctions-evasion networks outside the formal state budget. Brew said its economic influence has expanded as sanctions have squeezed Iran's private economy.

"If the IRGC is the single most important institution in the country, and if it largely benefits from sanctions and blockades and various forms of U.S. economic pressure, then there's a world in which it benefits from this new environment rather than being squeezed like the overall government budget," Brew said.

Iran's oil squeeze has been months in the making.

Tehran held roughly 135 million barrels of crude in floating storage before the war began in February, providing a stockpile it could continue selling after U.S. forces restricted new exports. That offshore inventory has now been exhausted.

U.S. Central Command estimates the blockade has already denied Tehran at least $25 billion in oil revenue.

Buyers of Iranian crude typically settle payments one to two months after delivery, creating the lag between the disappearance of Iran's floating inventory and Kpler's projected revenue cutoff in December.

Even if that projection broadly holds, however, Iran is unlikely to stop earning money from hydrocarbons altogether.

Smaller quantities of oil can still move overland by truck and rail and across the Caspian Sea, while refined petroleum and petrochemical products can travel through routes outside the seaborne crude trade targeted by the blockade.

"While the amount of Iranian oil that can be tracked has fallen to zero, it's unlikely Iran's oil exports completely fall to nothing," Brew said. "The amount will likely be fairly small, but it won't be completely zeroed."

Both experts said an agreement with Washington allowing Iranian tankers to resume leaving the Persian Gulf could reverse the trajectory . Without a deal or relaxation of the blockade, the financial pressure is expected to build into next year.

The bigger test may arrive in March, when Iran begins its new fiscal year and must reconcile its spending plans with dramatically lower oil income.

"There's going to be a fiscal squeeze, and there's going to be pressure around the release of the new budget," Brew said. "There's going to be economic unrest, potentially even economic turmoil as the government rolls out spending cuts."

By then, Tehran could have gone months without its traditional seaborne crude income, forcing the regime to decide how much of the financial squeeze it is willing to pass on to its population while preserving spending elsewhere.

#4055. Why do dead bodies move and make noise?

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Published Oct 25, 2026 7:54 AM EDT

Death is supposed to be the end of the road. The lights are out, the curtain has fallen. No breathing, no heartbeat—end of discussion, right? Not quite.

In some cases, corpses twitch, flex, shift, change positions, or even make noises that could easily be mistaken for messages from beyond the grave. But before you call the Ghostbusters, take a deep breath. The explanation is less zombie apocalypse and more basic biology.

Even after the heart stops beating, the body still has a few tricks left up its sleeve. The results can be creepy, sure, but experts say they’re perfectly natural. Here’s why corpses sometimes move and make noise.

Our bodies can continue moving long after death. When researchers in Australia used time-lapse cameras to keep tabs on a decomposing human body, they discovered that it continued to shift for at least 16 months, with some parts moving as much as 20 total inches over the course of the study period.

Researchers call this “post-mortem movement”—and it’s a lot less dramatic than you might imagine.

“A body can move, but not necessarily in the way that I think most people envision,” Derek Boyd, a biological anthropologist who co-directs the Forensic Anthropology and Bioarchaeology Laboratory at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas, tells Popular Science.

“The movements are very subtle—you’re not going to see a body sit up or turn its head.”

After death, the human body moves through a pretty predictable series of decomposition stages. One of the earliest is rigor mortis, when the muscles stiffen and the body becomes rigid. As this process unfolds, a corpse may shift, creating the appearance of movement—but it’s easy to miss, Boyd says.

“You’re not necessarily going to see [the movement] unless you’re laser-focused on the body for the first 48 hours—and even then it’s just going to look like tension, just the body stiffening,” he says.

The next stages in which movement might occur are boat and post-bloat, says Boyd. When someone dies, the body’s own enzymes begin breaking down its cells, a process known as autolysis.

Meanwhile, bacteria and microbes start spreading throughout the body, digesting tissues and producing gases. This process, known as putrefaction, can “cause the body to become distended or start to bloat, and that bloating can result in a dramatic expansion,” he says. “The body inflates in various places. But, again, this is something that happens gradually.”

Eventually, those gases escape—and the body gradually deflates, sometimes causing small shifts in its position.

Finally, in the advanced stages of decomposition, the body’s soft tissues begin to dry out, a process known as desiccation. “We have ligaments that are attaching bones together, tendons connecting muscle to bone, and in these later stages of decomposition, those connective tissues can shrink and tighten—and, in doing so, they can pull on our skeleton in various ways,” he says.

Of course, not all postmortem movement comes from decomposition itself. Outside forces can also cause a body to shift or change position. Scavengers may drag or disturb remains while feeding, for example, while insects can move small body parts or contribute to the breakdown of tissues. Environmental factors, such as wind, flowing water, or shifting soil, can also displace remains over time.

While we’re alive, our bodies are constantly making noise—burps, farts, stomach gurgles, and growls, creaks, and cracks. Considering how noisy our living bodies can be, it shouldn’t come as much of a surprise that they can continue making noises after death.

As gases build up during putrefaction, for instance, the body can produce gurgles, bubbles, groans, or even noises that sound like sighs, Sarah Chavez, executive director of the Order of the Good Death, a nonprofit that aims to change how people think about death and make end-of-life care and funeral options more accessible and equitable, tells Popular Science. Fluids and gases can also get pushed through the mouth or nose, which can sometimes create surprising sounds, she says.

“It can be startling if you’re not expecting it, but there’s nothing supernatural happening,” Chavez adds. “The body is just continuing to do what bodies do—break things down, move fluids and gases around, and occasionally make unintentional noises.”

Ultimately, the fact that bodies can move or make noises after death is a reminder that the human body is “a beautiful ecosystem of its own,” says Boyd.

In life, our bodies are home to countless microorganisms that interact with one another and help keep us alive. After death, those same microorganisms continue to play a role in the body’s breakdown, creating the conditions that can cause a twitch, a shift in position, or an unexpected sound.

The body no longer has thoughts, feelings, or aspirations, but it remains a complex biological system in its own right. “We are walking ecosystems, and we remain ecosystems even after death,” says Boyd.

And scientists are still learning what these postmortem ecosystems can tell us. For example, Boyd says researchers are exploring whether changes to the body’s microbial communities could help forensic investigators estimate how much time has passed since someone died—a “microbial clock” of sorts. In other words, the very same processes that can make a corpse move or make noises may also offer valuable clues about the biology of death itself.
