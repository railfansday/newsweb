#3300. Packers’ simple pressure disguise creates chaos for the Cowboys

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

In the last article, we broke down Evan Williams' interception against the Dallas Cowboys. That play set the tone, but it was only the opening chapter of a long night for Dak Prescott. By halftime, Green Bay led 17-0, and Prescott had been picked off twice.

The second interception wasn't a product of disguise so much as bad luck. Dallas was driving inside the Green Bay 30 when CeeDee Lamb lost his footing on a route, leaving the ball for rookie Brandon Cisse, who came away with the first interception of his career.

Still, it fit the larger pattern. The Cowboys could move the ball in spurts, but every time they got close, something went wrong. And Gannon kept the pressure on in the second half, and the same theme kept showing up on tape: the look before the snap rarely matched what happened after it.

From the above pressure package disguise, he shifted the defense into at least four different coverage rotations that the Cowboys were unable to decipher. Each of those above presents the same or similar pre-snap alignments. And each one has a different rotation behind and different zone droppers.

After halftime, Gannon went back to what was working. On a third-and-7 in the third quarter, Green Bay lined up six defenders across the line, and Dallas set its protection to account for that front. Then Williams started walking down late in the play clock.

Prescott saw it with roughly three seconds left, too late to reset the protection. The math was simple and bad for Dallas: four rushers coming off the weak side, three blockers to meet them. Prescott knew the ball had to come out immediately.

It never had a chance to. The rush got home almost at once, and the quick throws Prescott was counting on weren't there either. Behind the pressure, Green Bay dropped three players into the short zones, one of them traveling all the way across from the other side of the formation to plug a throwing lane.

Pressure Dallas couldn't block plus coverage it didn't expect left Prescott with nothing, and the Cowboys punted. Few third-down calls are cleaner. The defense had the play beaten at the snap.

Gannon's creativity showed up on a drive in the fourth quarter. With Green Bay up 20-7 and Dallas needing to stay within striking distance, he called three straight plays that looked identical before the snap and played three different coverages after it. The sequence ended with the Cowboys running a fourth-down play that had little chance to succeed.

It began on 2nd-and-7. The picture was a cover-0 pressure look: a loaded front, with four defenders in off coverage but sitting inside and off anywhere from press coverage to 10 yards. Green Bay had already brought real zero blitzes from that alignment earlier in the night, so Dallas had to respect it every time.

On 2nd-and-7, the Packers showed it and then backed out into cover-4. The Cowboys had nothing designed to attack quarters and settled for 5 yards, making it 3rd-and-2.

On third down, the look was identical, but this time the blitz was real. Green Bay sent everyone with no help behind it and got a rusher through untouched. Prescott was forced to throw before he could set, and the pass sailed incomplete.

Once again the heat arrived so quickly that Dak had almost no time to deliver an accurate ball, and the pass fell incomplete.

That set up 4th-and-2, and Dallas tried to take the blitz out of the equation by rolling Prescott out to his left. It's a sensible answer to a zero blitz, because it moves the launch point away from free rushers. But Gannon had a counter.

Green Bay didn't rush extra at all and sat in cover-3. A rollout only gives the quarterback half the field, so once the first option to the left was covered, Prescott had nowhere else to look. He tried to force it, and the underneath defender knocked it away. Dallas called a play built to beat pressure, and Green Bay didn't send any.

Instead, the Packers dropped into cover-3. The downside of a sprint-out is that it eliminates half the field, and if the first read on a throw to the left isn't there, there's very little left to work with. Prescott tried to squeeze the ball in quickly, knowing his options were limited, and the underneath defender broke it up.

Had Dallas known before the snap that cover-3 was coming, it almost certainly wouldn't have called that play. That's the true value of disguise. It keeps an offense from getting into its best call against what the defense is actually running. Green Bay added another field goal early in the fourth quarter to take a 23-7 lead not long after.

The fourth coverage came on the very next Dallas drive. Same alignment, but this time the Packers rotated into cover-2. The corner at the top of the screen squatted on the short route because he knew he had a safety over the top.

Prescott was a beat late recognizing it and nearly threw his third interception of the night. A hitch to the outside is about the worst throw you can make against a cover-2 corner, and it's a throw Prescott likely never attempts if he reads the coverage before the snap.

Prescott didn't diagnose it fast enough and nearly threw a third interception. If he had known cover-2 was coming, he wouldn't have thrown an outside hitch into a cloud corner, and he might have checked to a different play at the line.

Dallas eventually got on the board late, with Prescott finding his receiver for a 6-yard touchdown pass with 44 seconds remaining, but a two-point try to Lamb fell incomplete and Green Bay recovered the onside kick.

The final box score, with Prescott completing 34 passes for 258 yards, one touchdown and two interceptions, doesn't capture how often the Cowboys were out of sorts. They ran too many non-competitive plays in critical moments, and that was the difference in the game. The loss dropped Dallas to 2-4, while Green Bay improved to 4-2.

The Packers' defense is rolling, and it's doing so without Micah Parsons, who still hasn't returned. The next test is a big one: Green Bay visits the NFC North rival Detroit Lions and a dangerous offense in Week 7.

#3299. Warriors rookie Yaxel Lendeborg says he 'lost all respect' for Luka Dončić in NBA debut: 'I told him he sucks'

There are a handful of players in the NBA that most veterans know not to trash-talk. Luka Dončić falls into that bucket. When someone can drop 30 on you in their sleep, the last thing you need is to light a fire under them during a game.

Of course, Yaxel Lendeborg is no veteran. The Warriors' rookie made his NBA debut on Wednesday, and he spent a good chunk of it guarding Dončić, one of the best players in the NBA. To his credit, he held up reasonably well. Dončić shot just 7-of-18 from the floor thanks in large part to Lendeborg's defense. But the two jawed at one another quite a bit, especially at the end of the second quarter, when Lendeborg picked up a technical foul. Moments later, Dončić hit a half-court buzzer-beater and pointed directly at the rookie.

Dončić, who scored a game-high 28 points, ultimately won the war. Once Stephen Curry cooled off after a blistering first quarter, the Los Angeles Lakers destroyed the Golden State Warriors, 127-105. Yet Lendeborg left the game thoroughly unimpressed with the MVP candidate he covered. In fact, he said he "lost all respect" for Dončić in the defeat.

"Being in there first hand, just watching all the whining and complaining, I lost all respect, man," Lendeborg said. "That was the awful. Like, me getting frustrated, it's not about like, the foul getting called. It's about him complaining the entire game. Then you get the foul call, congratulations bro, and then you talk to me like you did something great, bro? Come on, dog. That shit weak."

When asked what exactly he said to Dončić, Lendeborg did not mince words. "I told him he sucks," the rookie explained. "He can't score on me." Lendeborg committed four personal fouls in the game, three of which sent Dončić to the line. Those fouls, according to Steve Kerr, are a potential learning experience. "He had probably three fouls that probably wouldn't be fouls in any other league in the world," Kerr grumbled. "But it's the NBA. He's gotta learn this stuff."

Veteran agitator Draymond Green ultimately supported his rookie for going at the superstar. "Everyone will say the rookie learned his lesson. What he learn?" Green asked. "The next time he see him, go at his ass again." Green, across 15 NBA seasons now, has gone at just about everyone regardless of status. It seems as though Lendeborg will follow in his footsteps.

As for Dončić? He appreciated the trash talk. "Everybody knows when somebody starts talking, you get a different me," he said after the game. "So that was it." Dončić's reputation for complaining about calls is well known, and it's not exactly uncommon in the NBA. Kerr acknowledged how different the whistle is in the NBA compared to any other league Lendeborg has experienced, but on that note, how players react to whistles differs as well. NBA players, especially stars, complain to officials constantly in part because they think it works.

Dončić led the NBA in free-throw attempts per game last season, so clearly, his approach is succeeding. He won't be the only star who tries to use the whistle as a weapon against Lendeborg, and if Lendeborg is going to grow into the sort of defensive stopper the Warriors hope he can become, learning how to work within the confines of the NBA whistle, and all of the talk that surrounds it, will be essential. Dončić may have complained on Wednesday... but he also won. That's the ultimate goal, and Lendeborg will have to learn how to navigate some of those less savory tactics as he adjusts to life in the NBA.

#3290. NBA one-game overreactions: The Warriors need to trade Stephen Curry, Ja Morant is a perfect fit in Portland

We're two nights into the 2026-27 NBA season and all but two teams have crossed their opener off the schedule. Only the Cavs, who are at Philadelphia on Thursday, and the Nuggets, who are at Oklahoma City in the nightcap of a two-game slate, are yet to take the court.

Plenty has happened, and as sure as the sun rises in the East, there are going to be a lot of hot takes in response to season openers. That doesn't mean these takes are wrong, necessarily. Take the Timberwolves, for instance. If you watched them dismantle what could be a top-five defense in Miami on Wednesday, you might be calling them the best offense in basketball. Maybe even a historic one. And you know what? You might be right!

Because, good grief, did Anthony Edwards and LaMelo Ball come out blazing. I don't know if the Wolves, who shot 50 3-pointers in Miami and connected at a 42% clip, are going to be a historic offense or even the tops in the league, but it's not a hot take to call it a possibility. They are loaded with athletes, shooters and creators -- and they know it. This team is going be a blast to watch all season.

You know who's not going to be a blast to watch? The Warriors. Which is where we shall begin out Game 1 overreactions. Get your oven mitts on for these hot takes.

We're going to shelve the fact that Curry doesn't seem to have any desire to be traded, and the Warriors would never have the stones to do it without his request, and just look at this from an entertainment standpoint, which, in the end, is what the NBA is about. Watching the Warriors, who got shelled by the Lakers in the season opener, play basketball this season is going to be worse than bad. It's going to be boring.

That word should never be used in a Steph Curry sentence, but here we are. The greatest showman, shooter and one of the greatest players in history showed us he's still got it with 16 first-quarter points against the Lakers on Wednesday, and then the Warriors proceeded to get smashed by the Lakers because they're small, slow, old, and just not very good. It's only because of Curry that we can even call this a potential Play-in Tournament team with a halfway straight face.

The Warriors -- starting with Joe Lacob and continuing down the front-office line -- have paralyzed themselves with all their next-era analysis, so why don't they just get on with it. If they really want the best future possible, then trading Curry, who at 38 going on 39 would still command pretty premium value, is their best chip. They could set up their next decade if they did it right, and just like everything else good that has happened to this franchise over the last decade-plus, Curry would be the reason for it. He would, in a weird way, be even more heroic to the Warriors. He gave them the greatest run they could have ever dreamed of and then set their table for the next one.

If I were Curry, which I realize I'm not and that he has earned the right to let his basketball life die on the Golden State vine if he so chooses, I would have a hard time resisting the temptation to compete for one last title. I know he has a family and the Bay Area is his home and he's a Warrior for life and all that, and I surely know that there's a lot more layers to this than just competing for another ring. But I go back to the entertainment aspect of the NBA and how important it is -- even more so, frankly, than winning. It's why these guys gets paid what they do. It why we pay what we do to watch them.

In the end, it's not just that Curry is still way too great to be wasting away on an irrelevant team; it's that he's too entertaining. This guy is still box office, and his going to a team with a real shot at a title would light the league on fire. I'm from Northern California. I say this as a lifelong Warriors fan. Golden State needs to trade Stephen Curry. Their run is over. But his isn't.

#3287. Three reasons why the Rays can finally win their first World Series title

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

The Tampa Bay Rays, even though they won a robust 98 games during the regular season and bested the New York Yankees in the American League East race, are going to be underdogs in the World Series. That's because they're running up against the Milwaukee Brewers, who during the regular season won more games than the Rays and put up a much better run differential, all against a tougher schedule.

"Underdog," however, does not mean "fated to lose," especially in a sport like baseball and just as especially when the Rays are such a strong team in their own rights. Any team is capable of beating any other in this sport, and in this instance, a Rays series win would be barely a mild upset. Now, it's hard to say the two teams are perfectly evenly matched, but they're not far from it, really. On that note, let's have a quick look at why the Rays may indeed be poised to win the World Series that lurks before us.

Yes, the Brewers have the one-two punch of Jacob Misiorowski, the NL Cy Young favorite, and rookie standout Logan Henderson, but the Rays boast Drew Rasmussen and deadline addition (and ALCS MVP) Freddy Peralta, the former Brewer stalwart. On paper, you'd lean Brewers on this front, but consider these rotation fronts have fared in the postseason thus far:

By no means has the Milwaukee tandem been bad, at least on the run-prevention front, but Rasmussen and Peralta have been much better in these playoffs. Of particular concern is Misiorowski's and Henderson's struggles at the command-and-control level, as measured by K/BB ratio.

Yes, this may be the small sample size at work, but it's also possible that the much younger Brewers rotation front is hitting a workload wall. Last season, Misiorowski – minors, majors, and playoffs combined – worked 141 ⅓ innings. In 2026, his regular-season and postseason innings tally is up to 183. That's a big jump, especially for a guy who's thrown, oh, almost 1,200 triple-digit fastballs on the year. He hasn't had a true ace-level start in more than a month. As for Henderson, he's also seen a sizable innings jump – from 103 innings, Triple-A and the majors combined, in 2025 to 131 ⅓ this season, Triple-A, majors, and postseason combined. None of these are irresponsible leaps forward or anything like that, but turning a six-month season into a seven-month season can be a challenge for any pitcher, especially a young one.

On the other side, Rasmussen and Peralta are older and, especially in Peralta's case, familiar with high workloads and the stamina and durability those workloads require. If the series goes long enough, each of these one-two punches will make a total of four starts. As such, this potential edge for Tampa Bay could be a real difference-maker. On another level, the presence of Nick Martinez in the Rays rotation could be another possible edge once Game 3 rolls around.

The Rays made some key additions leading up to this year's trade deadline, and those additions raised the ceiling for this team. Peralta's recent merits are noted above, and after being acquired from the Mets, he made some adjustments and registered a 131 ERA+ in 10 stretch-drive starts. He's been even better than that lately.

Acquiring Liam Hicks from the Marlins – a fairly bold maneuver, since contenders typically don't like to switch primary catchers in the middle of the season – also paid off. The underrated Hicks batted .288/.379/.440 for Tampa Bay after the trade and thus drastically upgraded the position. He was Kevin Cash's No. 3 hitter in the clincher over Cleveland. Elsewhere, right-handed reliever Tyler Wells has improved the Rays' setup depth – a pronounced need leading up to the deadline.

In at least somewhat related matters, the Rays since the deadline are 40-20, counting the postseason to date.

Sometimes in a postseason series, one player is capable of taking over, and Rays third baseman Junior Caminero is the leading threat to do just that. During the regular season, Caminero – in his age-22 campaign – played in every game, put up an OPS+ of 144, and smacked 42 homers. In 2025, he hit 45 homers. Backing it up is some of the best quality-of-contact indicators in all of baseball. He's an elite hitter, and no one in this series is more likely than he is to take over a game, and perhaps a series, with his bat-work.

Trevor Lawrence's blind side protector, Anton Harrison, is done for the rest of the season due to a torn ACL. Harrison underwent arthroscopic surgery on Thursday, but during the surgery, doctors discovered he had torn his ACL as well, according to ESPN.

Harrison, 24, hurt his knee in Week 5 during Jacksonville's eventual win over the Eagles in London and was carted off. The Jaguars, who also played in London in Week 6, placed Harrison on IR but were hopeful he'd be able to return at some point this season. Then Thursday's surgery revealed the full scope of the injury.

The No. 27 overall pick in the 2023 NFL Draft, Harrison moved from right tackle to left tackle this offseason. It had been a bit of an up-and-down year for him in his new spot -- he allowed three sacks and 12 pressures in just five games -- but it's a significant loss for Jacksonville nonetheless.

Veteran swing Walker Little took over for Harrison in Week 5 and started in his stead in Week 6 against the Texans. The good news for Jacksonville is that Little has significant experience (40 career starts) and performed ably -- no sacks, two pressures allowed -- in Week 6 against a ferocious Houston front.

The bad news, outside the obvious bad news that comes with losing a left tackle, is this leaves Jacksonville paper-thin at tackle. Little was the only tackle on the roster outside Harrison and starting right tackle Cole Van Lanen. The Jaguars do have veteran offensive lineman Geron Christian Sr. on the practice squad but could look to make moves ahead of the Nov. 10 trade deadline.

A second-round pick out of Stanford in 2021, Little has spent his entire career with the Jaguars, and he started at least three games every year from 2021-25. Last year, he started 14 games, including 12 at left tackle. Overall, he allowed 2.9 pressures per game, the second-highest rate among offensive linemen, only behind Tennessee's Dan Moore Jr., and nine sacks, tied for third-most. He also committed seven penalties. By the end of the season, the Jaguars had moved him to right guard and moved Van Lanen to left tackle.

But coach Liam Coen said last week, "I have a lot of confidence" in Little.

"I probably can't overstate how important it is because we really are fortunate to not have to play a backup in a situation when a tackle goes down," Coen said a day later. "I don't look at Walker as a backup tackle or a backup guard."

This -- plus calling up someone from the practice squad -- seems to be the clear solution for now, but if Little struggles, the Jaguars could pursue other options.

Van Lanen impressed moving over to left tackle late last season, so much so that he got a three-year, $51 million extension. Should Coen and his staff deem it worth doing again, they already have proof of concept.

The issue, though, is that it would create a hole at right tackle, where Van Lanen has worked the past several months. Little stepped in for some snaps at right tackle in two games this season, but both times were at the end of blowouts. He has never played meaningful right tackle snaps in his NFL career, though the Jaguars clearly trusted him to do so, considering he was their swing tackle prior to Harrison's injury.

Unfortunately for Jacksonville, it's not like capable left tackles are a dime a dozen. However, there are some options potentially available before the Nov. 10 trade deadline.

The Commanders and the Eagles are two potential sellers in the lowly NFC East. The Eagles have already traded away Cam Jurgens amid a nightmarish season for the entire organization. Last year, the Jaguars traded Fred Johnson to Philadelphia for a 2026 seventh-round pick. Might they be interested in a reunion?

Andrew Wylie is another name that could be of interest. He has not played meaningful snaps at left tackle, but he has played a ton at right tackle, right guard and left guard. If anything, he could be the Jaguars' swing tackle, the role Little had before Harrison's injury. Wylie has played sparingly this season, though it was at left tackle.

Both Washington and Philadelphia are still in the division race (somehow), but if either decides to sell and Jacksonville decides to buy, both options could make sense. GM James Gladstone made a deal for Jakobi Meyers last year at the deadline and a blockbuster for Travis Hunter during last year's draft. If there's anyone who will go hard after making his team better, it's him.
