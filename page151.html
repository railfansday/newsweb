#657. ‘This Is Not a Drill’: Critical PHP Vulnerabilities Are Setting the Internet on Fire

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Internet security and web administration teams around the world are scrambling to prevent widespread disaster. On Wednesday, the developers behind the much-used PHP programming language released emergency security updates designed to fix critical flaws that could be exploited to allow attackers to gain dangerous remote control of victim systems.

The PHP Foundation detailed the two critical vulnerabilities—CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619—which impact “all PHP installations serving web requests” going back to PHP version 5, released in July 2004. However, only PHP 8.2 and newer are still supported and will receive patches, according to the alert. The PHP Foundation noted, though, that some PHP distributions and third-party vendors still produce legacy fixes. The caveat is significant because ubiquitous and foundational web layers like PHP can persist forgotten and un-updated in historic internet infrastructure.

“There are no complete mitigations, updating is the only safe choice,” the PHP Foundation wrote in its advisories. Security experts warn of the potential dire consequences of overlooking unpatched systems.

“This is not a drill,” says Lukasz Olejnik, an independent security consultant and visiting senior research fellow at King’s College London. Since its development in the 1990s, PHP has evolved into an essential scripting language for web development and is used across vast swaths of the internet in websites and software. Everything from publishing systems like WordPress and Drupal to e-commerce platforms like Adobe’s Magento are built on PHP.

Olejnik likens the widespread use of the programming language to critical infrastructure.

“The impact is enormous,” he says of PHP’s security flaws, adding that millions of systems could be impacted. “Especially if attackers would turn to exploiting these vulnerabilities fast. Worst case is wormable malware affecting big chunks of the internet.”

The overriding fear for security teams and developers is that malicious hackers could quickly jump upon the vulnerabilities and begin exploiting deployments before they are fixed. Internet infrastructure company Cloudflare reported it was “seeing early attacks emerging” before the PHP Foundation had detailed the vulnerabilities and released fixes. “We expect mass attacks to start within 15 minutes of the release,” security firm Sansec wrote ahead of the fixes, which were released just before 10 am ET on Wednesday.

“Most of the web runs on PHP and this bug is in a file upload interface that’s like a front door with no lock,” says longtime security and compliance consultant Davi Ottenheimer. “An attacker does not need to know which site they are hitting or even the actual software it runs. They send one crafted upload to any server they can find, and that is the entire attack.”

Ahead of the updated versions of PHP becoming available, some organizations took their websites offline as a precautionary measure. Multiple sites belonging to the Finnish government, including the National Land Survey, were inaccessible, according to reports. French government websites and the Port of Rotterdam’s website were also offline, according to Reuters.

“Everybody should patch immediately, exploitation may happen very fast and at a scale,” says Olejnik. “AI makes exploiting such vulnerabilities of immediate importance. Like, indeed, a minute or so.”

Security company Wiz, which is owned by Google, claimed its researchers discovered the vulnerabilities in PHP partially using AI tools, while OpenAI and security firm Calif also say they independently discovered the issues and worked together. Dubbing an exploitation demonstration php2shell , the Wiz researchers write that in some cases the vulnerabilities can be exploited “fairly easily.”

“The vulnerabilities can allow unauthenticated attackers to execute code on affected servers or read sensitive process memory,” Wiz writes in its research. “That memory may contain passwords, authentication tokens, API keys and certificates belonging to other requests or the server itself.”

Details of the upcoming vulnerabilities first appeared from the maintainers behind PHP on October 20. In a short post on the PHP website, the PHP Foundation wrote it was giving “advance notice” of the security flaws and upcoming release of the fixes. While omitting specific details about the vulnerabilities or the versions impacted, it said both flaws are considered to have a “critical” 9.2 score out of 10 on the Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS).

“We reserve the right to release earlier should details of either vulnerability become public, or should evidence of active exploitation appear in the wild,” the security warning on the PHP site said ahead of Wednesday’s release. According to reports from Bleeping Computer and Ars Technica, some details about the vulnerabilities may have leaked before they were due to officially be published.

The early warning from the PHP Foundation may have given some developers, security teams, and companies enough of a heads up to start preparing to update systems using PHP. Ahead of the details being published, warnings from the EU’s Computer Emergency Response Team (EU-CERT) and Australia's cybersecurity agency said IT teams should be prepared to update their systems as soon as possible, with the potential for websites to be taken offline while fixes are applied.

Similarly, the UK’s National Cyber Security Center (NCSC) published an alert saying organizations should “prepare for immediate updating of systems” using PHP. “Where immediate remediation is not feasible, organizations should consider taking the system offline until it can be fully mitigated,” the agency wrote online.

The NCSC advises that organizations and businesses should identify systems running PHP across their tech infrastructure. There should be a particular focus on systems using PHP that are connected to the internet, including monitoring them for any potential exploitation. The next step is applying PHP updates as they are available and monitoring for updates from software companies as they may use PHP within their apps or services.

“If you find signs of compromise, isolate the system, preserve your web server and PHP logs, and report it to your local FBI field office,” Brett Leatherman, the FBI’s cyber assistant director posted online. “Those reports help us identify the actors involved and warn other organizations before they become victims.”

Why Blue Origin may stay a private company for a while longer.

When you purchase through links on our site, we may earn an affiliate commission. Here’s how it works.

Daniel Biedermann is a partner at NewSpace Capital. He contributed this article to Space.com's Expert Voices: Op-Ed & Insights.

Jeff Bezos, the founder and former CEO of Amazon, recently said that Blue Origin, his space technology company, is likely to go public in the coming years. The announcement has (unsurprisingly) attracted considerable attention, not least because Bezos has spent more than two decades financing the company largely from his personal wealth. He has invested billions of dollars in developing rockets, spacecraft and other forms of technology, often with very little prospect of an immediate return. But although Bezos has now raised the possibility of an initial public offering, he has given no firm indication of when one might happen. He may be right to keep his options open.

Bezos founded Blue Origin in 2000. Because he has financed so much of its development by himself, the company has been able to pursue ambitious engineering projects without the financial pressures that publicly listed businesses typically face. But developing reusable rockets, manufacturing spacecraft and building the infrastructure needed for future lunar missions requires extraordinary amounts of capital: These projects can take decades to complete, and even the wealthiest investors must think hard about how best to finance them over the long term. Bezos, then, has good reasons to seek additional sources of investment — particularly as Blue Origin prepares to expand its commercial operations.

An IPO would give Blue Origin the means to raise substantial capital from public investors and reduce its dependence on Bezos's personal wealth. The company could then use that money to expand manufacturing, develop new technology and compete for a greater share of the growing space market. Public ownership would also give existing investors the power to sell some of their holdings and could make it easier for Blue Origin to finance acquisitions. But the company would also have to meet the expectations of shareholders, who would expect to see regular evidence of commercial progress. This, I suspect, is why Bezos wants to establish a stronger business before committing to a listing.

It might have much to prove commercially, but Blue Origin’s technical progress has been remarkable. In January 2025, its New Glenn rocket reached orbit for the first time — a significant achievement for a vehicle built to compete in the heavy-lift launch market. The company has also secured government and commercial contracts and continued developing its lunar capabilities. But it will eventually have to demonstrate that it can launch New Glenn frequently, reliably and at a commercially competitive cost, and it is still some way away from that point.

Some will be asking whether Bezos wants to raise money to accelerate growth or simply to reduce the amount he himself must invest. Such doubts could affect the company's valuation and make it harder to attract investors who share its long-term ambitions. But again, this suggests Bezos is playing the long game. And given the enthusiasm that has surrounded some billionaire-led space ventures, this is sensible. Investors have sometimes attached extraordinary valuations to companies with ambitious plans but limited commercial experience. Space businesses certainly require ambition: engineers will often spend years developing technology before they can sell it. But investors have to distinguish between technical achievement and commercial performance. At the end of the day, they are seeking a strong rate of return.

What’s also worth considering is how Blue Origin's progress could benefit the wider space industry, particularly the specialist companies that supply technology to larger manufacturers and operators. SpaceX has achieved remarkable success in part by developing and manufacturing much of its technology internally. This includes its rockets, spacecraft and satellites. Blue Origin also does something similar, undertaking substantial engineering and manufacturing work itself, but it is not quite so vertically integrated, and thus may have greater scope to work with external suppliers as it expands. It already buys parts, equipment and services from hundreds of businesses. If it can successfully increase its production and launch activity, these suppliers could receive more orders and gain opportunities to develop new technologies. That’s to the good of the sector as a whole.

Why? Because a successful space industry needs more than a handful of large launch providers. It needs companies specializing in satellite communications, propulsion, electronics, software and other areas. Such businesses may have developed highly sophisticated products, but they need bigger customers and predictable demand to expand and produce more. Blue Origin could become an important customer for some of these, particularly if it chooses to buy specialist technology rather than make everything internally. Greater competition between the bigger space companies could translate to more potential customers for suppliers and fewer dependent commercial relationships.

Investors would benefit. There’s real potential in businesses that provide the technology and services on which larger space companies depend — the ‘picks and shovels.’ These can sometimes serve several customers and markets, making them less dependent on the success of a single program. A growing Blue Origin would create more opportunities for companies like these — although, of course, much will depend on how Bezos chooses to organize its supply chain. We shouldn’t assume that Blue Origin will become more open to external suppliers than its competitors, but the possibility is at least worth entertaining.

As it is, Bezos still faces substantial challenges. Blue Origin is not yet a commercially mature business. The company must increase its launch activity; it must demonstrate its reliability; and it must compete for customers in the public and private sectors. It must also decide how much technology to manufacture internally and whether and where third parties can provide better products or products at a lower cost. Bezos has the resources to give the company time to answer these questions, and he appears to be willing to use them. If he continues to prioritize commercial performance before pursuing an IPO, then Blue Origin could eventually enter the public markets in a much stronger position.

Daniel Biedermann is a Partner at NewSpace Capital, a private equity firm investing in high-growth space companies. He has more than 30 years' experience in the space industry and was previously SVP of Corporate Strategy at SES, where he played a leading role in acquisitions and the creation of joint ventures, including LuxGovSat. Before joining SES, he held engineering and mission management roles at Arianespace and Contraves Space, working on more than 40 space and launch missions.

Between the duo of volumes, sections are divided into chapters dealing with topics such as history, children's stories, the American West, everyday life, romance and adventure, photography, science fiction and fantasy, and, of course, Star Wars in Motion.

They take you on an overwhelming journey through the gallery’s incredibly diverse holdings, not least of which are archival items and concept art chronicling Lucas's involvement in the galaxy far, far away.

This remarkable "Star Wars" section will be of particular interest to sci-fi aficionados, as it contains 100 pages of original props, paintings, concept artwork, costumes, models, and digital renderings created by some of Lucasfilm and Industrial Light and Magic’s best and brightest talents.

Taken with the rest of the 832 combined pages of Taschen's ambitious release that fully captures the dizzying breadth of the Lucas Museum of Narrative Art, it's a brilliant keepsake to spark your soul and imagination.

#642. Resignation of Bärbel Bas shines light on harsh era of German politics

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

German Labor Minister Bärbel Bas has stepped down, citing her need to grieve a loved one and leave behind the harsh realities of government. So how did German politics get so heated?

For a brief moment, a sense of quiet sympathy could be felt in Berlin's often unforgiving political arena: On Monday morning, Bärbel Bas, Germany's labor minister and the co-leader of the center-left Social Democrats (SPD) announced her resignation from all government and party positions. The 58-year-old politician cited a personal tragedy as the reason for her decision: "Following the death of my stepdaughter, I have come to realize that I need time to work through my grief," Bas wrote in a statement.

Bas also wrote that sustained criticism from the conservative governing parties, the Christian Democratic Union (CDU) and Christian Social Union (CSU), had contributed to her decision to step down. In her statement, she wrote that she had been forced to "conclude that public attacks on me, unfortunately also from within the ranks of our coalition partner, continued to intensify during this personally difficult time."

The labor minister had recently been responsible for implementing complex changes to Germany's public pension system — including increasing the retirement age — and had been urged by some conservative politicians, in rather harsh terms, not to delay the project any further.

According to a report in the Süddeutsche Zeitung newspaper, one remark in particular appears to have weighed heavily on her: During an off-the-record briefing with journalists, Chancellor Friedrich Merz, of the CDU, reportedly criticized the pace of her efforts despite knowing that she was working through a personal tragedy.

Did Bas feel so overwhelmed by pressures both personal and political that she chose to step down rather than continue to endure them?

Mirco Liefke, a researcher with the Institute for Media and Communication Studies in the Department of Political and Social Sciences at the Freie Universität Berlin, told DW that unfairness and harshness have always been part of politics. However, he added: "What I do think is new is the potential for escalation and the speed that social media have brought to public debate. Along with that has come an incentive for individual actors to adopt ever more radical positions."

There are few public spaces left in which politicians can speak with the expectation that their remarks will not be reported. Merz is said to have made critical remarks of Bas during an off-the-record briefing whose contents were not intended for publication.

Though he was fully aware of her personal circumstances, Merz reportedly said Bas was barely visible in public and failing to put forward proposals of her own.

"It has effectively become a business model to leak information that is meant to remain confidential and thereby generate attention," Liefke said.

A spokesperson for Merz declined to confirm or deny the report.

Felix Banaszak, the co-leader of Germany's center-left Greens, has known Bas fior years, with both hailing from Duisburg, in the state of North Rhine-Westphalia, albeit born a couple of decades apart.

"Toughness and ruthlessness are nothing new in politics," Banaszak told DW. "But we still need to find a different way of doing things if we do not want to keep wearing people down."

Banaszak said the SPD was partially responsible for the treatment of Bas. "The fact that Bärbel Bas encountered so little understanding for her personal situation from within the ranks of the coalition partner, and that apparently no one in the SPD was willing to step forward, defend her, and take her out of the line of fire, is simply not a good sign," he said.

"It would be naive to pretend that the attacks on a man would have unfolded in a similar way," he added.

Liefke offered a similar view: "Women politicians are subjected to an extraordinary degree of misogynistic and sexualized abuse — and they face a level of public exposure that is entirely new."

The debate over how Bas and her grief should have been treated soon took on some of the same characteristics as the attacks that had preceded her resignation. Manuela Schwesig, the SPD state premier of the northeastern state of Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, described the attacks by the CDU/CSU as "inhuman," drawing sharp criticism from many conservative politicians. Merz publicly expressed his regret over the labor minister's resignation and wished her all the best.

Liefke said this was another tremnd in German politics. Once a politician decides to step down, he argues, public expressions of concern are often far more intense than they used to be and come from all sides: "This is a facade of concern," he said. "There is something ritualistic, almost reflexive, about it. People react this way because they are following another major trend: Emotionalization."

The attacks on Bas are just one example of the increasingly harsh tone of political debate. Formal reprimands in the Bundestag have risen sharply, particularly since the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) entered parliament in 2017. High-profile resignations have also made headlines, including that of Kevin Kühnert, the SPD's former secretary-general.

Kühnert stepped down in 2024. In April 2025, however, he told Die Zeit newspaper that he had felt constantly threatened by far-right extremists. Political debate, he said, is showing "an increasing lack of respect for the views of those who think differently."

Liefke said politicians once managed to maintain two distinct modes of communication: one public and one private. "When you think of earlier generations of politicians, what comes to mind is the distance they maintained, the relative lack of emotional display," he said. "Take Helmut Schmidt, for example. He did not openly grieve over things that came crashing down on him. At least, he did not show it publicly." Schmidt, of the SPD, served as chancellor from 1974 to 1982.

"Today, politicians such as Bärbel Bas benefit from drawing on their own life stories when they say, 'I come from a working-class background and worked my way up,'" Liefke said. "That can work to their advantage — but it also makes them far more visible, and far more vulnerable, as individuals."

Bas, who served as president of the Bundestag from 2021 to 2025, intends to retain her parliamentary seat. Life is likely to become quieter for her now as other politicians move into the spotlight, both in public and, perhaps, in private as well.
