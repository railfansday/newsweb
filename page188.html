#4122. Earth’s Magnetic Shield Is Rippling Unevenly – Scientists Have Uncovered the Cause

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

The solar wind can weaken the very waves it creates along Earth’s magnetic shield, simulations show, helping explain why some regions are more active than others.

Earth’s magnetic shield protects the planet, yet particles and energy from the Sun can still find their way through. Along its outer edge, a constant stream of solar material brushes past, stirring up waves where the two environments meet. A new study led by researchers at the University of Oulu explains why those waves grow stronger in some places than in others, revealing how uneven this boundary can be.

The protective region created by Earth’s magnetic field is called the magnetosphere, while the stream arriving from the Sun is known as the solar wind. Their interactions help drive space weather. On Earth, the effects can appear as auroras, while strong solar storms can disrupt satellites, positioning systems, radio communications, and power grids.

The boundary waves are known as Kelvin–Helmholtz waves. Similar patterns sometimes appear in clouds, and related phenomena occur in the Sun’s atmosphere, along the edges of other planets’ magnetic fields, and in interstellar space. Around Earth, however, satellites rarely pass through the most active regions.

Those limited encounters have made it difficult to establish how the waves behave under different solar wind conditions, so the researchers used computer models of the entire magnetosphere to examine their formation.

“This finding grew out of a long process of thinking through what I was seeing. In 2020, when I was visiting NASA as a postdoctoral researcher, I noticed unusual simulation images of the magnetosphere. I later realized that they showed Kelvin–Helmholtz waves at the edge of the magnetosphere, and that the different behavior of the waves on different sides was part of the same phenomenon,” says Academy Research Fellow Lauri Holappa from the University of Oulu.

The models traced the uneven wave activity to the way the solar wind’s magnetic field meets Earth’s field. Rather than wrapping evenly around the magnetosphere, the solar wind’s field drapes asymmetrically around it. This draped field can dampen the waves, but its damping effect varies from place to place, allowing stronger wave activity in some regions than in others.

“Asymmetries in the wave motion at the edge of the magnetic field have been observed before, but now we were able to show how they arise,” Holappa says.

The strongest activity appeared in the winter hemisphere, which faces away from the Sun. Within that hemisphere, the direction of the solar wind’s magnetic field determined whether the waves were concentrated on the dawn side or the dusk side.

Reference: “IMF By-Controlled Dawn–Dusk and Hemispheric Asymmetries of Kelvin–Helmholtz Waves and Reconnection Geometry Under Negative Dipole Tilt” by L. Holappa, K. Nykyri, J. Laitinen and B. Burkholder, 16 September 2026, Geophysical Research Letters. DOI: 10.1029/2026GL124835

#4266. This College of Magic turns ordinary kids into cool magicians

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Lubelo Mthenjana, who's 11, performs a magic trick involving fire during the annual junior magic contest at the College of Magic in Cape Town, South Africa. Tommy Trenchard for NPR hide caption

Dressed in a sharp black three-piece suit and a dapper yellow bow tie, Lubelo Mthenjana stands alone on a dark stage, taking in the last few seconds of quiet before the curtain opens. He takes a deep breath as his name is announced. Then he opens his arms wide and flashes a bright smile.

"Hello everyone! My name is Lubelo Mthenjana, and I will be performing for you … some magic!"

The audience of about 30 children and parents is spellbound. They laugh out loud as he makes a string of paper clips vanish into a plastic cup and gasp with surprise as the clips reappear inside a matchbox. Then comes his best trick: He conjures sweets out of thin air and tosses them into the crowd. The room erupts. As Lubelo takes his final bow to thunderous applause, his mom beams a proud smile at the 11-year-old apprentice magician.

On this cloudy Saturday morning in September, Cape Town's College of Magic is buzzing with energy as dozens of students are competing in the annual junior magic contest. Heading to their classrooms, children in top hats, crisp white shirts and ties dash up and down a staircase decorated with a riot of brightly colored posters. On every floor, display cabinets overflow with magic wands, sparkly hats, silk scarves, handcuffs, dice, Rubik's Cubes, toys, photographs and dozens of trophies earned by students in competitions all over the world.

Students examine a set of trick playing cards during a class at the College of Magic, whose pupils are ages 10 to 18. Tommy Trenchard for NPR hide caption

Aspiring magician Sibabalwe Sibaj, 17, juggles while riding a unicycle. Tommy Trenchard for NPR hide caption

Outside the old red-brick Victorian mansion, framed by palm trees, two teenage boys zip around the car park on unicycles. Balls, clubs and diabolos shoot up into the air as a handful of kids practice juggling on the porch.

Tucked away in a peaceful suburb, this extraordinary school is a real-world Hogwarts: Africa's only school for youthful stage magicians and illusionists. Every Saturday morning, about 190 starry-eyed students ages 10 to 18 attend classes in everything from puppetry, mime and clowning to juggling, card tricks, illusions, mentalism, stage magic, theater production and escapology. "All the skills that will make a good magician," sums up David Gore, the school's founder.

In 1980, Gore was studying law and paying his way by performing at private magic shows when he and a friend came up with the idea of starting a magic school.

David Gore founded the Cape Town College of Magic in 1980, defying apartheid era laws by teaching multiracial classes. In the decades since, the college has trained thousands of magicians, illusionists and performers. Tommy Trenchard for NPR hide caption

"We walked into our local newspaper house with our top hats and tails, told our story about how we were starting the College of Magic, did magic for the journalists in the newsroom, and they published an article," he recalls. "From that article, we had 34 young people who started attending formal weekly classes studying magic."

At the time, South Africa was still in the grip of apartheid, a brutal system of racial segregation that dictated almost every aspect of daily life. State schools were strictly segregated. Students of different races could not even share park benches, some building entrances or take the same public transport to school. Yet the College of Magic was multiracial from day one.

The College of Magic Students are entranced by a class on close-up magic. Tommy Trenchard for NPR hide caption

"When we started out, our organization was illegal," says Gore, referring to its no-segregation policy. "This was an opportunity for young people from all backgrounds to come together around a common interest in magic, to learn about each other and understand each other."

Nearly 50 years later, the school continues to stand out in a city still deeply divided along racial and economic lines.

"Many of our young people remain siloed in their socioeconomic groups, very often along either language or racial lines," Gore explains. "Some arrive in very fancy cars, others have taken an hour in a collective taxi to get here. But when they're here, they work with the same equipment, the same classes, the same stages and get the same food."

Though the six-year course costs a fee, about 80% of the students cannot afford to pay and are sponsored, mostly through private donations.

How do you master the arts of juggling and spinning plates? Practice, practice, practice. Tommy Trenchard for NPR hide caption

"Magic is such a wonderful companion for our young people here in South Africa," says Gore. "They're able to carry it in their pockets, into the communities and the marginalized areas where most of our students come from. It's an art form that can be performed without being wealthy or needing very expensive instruments."

Multiple studies have found that learning magic can help children develop self-esteem, curiosity, lateral thinking, fine motor skills and problem-solving abilities. A 2021 study conducted by researchers at the Universities of Hertfordshire and Edinburgh found that children who learned and performed a magic trick showed greater improvements in creative fluency and originality than those who completed a similar art activity.

Themba Van der Westhuizen, 12, practices a magic trick on a schoolmate at the College of Magic. Van der Westhuizen says he enjoys impressing friends and family with his tricks and that learning to become a magician has boosted his self-confidence. Tommy Trenchard for NPR hide caption

Even watching a magic trick can challenge our assumptions about the world and stimulate more original thinking. In another study, researchers found that it stimulated areas of the brain associated with cognitive flexibility and processing unconventional information.

In this photo from 2021, Anele Dyasi, a former student and now teacher of at the College of Magic, reaches out to a white dove at the school's dove enclosure. The college has since stopped using animals in its performances. Tommy Trenchard for NPR hide caption

"Many of our graduates don't become performers or magicians. But one of the skills that this young generation is most going to need for their careers is creativity," adds Gore. "That is what's so fantastic about magic: Nothing's impossible for us."

For 11-year-old Lubelo, magic has "opened up a new world." After watching a family friend perform a trick, he was determined to join the College of Magic. He had to wait until he turned 10 to enroll. Now, he's hooked.

At home in Makhaza township, about 40 minutes from the college, Lubelo spends his free time watching magic shows online and practicing tricks on his brothers and parents. He keeps his props, tools and other secrets in a box hidden away in his room. He's even chosen a stage name: Spade Boy — after his favorite card suit.

Aspiring magician Lubelo Mthenjana is clearly the family star as they stand outside their home in the Khayelitsha neighborhood of Cape Town. Tommy Trenchard for NPR hide caption

"If my mom is cooking and asks me to pass the potatoes, I'll start juggling with them," he says mischievously.

Lubelo has also persuaded several friends to join the school, becoming something of an ambassador in a community where magic is often frowned upon and associated with witchcraft.

"Many people are afraid of magic. But at the College of Magic, we don't do dark magic," he explains. "We just do the kind of magic that makes somebody smile, makes them happy and takes the stress away."

Sometimes, he says, other children ask whether he can turn people into pigs. "Then I just say: 'Let me start with you!' and they run away," he laughs.

Lubelo's mother, Yandiswa, says the magic lessons have boosted her son's confidence. They have brought another kind of relief, too. "It keeps him occupied and away from this environment and everything that's happening outside," she says.

With nearly 60 murders per day, South Africa has one of the world's highest crime rates, and several of Cape Town's townships are among the areas hardest hit by violent crime. From his home in Makhaza, Lubelo often hears gunshots. He says boys his age are drawn into using and selling drugs.

The magic community hasn't been spared. Just a few weeks before our visit, the school held a memorial service for Duma Mgqoki, a promising young magician, former student and volunteer teacher who was killed in a shooting in July. He was 22.

In this photo from 2021, Duma Mgqoki tries his hand at a card trick at the Cape Town College of Magic. In 2026, Mgqoki was killed in a shooting in the Philippi neighborhood of Cape Town. Many of the College's students come from neighborhoods marked by unemployment and violent crime. Tommy Trenchard for NPR hide caption

"On a weekly basis, some of our young people come to us saying they've experienced crime on a firsthand basis," says David Gore. "These things need to change, and we're very hopeful that the younger generation of today are going to be the force of change to bring about the society which we all would like to live in."

Students from the Cape Town College of Magic do their magic trick homework in the Mfuleni neighborhood where they live. Tommy Trenchard for NPR hide caption

Back at the college, after finishing his show, Lubelo steps off the stage to a chorus of fist bumps and backslaps from his excited friends.

"He was so good! We're swimming in happiness!" one teenager beams. Without skipping a beat, the boys run outside, pick up colorful leather balls and launch into a juggling competition.

Lubelo Mthenjana begins his performance during the annual junior magic contest at the College of Magic. Spoiler alert: He won in his age class! Tommy Trenchard for NPR hide caption

By now, a light drizzle has set in, but the two unicyclists are still making their rounds in the car park, seemingly undeterred. "Even when the storm comes, even when the world ends, I'll never stop unicycling," declares 14-year-old Joshua Clyde as he whizzes past.

Inside the cafeteria, a wide-eyed crowd has gathered around a teacher showing off a magic trick. With a snap of his fingers and what seems like a touch of superhuman power, he makes a coin disappear from a student's closed fist. It reappears beneath the student's watch.

Others have opted for the puppeteering class, upstairs, where pairs of students are taught to bring their humanoid puppets to life.

Puppets are part of the curriculum at the College of Magic. Teacher Jesse Brooks shares his puppet mastery with 12-year-old Okuhle Ntshutshe. Tommy Trenchard for NPR hide caption

Footsteps drum up and down the staircase as the remaining performers in this Saturday competition rehearse their routines, nerves and excitement mounting. "Why am I so nervous? I've got this!" says bubbly Kungentando Ndenetya, 12, repeating the mantra to herself.

Aspiring magician Kungentanda Ndenetya performs during the annual junior magic contest at the Cape Town College of Magic. Tommy Trenchard for NPR hide caption

Once every child has performed, the jury retires to a quiet office to deliberate. All four judges are former students or teachers at the College of Magic. Twenty-five-year-old Caitlin Hutchison, who comes from "a long line of magicians," says the school taught her "resilience training." Monica Nyakatya graduated in 2009 and now works in logistics but has remained close to the institution that, she says, "shaped" her.

The judges at the annual junior magic contest applaud after a performance at the College of Magic in Cape Town, South Africa. Tommy Trenchard for NPR hide caption

The deliberations are strictly confidential. When the judges return, everyone gathers in the small theater. The young magicians hold hands, waiting anxiously as the results are announced. Then comes Lubelo's age group.

Prestidigitation pupil Lubelo Mthenjana adjusts his bow tie. Many of the College's students sport traditional magician attire like bow ties and top hats. Tommy Trenchard for NPR hide caption

Beaming, he strides onto the stage to shake each judge's hand — then runs into his mother's arms.

As soon as he gets home, he will practice his newest tricks.

#3494. Brazil votes in tight runoff between leftist Lula and right-wing Flavio Bolsonaro

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

The high stakes election will decide whether Brazil will move toward the right or retain a leftist government.

Voters in Brazil headed to the polls on Sunday for a presidential runoff election between incumbent Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva and Flavio Bolsonaro, the son of convicted former President Jair Bolsonaro.

In the first round earlier this month, Bolsonaro surpassed Lula by more than 2.2 million votes. More than 33 million voters out of nearly 160 million abstained.

However, Sunday's runoff election is expected to be neck-and-neck, with recent polls showing Lula closing the gap.

Candidates need more than 50% of the vote to become president.

The high-stakes election is set to determine Brazil's political direction, pitting left-of-center veteran Lula, 80, against right-wing Bolsonaro, 45, who is an ally of the US.

If Bolsonaro wins, it will signify a shift to the right for Brazil and could determine the extent to which the US will be able to grow its influence in Latin America.

Lula has campaigned to continue his social policies to boost employment and reduce poverty, while Bolsonaro has pledged a tougher course against crime and a political fresh start.

The 80-year-old was previously head of state from 2003 to 2010, and became president again in 2023.

Flavio Bolsonaro has sat in the Senate since 2019 and is running for the presidency for the first time.

The rematch between Lula and Bolsonaro comes against the backdrop of corruption scandals, economic downslide and accusations of US interference, which Washington denies.

Around 159 million people are eligible to vote in the election.

Preliminary results are expected on Sunday night.

#4126. Earth’s Ice Ages May Have Started With a Planet-Wide Ocean Domino Effect

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

New research suggests that cooling in the Northern Hemisphere may have triggered far-reaching ocean changes that helped drive Earth’s past ice ages.

Growing ice sheets in the Northern Hemisphere may have helped push Earth into past ice ages by triggering changes in ocean circulation thousands of miles away, near Antarctica. New research from the University of St Andrews identifies a possible mechanism connecting these distant regions, helping explain how the planet naturally reduced atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2) as ice ages developed.

Scientists have long known that the Southern Ocean plays a central role in regulating atmospheric CO2. Changes in its circulation and sea ice can increase the amount of carbon stored in the deep ocean, contributing to the cooling associated with ice ages. But researchers have struggled to explain how expanding northern ice sheets could influence waters so far south.

The growth of massive Northern Hemisphere ice sheets begins with changes in Earth’s orbit that alter how solar energy is distributed across the planet. According to the new study, published in the Journal of Climate, these expanding ice sheets may set off a much larger chain of events by cooling nearby North Atlantic waters.

As those colder waters move through the global ocean circulation system, they can eventually reach the Southern Ocean. If the northern cooling is strong enough, it could alter circulation around Antarctica and promote conditions that allow more CO2 to remain stored in the ocean rather than the atmosphere.

This proposed connection provides a physical explanation for how ice sheet growth in one hemisphere could help amplify cooling across the planet.

To test the hypothesis, researchers used computer simulations that cooled only the ocean’s northernmost latitudes (mimicking the effect of a large ice sheet). They then examined whether the temperature changes could travel south and alter Southern Ocean conditions in ways that reduce atmospheric CO2.

The results showed that cooling northern waters alone produced a substantial response. The simulated reduction in atmospheric CO2 reached 74% of the reduction produced when the researchers cooled the entire planet.

The experiments support the proposed connection between Northern Hemisphere ice sheets and Southern Ocean carbon storage. However, they demonstrate that the mechanism is physically plausible in a model, rather than establishing exactly how much it contributed to individual ice ages in Earth’s past.

Lead author Dr. Madison Shankle, from the University of St Andrews’ School of Earth and Environmental Science, explained:

“Essentially, we’ve produced evidence of a plausible mechanism connecting these two far-flung puzzles pieces – Northern Hemisphere ice sheets and Southern Hemisphere ocean circulation changes leading to CO2 drawdown – which has been a tricky thing to explain in our theory of Earth’s past ice ages. Our study takes the step of actually demonstrating them working in model simulations, which is a big step forward for this field.”

Ice age research has traditionally emphasized processes occurring around Antarctica, where ocean circulation strongly influences the exchange of carbon between the atmosphere and deep ocean. The new findings suggest that temperature changes in distant northern waters may also be an important part of that system.

Past ice ages provide natural examples of how carbon moves between Earth’s atmosphere and oceans over long periods. Understanding these processes can help scientists improve climate models and investigate how the global carbon cycle responds to changes in atmospheric CO2, including the additional carbon dioxide released by human activities.

Reference: “Isolated Northern Hemisphere Cooling in an Idealized Model Promotes Ocean Carbon Storage and Glacial-like Ocean Features” by Madison G. Shankle, Graeme A. MacGilchrist, Andrea Burke, Alice Marzocchi, Malte F. Jansen and James W. B. Rae, 22 October 2026, Journal of Climate. DOI: 10.1175/JCLI-D-25-0355.1

#4162. Scientists discover how pancreatic cancer rewrites its DNA to escape treatment

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Scientists have uncovered new details about how pancreatic cancer constantly changes its genetic makeup, allowing it to become more aggressive, spread throughout the body, and resist treatment. By examining nearly half a million individual cancer cells, researchers identified genetic changes that could reveal new ways to fight one of the deadliest forms of cancer.

The study, conducted by scientists at NYU Langone Health and its Perlmutter Cancer Center, focused on pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, a particularly difficult cancer to treat. Nearly 85 percent of patients die within five years of being diagnosed.

Although researchers have already linked more than half a dozen genes to the disease, much remains unknown about how pancreatic cancer develops and adapts. By examining tumor cells individually rather than studying them in large groups, the team gained a clearer understanding of how different populations of cancer cells change their DNA as the disease progresses.

How Pancreatic Cancer Evolves To Escape Treatment

The researchers discovered that pancreatic tumors contain a variety of distinct cell populations known as clones. Each clone consists of cancer cells with similar genetic characteristics, but their behavior can differ dramatically.

Some clones develop changes that help them withstand cancer treatments, while others become better equipped to invade surrounding tissues and spread to other parts of the body. These findings suggest that the cancer's ability to continually reorganize its genetic material plays a central role in both treatment resistance and metastasis.

Previous studies generally divided pancreatic cancer cells into two main categories: classical (less aggressive) and basal (more aggressive). However, because those studies examined large collections of cells together, scientists could not fully explain how cancer cells move from one category to the other.

The latest findings help explain this process. Genetic changes occurring within individual tumors allow cancer cells to shift between these two states, potentially making the disease more dangerous as it develops.

Scientists Identify a Gene That Could Make Pancreatic Cancer Less Aggressive

Published on October 13 in Nature Genetics, the study identified several genetic changes associated with pancreatic cancer progression. One particularly important finding involved MYC, a gene that showed heightened activity in some of the most aggressive cancer cell populations.

In additional laboratory experiments, the researchers blocked MYC activity in aggressive basal pancreatic cancer cells. Remarkably, the cells shifted back toward the less aggressive classical state.

The change also made the cancer cells more exposed to immune cells, suggesting they could become easier for the immune system to recognize and attack. Although further research is needed, the results point to MYC as a promising target for future pancreatic cancer treatments.

"Our study provides the most granular view to date of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, revealing how ongoing genetic reshaping drives tumor evolution over the course of disease," said study lead investigator Despoina Kalfakakou, PhD, a postdoctoral research fellow at NYU Grossman School of Medicine and Perlmutter Cancer Center. "These findings suggest that targeting the mechanisms underlying tumor evolution, including MYC, may help restrain the cancer's rapid progression."

"Our research also suggests that single-cell clonal analysis offers a better approach to understanding complex cancers, like pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, as it develops over time, by mapping how tumors evolve and adapt at each stage of disease progression," said study co-senior investigator Aristotelis Tsirigos, PhD, a professor in the Departments of Medicine and Pathology at NYU Grossman School of Medicine and a member of Perlmutter Cancer Center.

"More broadly, our study results highlight the critical role of clonal diversity, genetic plasticity, and interactions with the tumor's microenvironment in shaping human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma biology," said Dr. Tsirigos, who is also co-director of the Division of Precision Medicine and director of NYU Langone's Applied Bioinformatics Laboratories.

Why Pancreatic Cancer Can Evade the Immune System

The investigation also provided important clues about pancreatic cancer's ability to hide from the immune system, a characteristic that may help explain why the disease often responds poorly to immunotherapy.

Researchers discovered that certain cancer cell clones showed increased activity of the TIGIT gene, which is associated with proteins that help tumors escape immune detection.

At the same time, these clones exhibited reduced activity of the PD1 gene. This combination may help explain why immunotherapies designed to target PD1 have shown limited effectiveness against pancreatic cancer.

Understanding these differences between cancer cell populations could help scientists develop treatments that interfere with the specific mechanisms tumors use to avoid immune attack.

According to Dr. Tsirigos, clinical trials investigating drugs that inhibit MYC or TIGIT are already underway for pancreatic ductal carcinoma and other cancers. He said the latest findings underscore the need for larger studies examining the different states cancer cells can adopt and how treatments might be tailored to target them.

Nearly Half a Million Cancer Cells Reveal Hidden Tumor Diversity

To investigate how pancreatic cancer changes over time, the scientists examined gene activity (using transcriptomics) in 443,451 individual cells collected from 62 tumors belonging to 56 men and women diagnosed with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma.

Six of these patients also provided tumor samples both before and after chemotherapy, allowing researchers to examine changes associated with treatment.

Unlike traditional approaches that measure changes in gene activity across large groups of cells, the new analysis examined individual cells. This provided a much more detailed picture of the different cancer cell populations living within the same tumor.

The results showed that individual tumors contained approximately two to 11 distinct clones, consisting of varying combinations of classical and basal cancer cells.

By tracing the genetic origins of these clones and comparing patterns of gene activity, the researchers connected differences in DNA copy numbers with the types of cells present in each tumor.

This approach allowed them to identify which genes, including MYC and TIGIT, were particularly active at different points during cancer progression.

Together, the findings provide a more detailed understanding of how pancreatic tumors evolve, resist treatment, and spread. They also suggest that interfering with the genetic mechanisms driving these changes could offer new opportunities to combat a disease that remains exceptionally difficult to treat.

#4133. Antarctica’s “Doomsday Glacier” Could Raise Sea Levels Twice As Much as Scientists Feared

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Even without further warming, Thwaites Glacier could contribute twice as much to sea level rise by 2100 as previously estimated, according to a revised ice sheet model.

Thwaites Glacier in West Antarctica has been retreating and thinning rapidly, while satellites show substantial acceleration in its ice flow. Traditional ice sheet models have depicted slower movement, leaving scientists struggling to reconcile their simulations with the observations. The glacier lies in the Amundsen Sea Embayment, a sector responsible for roughly one-third of the Antarctic Ice Sheet’s mass loss.

A new ice sheet model developed by glaciologists at the University of California, Irvine and NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory projects that melting at Thwaites could contribute up to 30 millimeters, or about 1.2 inches, to sea level rise by 2100. That is twice previous estimates, even without additional climate warming. The study was published in Nature Communications.

“Glaciologists have long based their assumptions of ice sheet dynamics on the ‘grounding line,’ a discreet, narrow and mostly stable demarcation where ice leaves the land and begins floating on water,” said lead author Mattia Poinelli, who was a UC Irvine postdoctoral scholar in Earth system science when the study was conducted. “We have come to acknowledge a different arrangement beneath Earth’s major ocean-terminating glaciers. Satellite observations demonstrate the existence of a ‘grounding zone,’ sometimes kilometers wide, where warm ocean water is reaching deep underneath the ice sheets and causing vigorous melting.”

Synthetic aperture radar readings from the Finnish ICEYE satellite constellation allowed the researchers to detect kilometer-scale fluctuations in this zone along the main trunk of Thwaites. The data indicate that seawater penetrates several kilometers beneath grounded ice and lifts it during tidal cycles. This flushes the underside of the ice with pressurized, warm seawater, which currents altered by climate change push toward the Antarctic coastline.

That contact with the ocean affects how firmly the glacier remains anchored to ridges on the seafloor. According to Poinelli, a NASA JPL research affiliate who is now a project scientist at UCLA, the study showed that grounding zone processes accelerate the glacier’s detachment from these stabilizing features, allowing faster ice flow and deterioration.

“The Thwaites main trunk is being temporarily stabilized by the Mouginot Ridge,” he said. “Once that ridge is unpinned, the glacier will continually retreat across a predominantly retrograde seafloor toward its next topographic high, the South Ridge.”

A retrograde seafloor slopes downward farther inland, so retreat would carry the glacier into deeper water before it reaches the next rise in the terrain.

The researchers calibrated their model against more than 20 years of observations. By including a grounding zone instead of a grounding line, they reconstructed changes in the glacier from the 1990s to the present that were four times as pronounced as those represented in traditional models.

Simulations incorporating a grounding zone 4 to 6 kilometers wide, or about 2.5 to 3.7 miles, predict a 60 percent probability that the glacier will lose its anchoring contact with Mouginot Ridge by the end of the century, Poinelli said. “When you factor in key physical processes and calibrate against historical observations, you gain a reliable pathway for narrowing uncertainty in projections,” he said.

“We have documented and quantified the impacts of climate change on our planet’s sensitive ice sheets for decades, but a mounting realization gained in recent years of what is happening on the undersides of glaciers like Thwaites is leading to an even greater sense of alarm,” said co-author Eric Rignot, UC Irvine professor of Earth system science. “Most projections up-to-present have struggled to match the rapidity of the mass loss because of missing physics; our new model with more complete physics is able to match the record and then produces projections that are twice larger than before. These results predict greater risk of and exposure for coastal flooding than anticipated in the coming decades.”

#3731. Oil Spills Wreak Havoc on the Bottom of the Ocean, Even a Decade Later

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

This story originally appeared on Inside Climate News and is part of the Climate Desk collaboration.

Across hundreds of miles of the northern Gulf of Mexico, a deepwater ecosystem collapsed after the massive 2011 Deepwater Horizon oil and gas spill, and recovery is uncertain, new research concludes.

The study, published Monday in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, found that animal populations—including octopus, krill, squid, and shrimp—declined by 60 to 85 percent in the three and a half years after the spill, with few signs of recovery since. Krill and lanternfish, both foundational species in the Gulf’s food web, were among the hardest hit. The findings also suggest that the die-off is causing genetic bottlenecks that could further hinder ecosystem recovery.

The researchers identified it as the “fastest and most severe faunal decline reported for Earth’s largest ecosystem type” ever documented. The widespread die-off of marine organisms may also reduce the ocean’s ability to transport carbon dioxide from the atmosphere to the deep sea. After ruling out other factors, such as water temperature and oxygen levels, the researchers identified the Deepwater Horizon blowout as “the most parsimonious explanation” for the ecosystem’s collapse.

Comprehensive natural resource damage assessments and restoration efforts involving federal, state, and tribal trustees are ongoing. In 2016, BP agreed to a $20.8 billion settlement, including up to $8.8 billion for environmental restoration.

Remote ocean depths and habitats miles below the surface may seem buffered from the impacts of human activity, the authors wrote, but their findings suggest an industrial disaster pushed part of it into a diminished state with “limited resilience to facilitate recovery.” They said their findings also show that proposed industrial deep-sea mining could damage the ecosystem before baseline conditions are assessed.

A transoceanic drilling rig, operating for BP about 50 miles southeast of the Mississippi Delta, exploded on April 20, 2010. The explosion and fire killed 11 people and triggered the largest accidental marine oil spill in history.

Oil and gas spewed uncontrollably from the broken well nearly a mile deep for 87 days, ejecting 4.9 million barrels of oil at a rate of about 400 tanker trucks per day, enough to fill up 1.2 million cars. The spill also released about 7.7 billion cubic feet of fossil gas into the deep sea at a rate of 88 million cubic feet per day, enough to supply about 820,000 US homes. State and federal agencies, including the Environmental Protection Agency, also authorized BP to apply 1.84 million gallons of chemical dispersants to try to reduce the impacts.

Some of the oil and gas dissipated and was transported out of the deep Gulf of Mexico region. Some coated barrier islands and devastated coastal wetlands and shellfish habitat, and some settled onto the ocean floor, where currents can stir it back into the water. But enough hydrocarbon and chemical-dispersant remnants stayed suspended long enough to do almost immeasurable harm.

A series of studies since the spill have shown a wide range of toxic effects on marine organisms, from impaired development and reproduction to increased mortality and lasting health problems in dolphins and whales. The new paper suggests that lingering pollutants may interfere with some species’ ability to find mates in the dark by disrupting their highly developed sense of smell.

The impacts continue to ripple through the ecosystem, said lead author Tracey Sutton, a professor of marine and environmental sciences at Nova Southeastern University in Florida. A 2024 study found an 80 percent decline in deep-diving toothed whales, he said.

“We used to have a big resident population of sperm whales right off the Mississippi River, but we haven’t seen a sperm whale in 10 years,” he said. Large predators need big, occasional dense patches of prey, and those have disappeared, he added.

Scientists have developed accurate ways to count fish. For this study, they started with a baseline from nine months of sampling in 2011 and compared it with nine subsequent surveys between 2015 and 2023. The researchers sampled deep-pelagic habitat along a large grid around the spill site from about 650 feet below the surface to about a mile down. The deep-pelagic zone accounts for about 90 percent of the Gulf’s total volume.

Across a grid of stations, scientists on slippery decks lowered and hoisted nets that open and close at different depths, with experts from different species groups to identify the catch. They sampled around noon and midnight to account for species that migrate vertically each day. Ship-mounted scientific echosounders provided an independent check, measuring sound reflected by marine life in the water column.

The research is “comprehensive and carefully composed,” marine biologist Bruce Robison, a senior scientist at the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute, said via email. “There is no other study that I know of with such an incredible number of net samples,” added Robison, who did not participate in the new study.

The research focuses on species that are not commercially fished and shows for the first time that human impacts can deplete marine life even in very deep ocean water, he said.

“It is important for the public to understand just how disastrous this oil spill was,” Robison said. “The Gulf has not recovered, and it’s been over 15 years.” Robison said other activities, such as deep-sea mining, could also have severe negative consequences for deepwater pelagic ecosystems by discharging wastewater containing metals or lubricants.

Three lines of evidence—net hauls, acoustic surveys, and genetic sampling—corroborate each other, which is “unusually strong evidence to support his conclusions,” said Jeffrey Drazen, a marine ecologist at the University of Hawaii at Manoa, who did not participate in the new study.

The study examines all possible causes of the decline and shows only one aligns with the scientific evidence, he said.

“Through logical elimination,” he said, “and without actually pointing a finger, they leave no doubt that the oil spill is the cause of the decline.”

#4113. Low-dose aspirin linked to 70% lower dementia risk, but there's a catch

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

A new analysis from Monash University suggests that low-dose aspirin may be linked to a dramatically lower risk of dementia in older adults with certain genetic characteristics. Researchers found that people with a specific genetic profile who took aspirin had a 70 percent lower relative risk of developing dementia, raising the possibility that genetic testing could eventually help identify people who might benefit from preventive treatments.

The findings, published in Alzheimer's & Dementia: The Journal of the Alzheimer's Association, were based on genetic data from more than 13,500 participants in the landmark ASPREE Trial (Aspirin in Reducing Events in the Elderly). Scientists examined whether differences in participants' genes could help explain why aspirin might affect dementia risk differently from one person to another.

Aspirin Linked to 70% Lower Dementia Risk in Certain People

The most striking results involved genes associated with platelet count. Platelets are small components of the blood that help form clots, and researchers wanted to determine whether genetic variations related to platelet levels could influence aspirin's potential effects on dementia.

After assigning participants genetic scores associated with platelet count, the team compared dementia rates among those who received aspirin and those given a placebo.

Among people in the highest 20 percent of platelet count genetic scores, just 1 percent of aspirin users developed dementia, compared with 3.3 percent of participants taking a placebo. That difference translates to approximately a 70 percent lower relative risk of dementia among those receiving aspirin.

However, the apparent benefit was accompanied by a significant safety concern. In the same genetic group, 4.4 percent of aspirin users experienced major bleeding, compared with 2.1 percent of those receiving a placebo.

A Genetic Clue Could Change Dementia Prevention

Lead author Dr. Peter Fransquet, a Research Fellow at Monash's School of Public Health and Preventive Medicine, said the results suggest that a person's genetic makeup could eventually play an important role in determining which dementia prevention strategies are most appropriate.

"Previous trials found aspirin didn't prevent dementia when everyone was considered together," Dr. Fransquet said.

"Our findings suggest there may be more to the story.

"In people with this particular genetic profile, we saw substantially fewer cases of dementia among those taking aspirin.

"It raises the possibility that genetics could one day help us identify who may benefit from a preventive treatment, rather than taking a one-size-fits-all approach."

The possibility of more targeted prevention is especially important as dementia becomes increasingly common. Dementia Australia estimates that approximately 446,500 people in Australia are living with the condition in 2026. That number is expected to exceed one million by 2065.

Blood Platelets Reveal an Unexpected Dementia Connection

Previous research has connected dementia with platelet activation and aggregation, the processes through which platelets become active and stick together. However, scientists have had a less complete understanding of how a person's overall platelet count might relate to dementia.

The new findings point toward platelet biology as a potentially important area of investigation. Notably, the genetic association identified by researchers did not involve the well-established genetic risk factors typically linked to Alzheimer's disease and other forms of dementia.

"What's particularly interesting is that the signal wasn't linked to the established genetic risk factors for Alzheimer's disease and dementia," Dr. Fransquet said.

"Instead, it's pointing us towards platelet biology and gives us a new avenue to investigate."

Why More Research Is Needed Before Taking Aspirin

Although the findings suggest that aspirin could have different effects depending on a person's genes, researchers emphasize that the results must be confirmed before they can guide medical recommendations.

The next step is to investigate whether the association appears in other populations. Scientists would then need to conduct a clinical trial specifically involving people with the genetic profile identified in the analysis.

"We now need to confirm the finding in other studies and ultimately test it in a trial designed specifically for people with this genetic profile.

"People shouldn't start taking aspirin to prevent dementia on the basis of this study without consulting with their doctor, particularly given the increased risk of serious bleeding.

"If it holds up, the fact that aspirin is already cheap and widely available could make personalized dementia prevention a real possibility."

#3736. Apple CarPlay and Android Auto Make Your Phone Hot. Here’s How to Keep It Cool

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Android Auto and CarPlay have transformed the way people drive.

Rather than putting up with whatever infotainment systems car manufacturers have put together, we're now able to interact with our phones from our car dashboards—with our contacts, our music, our podcasts, and our mapping apps all available. And the list of supported vehicles just keeps growing.

Both the Google and Apple solutions for this are really simple to use too. Most of the time they'll connect up automatically once you've done the initial setup. What you might not realize, though, are the demands that Android Auto and CarPlay put on your phone. Your handset is essentially doing all the regular phone stuff and then powering a large tablet-like display at the same time. That typically includes constantly updating a map in real time.

With that in mind, your Android phone or iPhone can quickly start to lose battery life and heat up when it's connected to your vehicle, which you've perhaps already noticed. To keep your handset cool and your battery in good shape, here's what you need to do.

Wireless Android Auto and CarPlay are convenient, just like wireless charging is convenient, but it puts more stress on your phone. If you've got the option of a wired connection, that's preferable—but that introduces a new potential heat problem, which is that your phone will always be charging up.

To counter that, you can set charge limits (Battery and charging from Android Settings, Battery > Charging from iOS Settings), or simply make sure your phone is kept in a cool, dark spot of the car.

This wired option has the added bonus of letting you switch to airplane mode on your phone, which will go a long way to limiting heat. Again there's a trade-off though: No one will be able to reach you, and you'll need to have your maps, music, podcasts, and whatever else you need downloaded to your device in advance.

If you do stick with wireless Android Auto or CarPlay, try to avoid charging your phone at the same time, especially on the wireless pad built into the car’s dash (which generates more heat). You don't want to end up with a dead phone half way through the journey, so you may have to compromise at certain times.

As mentioned above, where your phone is in the car makes a real difference to how hot it's going to be. If you have your phone's interface up on your car dashboard, then you don't necessarily need to have it fixed to the dashboard, too, possibly under direct sunlight. The glove compartment will do just fine.

If you have a mount for your phone, think about attaching it in front of one of your car's air vents. Blasts of air conditioned air will significantly reduce how hot your handset gets, working like a DIY cooling system.

You might also want to think about taking the case off of your phone for the length of the trip, if your phone is fitted with one. Your handset is less likely to be dropped or knocked while you're driving anyway, and a case can trap the heat in.

The rules aren't too different to keeping your phone cool generally, though with the added wrinkle of needing to keep it connected to your car somehow. Make sure the cabin in general is at a comfortable temperature, though you're probably already doing that for your own sake. The bigger thing to remember is to avoid leaving your phone behind in a vehicle that's going to heat up in the sun if you're stopping off somewhere.

If your phone is hooked up to Android Auto or CarPlay, then it's probably got some work to do—whether it's getting you to a destination or streaming music. However, you can still reduce heat by keeping phone tasks down to a minimum. If your calls and (voice-activated) messages can wait, leave them until the end of the journey.

We mentioned offline syncing above, but it doesn't have to go along with airplane mode. If you sync your tunes, podcasts, and maps to your phone ahead of time, your handset has less work to do while you’re driving since it doesn’t need to pull data from the cloud while also constantly searching for a wireless signal as you travel. Less stress on the modem means less heat overall.

Both Apple Maps and Google Maps let you download maps ahead of a trip. In Apple Maps, tap your profile picture, then Offline Maps, then Add Offline Map and find your location. With Google Maps, tap your profile picture, then choose Offline maps > Select your own map to choose the coverage.

There may be times when your phone has to be charging, wirelessly connected, making calls, in direct sunlight, and streaming music over a cellular connection, but try to keep these times to a minimum. Check regularly on how hot your phone is getting while connected to Android Auto or CarPlay, so you can take action if needed.
