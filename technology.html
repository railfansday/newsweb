Spending face-to-face time with friends and other people is the strongest predictor of their mental health, according to a 2026 study of nearly 80,000 students that I conducted with the help of my students.

This finding matters because young people are spending less time together in person and more time communicating through screens.

Between 2010 and 2022, the percentage of U.S. high school seniors who reported seeing friends face-to-face almost every day declined from 44% to 32%.

Against this backdrop, mental health challenges among young people have become an increasing public health concern. Suicide remains the second-leading cause of death among Americans ages 15 to 34.

But mental health is about more than the absence of mental illness. My student researchers and I wanted to ask a somewhat different question: What helps young people flourish?

To answer that question, we created a broad measure of well-being that captured more than the lack of mental health problems, such as psychological distress. It also included positive aspects of life, such as having supportive relationships, hobbies and a sense of meaning and purpose.

Our research team analyzed responses from 79,246 college students across the United States and Canada. We examined 51 aspects of students’ lives, including their substance use, the discrimination they encountered, their financial stress, any sleep problems and different relationship challenges.

We also examined how students spent their free time, whether that meant exercising, studying, socializing, meditating, playing video games or scrolling on social media.

We used statistical methods that allowed us to compare dozens of potential influences on student well-being simultaneously, helping us identify which factors were most strongly associated with flourishing.

Among the dozens of factors we examined, six stood out as the most important and influential.

The strongest factor connected to student flourishing was remarkably ordinary: spending time with other people. Students who spent more hours socializing with friends, attending student organizations and participating in cultural events tended to report feeling an overall sense of well-being.

The second most important factor surprised us. Students who spent more time on obligations, including paid work and unpaid family responsibilities, also tended to report a greater sense of well-being.

Roxanne Prichard is a Professor of Psychology at University of St. Thomas. This article is republished from The Conversation under a Creative Commons licence. Read the original article .

Our results raise an intriguing possibility: Well-being may come not only from leisure and self-care but also from contributing to something larger than oneself.

While problematic substance use, passive screen time and sleep problems tended to compromise well-being, these factors weren’t the biggest detriment. It was difficulty navigating interpersonal relationships, including problems with friends, romantic partners and roommates.

Making friends, maintaining healthy relationships and working through conflicts requires practice, and some of that practice time now competes with communication mediated by screens.

Notably, the contributors to student flourishing were similar among students diagnosed with anxiety or depression and those without these diagnoses. That suggests these everyday experiences may matter across a broad spectrum of mental health.

Supporting students’ mental and emotional health is not simply a matter of reducing screen time. It also means making it easier for young people to spend time together in person, whether through clubs, recreation programs, community events or other welcoming spaces.

Counseling and educational programs can likewise help students build friendships, communicate effectively and navigate conflict in healthy ways.

None of this means mental illness can be solved by telling a struggling young person to put down their phone, join a club or get more sleep. Mental health conditions are complex, and people experiencing them need access to effective professional care.

But our findings suggest that helping young people thrive requires more than treating symptoms. It also requires cultivating supportive relationships, healthy sleep and meaningful engagement in work, school and family life.

India’s wildlife laws were largely crafted to protect native species like elephants, rhinoceroses, and tigers from being trafficked overseas. Regulatory gaps in controlling imported animals complicate efforts to trace the origins of exotic wildlife.

Studies have found that in both male and female viewers, this upright advantage shrinks when it comes to sexualised images of women but not men – suggesting they are processed more like objects.

“Our findings are initial evidence that the way our visual system processes sexualised images of women can be changed, at least temporarily, by mildly stimulating the brain. They were then asked whether they recognised the models when these were mixed in with unseen images.

In the placebo group, participants recognised images of male models much easier when they were upright, compared to them being upside down. For images of women, that advantage was significantly smaller, closer to the pattern seen for objects.

What do the IRS, banks, airlines, and government infrastructure around the world have in common? They’re all running on systems propped up by antique computer code that nobody dares touch, lest everything come crashing down.

OpenAI thinks it may have a solution. On October 19th, the company touted “substantial improvements” in its latest AI models’ ability to migrate aging software written in older programming languages into modern code, which it claims could help organizations with “maintaining and modernizing older systems.”

That’s a potentially massive deal. COBOL, one of the languages highlighted by OpenAI, remains stubbornly embedded in vast portions of the world’s critical infrastructure. And despite decades of efforts to banish it to history, the language has limped on, simply refusing to die.

“Nobody in their right mind would use COBOL for a new development project.”

It is hard to overstate COBOL’s ubiquity. Short for “common business-oriented language,” COBOL came into being in 1959. Nearly seven decades later, it is still everywhere. According to IBM, COBOL is the foundation for more than 40 percent of online banking systems, supports 80 percent of in-person credit card transactions, and handles 95 percent of ATM transactions. It also underpins many government systems, from Social Security to the IRS to services run by most states. One rough estimate puts the annual cost of COBOL’s inefficiencies to the US GDP at around $105 billion.

But replacing COBOL is fiendishly difficult. The same goes for other legacy programming languages, like Fortran, still in use today for everything from scientific computing and weather forecasting to atmospheric and ocean studies and codes critical to nuclear security. These systems are often too important to risk breaking, and many of the programmers who understand them have long since retired or died.

For all their impressive advances in modern software development, AI coding tools have struggled to make much headway with these legacy systems. But recent developments suggest that may be changing.

In its latest blog post, OpenAI reported promising results migrating COBOL code to more modern programming languages, including C++, Java, Kotlin, Python, and Rust, without changing what the original software does. That last bit is crucial for organizations looking to ditch code like COBOL, and OpenAI acknowledges that “a migration is considered successful only if all tests pass.”

In OpenAI’s own tests, GPT-6.1 Sol achieved 87 percent behavior preservation accuracy at low reasoning effort — how many computational steps the model spends analyzing a problem before giving an answer — climbing to 98 percent at higher reasoning. By comparison, at low reasoning effort, GPT-6 Astra scored around 86 percent, GPT-6 Sol just 21 percent, and GPT-5.6 Sol a mere 5 percent.

“Rewriting code is not the same as replacing a system.”

“These evaluation results demonstrate progress toward more accurate, affordable code migration for teams modernizing the software their businesses depend on,” OpenAI wrote.

The company also reported improvements on its models’ ability to build database software in various programming languages, including COBOL.

Anthropic also sees an opportunity in legacy systems. In February, it published a step-by-step “code modernization playbook” and pitched its coding agent, Claude Code, as a way to migrate COBOL code into modern languages like Java or Python. It offered no performance benchmarks showing how well Claude Code could perform such migrations, but declared that “the economics of COBOL modernization have shifted.” Shares in IBM, which derives part of its revenue helping companies maintain legacy systems, nosedived following Anthropic’s announcement.

Experts who spoke to The Verge agreed that AI tools have made strides in tackling legacy code in recent years. But they cautioned that advances trumpeted by OpenAI this week are a very long way from making it possible to safely replace critical legacy systems built around it.

OpenAI spokesperson Oscar Haines acknowledged in an email to The Verge that legacy code “is still far from a solved problem” and said the company is listening to feedback from developers and enterprises and exploring ways to build better tests and continue improving its models’ understanding of coding languages. Anthropic did not provide comment in time for publication.

“The evaluations shown by OpenAI don’t address the key difficulty: Breaking up a complex legacy system into smaller units that can be replaced individually.”

“Rewriting code is not the same as replacing a system,” said Vadim Zaytsev, an associate professor of software evolution at the University of Twente in the Netherlands. Systems do have a lot of code — Zaytsev said the largest system he’d seen had 250 million lines of COBOL, “definitely not the world’s largest” — but that’s only part of the problem. Code may be written in multiple languages and there’s data, potentially stored in various formats, to consider, he said. There are also technical integrations with other systems and the expectations of the people and organizations to bear in mind.

All that complexity means “AI is not playing a major role at the moment in replacing or migrating legacy COBOL systems,” Zaytsev said.

OpenAI’s evaluations don’t capture this complex reality either, Arie van Deursen, a software engineering professor at Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands, told The Verge. He described the company’s blog as “superficial,” noting it offered few details about the migration task, while its language benchmark tested models on a relatively simple and well-defined task involving systems similar to those they would have encountered in training. He also stressed the huge gap between maintaining an aging system and modernizing it. “Maintaining is of course a lot easier than modernizing,” van Deursen said, calling the tasks OpenAI used to assess its models “lightyears” away from actual modernization. “The evaluations shown by OpenAI don’t address the key difficulty: Breaking up a complex legacy system into smaller units that can be replaced individually.”

The biggest challenge isn’t necessarily rewriting code. “The main challenge is indeed not understanding how the system works,” said Alastair Donaldson, a professor of programming languages at Imperial College London. Such a lack of understanding is a “very common” problem for legacy systems using languages like COBOL, he said, adding that very few people know how to program in them anymore. And while it is everywhere, Donaldson said “nobody in their right mind would use COBOL for a new development project.”

“In many cases even minor differences in system behaviour before and after migration are unacceptable.”

That makes robust testing crucial, Donaldson said. One approach could be to run the same workflows through the old and new systems to check they behave identically. AI could also document its code translation, ideally allowing humans familiar with the legacy language to review the work. Formal verification, which uses mathematical techniques to computationally verify that code behaves correctly, is another option, said Jacopo Mauro, a mathematics and computer science professor at the University of Southern Denmark.

Such methods are already used to verify AI-generated mathematical results, but applying them to legacy code would require a “huge amount of work,” Mauro said. Using those methods would mean code could be checked automatically, Mauro said, adding that “having this will be better than having a human check that the translation is correct.” Until then, he said, “human verification remains essential.”

And when the systems involved are as critical as those running on COBOL, experts say you need to be absolutely certain the replacement works. “In many cases even minor differences in system behaviour before and after migration are unacceptable,” Zaytsev said. While AI can definitely deliver savings and speed things up, the complexity of legacy systems means partial automation isn’t enough.

“Suppose you have a magic AI wand that handles 99 percent of your code,” Zaytsev said. “What do you do with the remaining 1 percent? That 1 percent can be a million lines of code, spread all over the place and subtly failing in more ways you can imagine within your lifetime.”

The difference can be startling. Is the price worth it though?

In late 2022, the European Union passed a landmark law requiring all smartphones and other electronic devices sold in the bloc to use USB-C for wired charging by the end of 2024. After years of resisting, Apple finally caved and swapped to USB-C.

To be precise, that’s Apple’s 1.8-metre Thunderbolt 4 Pro cable. And it’s not the same thing as an ordinary USB-C charging cord. Apple’s range also includes a $69 Thunderbolt 5 cable that supports substantially faster connections, although it’s shorter.

Why on Earth would Apple charge that much for a USB-C cable when most calbes are dirt cheap? It’s tempting to accuse Apple of corporate greed, and there’s a reasonable chance greed is also involved. But as it turns out, the story is more complex.

Different standards govern how USB-C cables handle charging and data transfer. That’s why two cables that look identical can perform wildly differently. To show just how deep those differences run, tech research company Lumafield used X-ray CT scanners to peer inside a range of USB-C cables—from bargain-bin cords to Apple’s high-end Thunderbolt 4 model. The X-rays show a clear difference.

Researchers peered inside the $5.59 USB-C cable by NiceTQ, with a modest 60 W charging. At first glance, it promises data transfer speeds of a whopping 10Gbps. But when Lumafield took a closer look, they discovered a different story.

The internal structure was barebones: no shielding, no PCB, just wires soldered directly to the connector pins floating in plastic. Out of the eight available pins, only four were active, meaning the cable could only reach around 480 Mbps, far below its advertised performance. And with no redundancy, if one pin fails, the whole cable is done for.

At the other end of the spectrum, Apple’s Thunderbolt 4 cable is a miniature feat of engineering. It supports blazing 40 Gbps data transfer, 100 W charging, and even daisy-chaining up to six Thunderbolt 3 devices.

Peeling back its layers, Lumafield’s scans exposed a robust stainless steel enclosure housing 24 independent pins on a 10-layer PCBA. Ten of these wires are co-axially shielded, each meticulously soldered to a complex PCB assembly. To ensure durability, a strain-relief fitting, crimped from eight directions, encases the entire assembly.

Lumafield also examined cables from Amazon Basics and ATYFUER, finding that the latter’s $3.89 model was surprisingly more sophisticated than the pricier NiceTQ cable.

There’s an important caveat here; or rather, a couple. These X-ray scans explain why Apple’s cable is so much more complicated than a basic charging cord. But they don’t prove that Apple makes the best cable for the money. Not at all. A certified Thunderbolt cable from another manufacturer may deliver comparable performance for significantly less.

And most people don’t need all this engineering in the first place. If you’re simply charging your phone or transferring the occasional file, a decent, appropriately rated USB-C cable is usually enough. The expensive stuff becomes useful when you’re connecting high-speed external storage, multiple displays, or other demanding equipment.

It’s also worth remembering that USB-C technology has moved on. Apple’s newer Thunderbolt 5 cable supports even faster data transfers and higher charging power, yet its shorter version costs considerably less than the $129 Thunderbolt 4 cable examined here.

The takeaway? Not all USB-C cables are created equal. Two cables can look practically identical on the outside and conceal vastly different technology. But that doesn’t mean the most expensive one is necessarily the best choice.

The best USB-C cable isn’t the most sophisticated one. It’s the one that does everything you need, without making you pay for things you don’t.

If millions of cars have the ability to send power back – or power the driver’s home (V2H) – it could help to solve one of the big problems of the transition away from fossil fuels to renewables: how to balance the grid during still, cloudy or dark periods when wind and solar generation is lower than electricity demand.

The move could give incentives to help to spread a technology that is not yet widely used, but which could lessen the load on the grid and the need to fire up expensive gas power plants to fill gaps in supply. Home batteries can do the same job.

Smart charging tariffs, which do not need V2G tech, already help with the opposite problem by encouraging electric vehicle (EV) owners to charge up when electricity is overabundant and cheap – reducing the need to pay renewable generators to switch off.

However, campaign groups said the ZEV incentives could also have the perverse effect of allowing carmakers to sell more polluting petrol models in the next few years.

It's unclear how widespread the problem is or whether it only affects a small number of units. (WIRED's review unit hasn't had any issues after more than two weeks of daily use.) Apple did not respond to WIRED's request for comment.

This is familiar territory for foldable phones. A hinge means strain on the screen and the components inside. It's also a place for dust to seep in and break the phone, as with Samsung’s original Galaxy Fold in 2019, forcing the company to pause sales and fix the issue. Some devices handle it better than others, and as the product category has grown, those issues have largely diminished. But fewer problems don't mean easier fixes.

On Sunday, repair company iFixit released its teardown of the iPhone Duo, including a video detailing the specific parts inside and what to look out for if people need to fix their $1,999 phone one day—maybe sooner than they think.

The phone got a provisional repairability rating of 5/10, though that score may be updated to factor in how Apple supplies replacement parts. A 5/10 doesn’t sound great, but it’s actually the highest repair rating iFixit has given any foldable phone.

“The state of foldables today is one of the hardest engineering challenges across a number of disciplines out there in the market today,” iFixit CEO Kyle Wiens said at an event the day before Apple announced its folding phone in September. “It is the pinnacle of material engineering and materials science. It is ludicrously difficult to accomplish this.”

The iPhone Duo earned good marks on some repair fronts. The battery and back panels are straightforward to remove and replace. The outer trim comes off in one piece. The 5.4-inch front screen comes off easily enough. The tricky part, according to iFixit, is the inner display. A thin matte nano-texture finish sits on top of the OLED screen, and if you’re not careful, you could easily peel the layer off. (Apple strictly tells users not to remove this finish on a support page.)

Removing the full inner screen requires melting the adhesive with a heat gun and some real elbow grease to pull the screen away. Users have reported breaking this part of the screen, and it seems it is the most fragile component and the hardest to replace. (Apple has an inner screen repair service, but it costs $999.) The Duo also collects dust in typical places like the USB-C port, outer edges, and speaker, but it accumulates along the inner hinge, causing it to sound "crunchy" like other folding devices when dust gets inside.

Apple long resisted letting users repair their own devices. When it finally started offering real repair options in 2021, its programs got off to a rough start and were less helpful than they could have been. Slowly but surely, thanks to various legislation in the US and the European Union, Apple has been introducing efforts to let people fix their own things. The iPhone Duo had a widely accessible repair manual on launch day, which hasn't always been the case for how the company shares that kind of information.

The iPhone Duo may not be perfectly repairable—folding phones might never get there. But when a device starts having problems like cracked screens, it’ll be nice to know how to fix the thing.

#4091. These 8 Incredible Pyramids Outside Egypt Are Even Stranger than You’d Think

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Home → Features → History and Humanities → Archaeology

Explore eight remarkable pyramids beyond Egypt.

Think of a pyramid, and chances are you’re picturing Egypt. But here’s a surprising fact: Sudan actually has more surviving ancient pyramids than Egypt. And that’s just the beginning.

In Mexico, one of the largest pyramids ever built is hiding beneath what looks like an ordinary hill, with a church sitting on top. In Guatemala, towering Maya temples emerge from the rainforest. And in Iraq, an ancient stepped monument was designed to bring worshippers closer to the realm of the gods.

Across Africa, the Americas, and Asia, ancient societies built various pyramid-shaped structures. Some architectural traditions developed independently, while others, like the pyramids of Nubia, were shaped by cultural exchange with Egypt.

Here are eight extraordinary pyramids outside Egypt, and the fascinating stories behind them.

The Pyramid of the Sun is the largest structure at Teotihuacan, an ancient city northeast of modern Mexico City. Its massive base measures about 225 by 222 meters, and the pyramid rises roughly 65 meters above the surrounding landscape.

But it’s not just an enormous pile of stone. Its builders placed it along the city’s central axis and constructed it as part of a large ceremonial complex surrounded by monumental temples, plazas and avenues. Archaeologists have also found ritual offerings associated with its construction, including the remains of sacrificed children, suggesting that building the monument was itself a religious act.

But perhaps the most intriguing thing about the Pyramid of the Sun is that we don’t know whether its builders actually worshipped the Sun there.

The people who built Teotihuacan left no known record calling it the ‘Pyramid of the Sun.’ They called the structure Tonatiuh Itzacual, which means “enclosure of the Sun,” giving the monument the name by which it is known today. Centuries after the city was abandoned, the Mexica arrived at the ruins and believed Teotihuacan was the place where the sun and moon had been created.

Modern researchers have found that the pyramid’s orientation corresponds to significant dates in the solar year. However, the identity of the deity originally associated with it remains debated. Possibilities include a solar deity, a fertility deity, or a supernatural figure connected to the underworld.

In other words, we know quite a lot about how this enormous monument was built. But its original religious meaning is still partly a mystery.

Egypt may have the world’s most famous pyramids, but its southern neighbour has more surviving examples. Sudan is home to at least 230 ancient pyramids, according to Guinness World Records.

In the Nubian desert of modern Sudan, the pyramids of Meroë are a reminder that the ancient Nile world extended far beyond Egypt.

The pyramid-building tradition of the Kingdom of Kush flourished from approximately the eighth century BCE into the fourth century CE. Royal cemeteries appeared at sites including El-Kurru, Nuri, and Meroë.

Meroë was once a major centre of the ancient Kingdom of Kush, a powerful Nubian kingdom centered in what is now Sudan. The Kushite pyramids are noticeably narrower and steeper than their Egyptian counterparts. Some rise to about 30 meters and were built over underground burial chambers for kings, queens, and members of the royal family.

Their steep sides, compact dimensions, and adjoining funerary chapels gave them a distinctive architectural identity.

The connection with Egypt was real, but Kush was not simply an Egyptian copy. Kushite rulers even controlled Egypt for nearly a century, making the pyramids part of a much larger story of cultural exchange along the Nile.

These monuments remind us that ancient Nubia wasn’t merely a peripheral part of Egyptian history. It was home to a powerful civilization with its own rulers, religious traditions, and architectural achievements.

Cholula’s pyramid has one of the strangest disguises in archaeology. It looks like a hill; and what a hill it is.

Beneath its grassy slopes lies an enormous ancient monument with an estimated volume of 4.45 million cubic meters. That makes Cholula the world’s largest known pyramid by volume, even bigger than the Great Pyramid of Giza.

Unlike many familiar pyramids, Cholula wasn’t constructed in a single monumental building campaign. Successive generations expanded the structure, building new architectural layers over older ones. Over many centuries, the monument grew into the giant artificial mountain we know today.

Centuries of abandonment allowed vegetation and soil to cover the monument. When Spanish colonists arrived in the early 16th century, the buried structure reportedly looked like a natural hill, and a church was eventually built on top.

The church, the Sanctuary of Our Lady of Remedies, remains one of Cholula’s most recognizable landmarks.

Archaeologists later excavated an extensive network of tunnels through the pyramid, revealing its successive construction phases. These passages helped researchers study the monument without dismantling the massive structure above them.

But what makes Cholula extraordinary isn’t just its size. It’s the way different periods of history occupy the same space: an ancient religious monument, centuries of changing cultures, and a Christian sanctuary overlooking the modern city.

At Tikal, pyramids rise from one of the world’s most dramatic archaeological settings—the rainforest of northern Guatemala.

The ancient Maya city is home to numerous monumental buildings, including six conventionally numbered main temples. Some of these structures still rise above the surrounding forest canopy, offering a glimpse of a once-powerful urban civilization.

The Maya built five major pyramid temples at the site between roughly 400 and 900 CE. After Tikal was abandoned, the jungle gradually reclaimed the city, leaving enormous structures emerging from the canopy.

Temple IV is the tallest, reaching about 65 meters. Built around 740 CE during the reign of the Maya ruler Yik’in Chan K’awiil, it is among the tallest surviving temples of the ancient Maya world. Its elevated position offers an extraordinary view across the forest, while the surrounding complex functioned as a major ceremonial and political center.

For the Maya, mountains were important spiritually. They could represent sacred places associated with creation, ancestors, and the supernatural world. Building towering temples allowed rulers to connect political authority with that sacred landscape.

Today, the experience is almost the opposite of what ancient builders intended. Instead of dominating a bustling city, Tikal’s temples rise above a tranquil sea of trees, reminders of a civilization that once transformed the rainforest around them.

Few pyramids in the world look as stunning as El Castillo in Mexico’s Chichen Itza.

Also known as the Temple of Kukulcán, this roughly 30-meter-high Maya pyramid is famous for its architecture and its apparent connections to astronomy and the calendar.

The pyramid has four stairways, each with 91 steps. Add the upper platform and the total becomes 365—matching the number of days in the Maya solar year.

But the pyramid’s most spectacular feature isn’t something carved into the stone. It’s something created by sunlight.

Around the equinoxes, sunlight creates a series of triangular shadows along the northern staircase that appear to form the body of a descending serpent representing Kukulcán, the feathered Serpent deity that the Maya people worshipped.

There are important caveats, however. The 365-step interpretation is widely cited, but the monument’s present form has undergone restoration, so the calendar connection should not be treated as definitive proof that every numerical feature was intentionally designed that way.

Similarly, the serpent-light phenomenon was traditionally associated with the equinoxes, but some studies indicate that the pattern of light and shadow follows a solar cycle throughout the year rather than appearing only on those exact dates.

Mexico isn’t the only place with a pyramid called El Castillo. Across the border in Belize, another monument of the same name rises above the ancient Maya city of Xunantunich.

At roughly 40 meters high, it is one of Belize’s most prominent ancient structures and once served as a ceremonial and civic center.

One of its most remarkable features is a series of elaborate stucco friezes. These decorated surfaces include symbolic figures associated with the supernatural world and offer clues to Maya religious and political beliefs. Parts of the original decoration have been protected, with replicas allowing visitors to appreciate the designs.

But Xunantunich also has a rather unusual claim to fame: a ghost story.

The site is wrapped in a famous local legend about a mysterious woman known as the “Stone Lady.” She is reportedly seen ascending the structure before disappearing into its walls.

In some versions of the tale, the woman wears white and has glowing red eyes. The story is folklore, not archaeological evidence, but it has become so closely associated with the site that the modern name Xunantunich itself means “Stone Woman” or “Maiden of the Rock.”

Between its carved monuments and its enduring local legends, Xunantunich shows how ancient places can acquire entirely new meanings long after the civilizations that built them have disappeared.

Borobudur is not a pyramid in the Egyptian sense. It is a Buddhist monument, and one of the most extraordinary examples of religious architecture ever built. Yet its unusual stepped form has led UNESCO to describe it as a “stepped, unroofed pyramid.” We thought that’s good enough for our list.

Built in Central Java during the 8th and 9th centuries, this temple rises through three major tiers with five square terraces, three circular platforms, and a huge central stupa. The upper platforms contain 72 openwork stupas, each enclosing a Buddha statue.

Its entire architectural arrangement represents a Buddhist understanding of the universe.

According to UNESCO, its successive levels symbolize three realms of existence: the world of desire, the world of forms, and the formless world. Moving upward through the monument becomes a symbolic journey toward enlightenment.

Where an Egyptian pyramid was generally associated with the burial and afterlife of a ruler, Borobudur was designed around a different spiritual experience. Its ascending terraces transformed an architectural journey into a representation of Buddhist cosmology.

Long before the Maya constructed their great stone temples, ancient Mesopotamians were building monumental stepped towers of their own. One of the most remarkable survivors is the Great Ziggurat of Ur, in present-day Iraq.

Built around 2100 BCE during the reign of the Sumerian king Ur-Nammu, the ziggurat originally measured approximately 64 by 46 meters at its base. Its estimated original height was between 21 and 30 meters.

Its massive mudbrick core was covered with fired bricks, while monumental staircases led upward through a series of terraces toward a temple dedicated to Nanna, the Sumerian moon god.

Unlike a conventional pyramid, the ziggurat was built as a series of terraces rather than a single sloping structure. It wasn’t designed as a royal tomb. Instead, it formed part of a larger religious and administrative complex at the heart of the ancient city.

The idea was that the raised temple brought the divine realm symbolically closer to the human world. It also made the building visible from far beyond the city, reinforcing the importance of Ur and its patron deity.

The ziggurat has undergone several major restorations, including work during the reign of the Neo-Babylonian king Nabonidus and a substantial reconstruction of its lower façade under Saddam Hussein in the 1980s. The original summit temple no longer survives.

The eight monuments in this list are only a glimpse of a much larger world of ancient pyramid-shaped architecture. From the deserts of Sudan to the rainforests of Central America, these structures reveal how different civilizations used monumental buildings to connect the earthly world with ideas about gods, kings, and the afterlife.

And archaeologists are still uncovering new details. In 2024, researchers announced the discovery of an approximately 1,700-year-old royal Maya tomb at Chochkitam in Guatemala, excavated in 2022. Inside the burial were a jade mosaic mask and carved human bones bearing imagery and glyphs that may help researchers reconstruct the history of a little-known ruling dynasty.

Ultimately, these pyramids tell us something more interesting than the fact that ancient people in different parts of the world liked building tall, triangular monuments. Similar shapes could serve profoundly different purposes. One civilization built a staircase toward the gods; another built a royal tomb; another created an architectural representation of spiritual enlightenment.

The pyramid may be one of humanity’s most recognizable architectural forms. But there has never been just one pyramid story.

#4066. Why Do Some People Get Fevers After Vaccines? Scientists May Have Found the Answer

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

The microbes in your gut may help explain why you develop a fever after a vaccine while someone else feels fine.

Two people can receive the same vaccine and have very different experiences. One develops a fever and feels miserable for a day, while the other barely notices anything. Scientists have long sought to understand why these reactions vary so widely, and new research suggests that part of the answer may lie in the trillions of microbes living in our intestines.

An international research team found that people who developed stronger fevers after vaccination already showed signs of heightened inflammation and distinctive gut microbial activity before receiving their shots. When researchers transferred their gut microbes into mice, the animals also developed stronger inflammatory responses to vaccination. The findings point to a bacterial protein called flagellin as a possible contributor and raise intriguing questions about whether diet could influence how the body responds to vaccines.

Vaccination prevents infectious diseases and protects public health, but concerns about fever and flu-like symptoms can discourage people from getting vaccinated. Understanding why these reactions vary could help researchers find ways to reduce them and address a source of vaccine hesitancy.

The gut microbiome is a plausible place to look because its trillions of microorganisms continually communicate with the immune system, helping shape its baseline activity. Because microbial communities differ between people, the same vaccine may encounter very different immune starting points.

To investigate, researchers led by Ruth Ley at the Max Planck Institute for Biology Tübingen established the µHEAT (Microbial-Human Ecology and Temperature) study. With support from Meral Esen at the Institute of Tropical Medicine in Tübingen, they recruited healthy young adults receiving a SARS-CoV-2 vaccine in 2021 and 2022.

Participants provided fecal samples for microbial analysis and blood samples for immune measurements. They also regularly recorded their temperature during the week before and the week after vaccination.

Organizing the study during the pandemic required the team to recruit participants before they had already received their vaccines, while keeping visits and measurements manageable.

“It was a challenging time to set up a new human study, with the pandemic ongoing,” says lead author Kelsey Huus, who led µHEAT during her postdoctoral research and is now an assistant professor at the University of Ottawa in Canada. “We didn’t want to burden participants with too many measurements or visits, and it was a bit of a race against time to get the study organized before everyone was already vaccinated. But it was absolutely worth it, because the project yielded some very exciting results.”

Some participants developed substantial fevers. Before vaccination, their gut microbes showed greater activity in genes involved in bacterial movement, particularly those used to make whip-like swimming structures called flagella.

Flagella are built from proteins called flagellins. Because pathogens such as Salmonella also produce flagellins, the immune system recognizes these proteins as potential danger signals and responds with inflammation. Blood and fecal measurements suggested that participants who developed stronger fevers already had higher levels of inflammation before vaccination.

The human observations revealed an association, but the researchers needed an experiment to test whether the microbes themselves could influence vaccine responses. They transferred participants’ fecal microbiomes into mice that lacked intestinal microbes of their own, then vaccinated the animals.

Mice receiving microbes from participants with stronger fever responses developed more severe inflammation than those receiving microbes from people with milder responses. The effect depended partly on the animals’ ability to sense flagellin, providing evidence that the bacterial protein contributed to the response.

“Vaccination provides a controlled way to understand why people can respond so differently to the same immune challenge,” says Ley, director of the Department of Microbiome Science. “Our findings show that microbial flagellin is one contributor to this baseline immune state, providing a potential link between differences in the gut microbiome and differences in immune responses.”

The team next explored what might encourage gut microbes to produce more flagellin. Participants eating plant-based diets tended to have weaker fever responses, making diet a candidate for further investigation.

“We could see that certain food additives increased flagellin production by gut microbes in vitro,” explains Benoit Chassaing, Inserm research director at Institut Pasteur. “Interestingly, these findings show that components of an industrialized diet may promote the growth of flagellated gut bacteria and increase overall flagellin production.”

Animal experiments offered further evidence. “We found that a high-fat Western-style diet enhanced inflammatory vaccine responses in mice,” says co-author Hirohito Abo of Georgia State University in Atlanta.

“While we could not show that diet determines vaccine responses in humans, our study findings suggest that diet is one factor capable of modifying a pro-inflammatory microbiome,” Huus says. “Interventional studies in people will be needed to determine whether dietary changes can alter microbial flagellin production in humans and, in turn, vaccine responses like fever.”

Future trials could investigate whether interventions targeting the microbiome can reduce strong fevers after vaccination. More broadly, the study suggests that gut microbes help establish an immune state that may influence responses to challenges beyond vaccines.

“Ultimately, this work adds to growing evidence that the gut microbiome plays a central role in human immune health,” Huus adds. “Understanding these interactions could now open new opportunities to improve how we predict, prevent, and manage individual differences in immune responses.”
