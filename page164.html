While it isn't happening to everyone, after checking my own home internet usage, I found out that I have been experiencing the same issue. My Google Home Speaker has used 487 GB of data in the past 10 days, using almost as much as the entire rest of my home between August and September.

Google confirmed this bug has only affected some devices and that it has rolled out a solution. A spokesperson from Google told WIRED: “A server misconfiguration caused some Google Home Speakers to repeatedly download a software update. This issue has now been resolved.”

Looking into this issue made me go beyond just Google's usage, though. My husband and I started reviewing the data usage for all of the individual devices that our router could identify and found some surprising numbers. There's also a mysterious device just labeled “WATCH” that used about 21 GB in the past week, but no one in my home has a smart watch that's been on in the last year. A mystery for me to solve another day.

#383. ‘Black October’: cybersecurity experts sound the alarm over new era of AI-enabled hacking

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

After discovery of severe bug in the PHP programming language, executives warn of ‘vulnerability tsunami’

Leaders in cybersecurity are sounding the alarm after the discovery of a major vulnerability this week. They are predicting that artificial intelligence tools will expose more fundamental weaknesses in global internet infrastructure in the months to come.

“I no longer think we’re going to be able to patch all bugs,” said Sam Altman on Thursday evening, joining the chorus of executives. In the midst of what’s being called “Black October”, for which researchers used AI to help identify critical flaws in a coding language that is used across millions of websites and may have been unpatched for years, tech executives are warning about the ways AI will be used both to identify and exploit security vulnerabilities at an unprecedented scale and pace.

The next few months will see a “vulnerability tsunami”, according to Jason Clinton, Anthropic’s deputy chief information security officer. “There won’t be enough time to patch critical infrastructure,” Clinton said in a post on X on Thursday evening. Marc Benioff, the Salesforce CEO, agreed and called it a “code red”.

AI is uniquely able to find and identify security flaws in a way and at a pace that will feel catastrophic because the security community will not be able to move quickly enough to address them, said Matthew Prince, the Cloudflare CEO. “It’s going to feel like the sky is falling. It’s going to feel like there’s nothing we can do.

“The next two years are going to be incredibly, incredibly, incredibly fraught with new tools that have infinite patience to go look for vulnerabilities across the Internet,” he wrote in a post on X.

While cybersecurity experts have been warning about a “vulnpocalypse”, or the ability for AI to more easily identify security bugs for months, it wasn’t until “Black October” that researchers were able to use AI to find a flaw as fundamental and critical as the one announced on 21 October.

Massive bugs were found in a programming language called PHP, which may have left the millions of websites that run on it vulnerable to hackers for years. Some experts estimated 70% of all the sites on the internet were at risk of hacking.

However, in the same way AI will be used to aid in identifying security vulnerabilities at an unprecedented pace, tech leaders worry it’ll also be used by malicious actors to exploit those bugs at an unprecedented pace.

Attempts to exploit the PHP vulnerability have already begun.

After the vulnerabilities were announced, government and private websites across the US and UK temporarily took down their sites to patch the bugs. The US National Security Agency (NSA) issued an alert recommending any site that runs on PHP implement the patches immediately. Malicious cyberactors may seek to further exploit these bugs to access servers without authorization, the NSA said.

“Such an access vector could help attackers steal sensitive data, disrupt services or move deeper into a network,” the warning read.

A weekly dive in to how technology is shaping our lives

In a post on X, Prince said CloudFlare, a security provider, had seen evidence of thousands of people trying to exploit the PHP flaws in the 10 hours before it was announced.

“That jumped to tens of thousands in the 45 minutes post announcement, hundreds of thousands within 2 hours, and millions now,” he wrote.

In a blogpost, researchers at AI security platform Wiz said that this was “among the most severe vulnerabilities discovered in PHP, and arguably in software history”.

“The underlying flaws had been present for decades, but demonstrating their exploitability required an exceptionally complex chain of steps, at the edge of what human researchers could practically achieve,” they wrote.
