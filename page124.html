Weakened US fuel-efficiency rules and promises of cheaper cars could yet see drivers pay higher running costs while increasing planet-heating emissions.

As part of its "Freedom Means More Affordable Cars" initiative, the US Department of Transportation this week rolled back fuel efficiency rules under the guise of lowering vehicle prices.

The Trump administration's transport agency says US taxpayers will save $138 billion (€122 billion) over the next five years — new vehicles will be $1,300 cheaper on average — since automakers will no longer have to invest in more fuel-efficient fleets.

But this does not account for soaring gas prices as Middle East conflicts choke global supply.

"Any savings on the front end get eaten up and spat out by excess costs of fuel… Fuel economy standards have saved drivers hundreds of billions of dollars," said Democratic Senator Sheldon Whitehouse from Rhode Island in a social media post.

Under the revised Corporate Average Fuel Economy (CAFE) rules, vehicle fleets only require an average fuel efficiency of about 35 miles per gallon (14.7 kilometers per liter) by 2031, down around one-third from 50 miles per gallon. Implemented by then-President Joe Biden in 2024, the updated efficiency rules aimed to incentivize the production of low emission electric vehicles and hybrids. The US National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) estimates the standards would have saved 70 billion gallons (265 billion litres) of fuel over 25 years.

Meanwhile, nonprofit advocacy group the Union of Concerned Scientists (UCS) says an earlier version of CAFE regulations has saved US consumers about $321 billion at the gas pump since 2010.

As the world's biggest petrol consumer, the US burns through 20 million barrels of oil per day, which is 20% of global consumption, notes Dan Becker, director of the Safe Climate Transport Campaign at the US-based nonprofit environmental group Center for Biological Diversity.

"Transportation is the biggest consumer of that oil, making strong auto standards the most effective way to cut pollution and oil use," Becker said in a statement.

The rules that have been diluted this week would have prevented more than 710 million metric tons of carbon dioxide emissions by 2050 — the equivalent of running 165 million gas-powered passenger vehicles for a year.

"With transportation representing nearly 28% of US greenhouse emissions, rolling back fuel economy standards is a dangerous setback for near-term climate goals," said Ben Scott, head of Energy Demand at UK-based climate think tank Carbon Tracker. "It risks keeping inefficient combustion engines on the road much longer than climate science demands," he told DW.

But following President Donald Trump's elimination of greenhouse gas emissions standards for light- and heavy-duty vehicles last year, Dave Cooke, senior vehicles analyst for the UCS Clean Transportation Program, said the rollbacks merely compound "a much more significant step backwards."

He said the trend sends "a specific signal to automakers," adding that "the United States is seeking to maximize oil usage to increase oil company profits."

This will be to the detriment of climate targets, with high-polluting oil contributing one-third of global fossil fuel emissions that continue to rise — along with temperatures.

The world just had the hottest August on record as severe heatwaves in Europe caused more than 16,000 excess deaths in Germany alone.

Sales of low emission EVs are down in the US in 2026, especially after Donald Trump scrapped the $7,500 tax credit for new electric vehicles.

American automakers also continue to push back against regulations that would incentivize a switch to EVs, arguing there is a lack of consumer demand.

"It's highly unlikely that the United States will suddenly become awash in electric options when neither the auto industry nor regulators seem interested in providing such choices for consumers," said Cooke.

But Ben Scott believes that while softening US fuel economy standards "will create temporary headwinds", it won't stall an inevitable transition to electric vehicles.

"With EVs delivering vastly lower running costs, electrification becomes a clear-cut economic choice," he said of cars running on "cheaper, domestically secure energy that will drive the transition forward, regardless of short-term politics."

In the moments which followed Mika Baur sweeping the ball into Celta Vigo’s net early in the second half, there was joy around Celtic Park. But you felt something was missing.

There was a lack of belief that the first goal of the night would be the last, a lack of trust in Martin O’Neill’s side to shut up shop and seal the three points.

Football supporters tend to be optimistic by nature yet they are not blinded to reality either. This Celtic side lost five goals away to LASK, three at home to Ferencvaros and four away to Benfica. They can look decent going forward yet they are routinely awful when asked to defend at that level.

You had to suspend reality to believe that a slick and accomplished Celta Vigo side would not score in the 43 minutes that remained.

The minute Javi Rodriguez netted one, the only question was when Celta would strike again. Three minutes was the answer, the defender the hero again for the visitors. Like the old Glasgow buses, Celtic concessions rarely come alone these days.

There was a feeling of acceptance among the home supporters that the game was up the instant the Spaniards got their noses in front. When you have seen so many similar stories unfold in recent times, it becomes harder to envisage an alternative ending.

Kieran Tierney holds his head in his hand as Celtic's players reflect on another European failure

Celta’s trip to Glasgow had evoked memories of how it used to be in these parts. Nearly 24 years ago, they had no response when Henrik Larsson netted in the first leg of the UEFA Cup tie.

In that run to the final, O’Neill’s side would claim the scalps of Blackburn, Liverpool and Stuttgart.

Around that time, Juventus, Lyon and Barcelona all left Parkhead defeated. After Gordon Strachan took charge, the names of Manchester United, Benfica, AC Milan and Shakhtar Donetsk were added to the list of the vanquished.

For a spell, Celtic Park was as tough a venue as there was in European football, the thunderous noise simultaneously inspiring the home side and spooking the opposition. It was truly a sight to behold.

For that dynamic to exist, though, supporters must have something to believe in. And, right now, the evidence of their own eyes offers very little by way of encouragement.

By time up on Thursday, you couldn’t help but compare and contrast the picture inside the ground to that of almost two years ago.

Under Brendan Rodgers, Celtic’s last Champions League campaign started with a 5-1 thumping of Slovan Bratislava.

After a chastening night in Dortmund, Celtic responded with a commendable draw away to Europa League holders Atalanta.

By common consent, the display Rodgers’ side produced in the 3-1 slaying of RB Leipzig on November 5 was the most complete in Europe’s premier competition in a generation.

With Celtic accruing enough points from Club Brugge, Dinamo Zagreb and Young Boys to progress, their prize was a shot at Bayern Munich.

While Rodgers’ men were ultimately eliminated by the Bavarians, their display in the Allianz was outstanding. It felt like a standard had been set which would see Celtic as regulars in the play-off round of the top-tier tournament, perhaps occasionally venturing further.

The demise to this point has been nothing short of staggering.

In their next outing, Celtic couldn’t score a goal in 210 minutes against Kairat Almaty as their Champions League ambitions for last season were ended.

They were picked apart by Braga, Midtjylland, Roma and Stuttgart in the Europa League.

Celtic's current woes are far removed from their outing against Bayern Munich two years ago

Since beating LASK at home in this year’s Champions League play-off, they’ve lost to the Austrians, Ferencvaros, Benfica and now Celta Vigo.

There was a time when Celtic could land a blow on the heavyweights of the European game. These days, they’re a punchbag for second and third-rate sides.

Anyone employed by the club who isn’t mortified by the current state of affairs certainly should be.

In the 36-team Europa League group, O’Neill’s side presently sit in 35th place. Only Ararat-Armenia, a club whose stadium capacity is listed as 1,428, sit beneath them. They are plankton in football’s ocean and they now have Celtic for company.

Sure, there are five games to go, 15 points to play for. But where is the form line to suggest that this Celtic team can stage the mother of all comebacks against the likes of Besiktas and Marseille?

It scarcely requires a deep insight to establish how such a desperate position has been arrived at so quickly.

Celtic’s predilection to selling its prized assets remains as strong as ever. From the team which performed so admirably in Munich, Nicolas Kuhn, Arne Engels and Daizen Maeda were flogged for big money. Kyogo Furuhashi had already been transferred.

Neither the Englishman nor Viljami Sinisalo have convinced. The same could be said of the majority of Celtic’s signings for long enough now.

On Wednesday, Shaun Maloney attributed the contrast between Celtic’s league and European results to the structure of the side rather than the individuals within it. In fairness, the club’s assistant manager could hardly claim anything else.

Few observers, though, put Celtic’s current travails in Europe down to a particular system and the players learning it as they go. It’s down to the dearth of quality both in the starting line-up and the bench.

Too many players who do a job domestically but who are routinely exposed when asked to step up a level. Chris Sutton recently labelled the team ‘flat-track bullies’ and you knew exactly where he was coming from.

You would not bet against Celtic winning the title again this season. They’ve done so in 14 out of the past 15 campaigns, after all. But domestic success no longer camouflages the wider picture of a club moving ever closer to being an irrelevance in Europe.

As a football club, Celtic has a history and a support which is the envy of most in the world.

Right now, though, at boardroom level, it badly lacks vision, strategy, ambition and humility. It requires modernising from top to bottom. It needs leadership and people taking responsibility when all it has is stuffed shirts and occasional rambling statements from a minority shareholder on the official website.

A football team on the park is always reflective of the functionality of those above them. It’s little wonder O’Neill’s outfit aren’t up to the job.

Ofir Haim managed various domestic teams in Israel before becoming head coach of the U19s and U20s

Football has long been the part of Israeli society where communities mix together who may not otherwise meet.

"The majority of the Jewish people who arrived in the mandate period [a spell between 1923 and 1948 where the region was run by Britain] emigrated from Europe," says Dr Omer Einav, a historian and author of Defending the Goal, a book on Israel's football history.

"They were Europeans and when you think about cinema, music, theatre, dance, they had nothing in common with Middle Eastern culture. Nothing, except football. That was the only common language, the only thing they both love in the same way."

Encouraged by the British administrators who ran the region, teams from different communities, and some made up of elements of each, would play against each other.

When the modern state of Israel was formed in 1948, much of the Arab population that remained inside its borders continued to identify as or with Palestinians. Most still do.

They are also an intrinsic part of Israeli football.

While they make up 20% of Israel's population, they represent about 35% of those involved in the sport at elite level nationally.

The first Arab player to represent Israel's national team was Rifaat Turk in 1976. In 2018, Bibars Natcho became the first Muslim to captain the team.

Turk was roundly abused. Natcho's decision not to sing Israel's national anthem, the lyrics of which relate to the Jewish search for a homeland, was condemned by some., external

For a short time in the summer of 2023, it seemed as if Haim's under-20 side might offer a watershed moment of unity and co-existence.

But football, like everything in Israel, changed on 7 October.

Israel came second in their group at the U20 World Cup and went on to finish third in the tournament

Matan Segal directed Kick It Out Israel, an anti-discrimination initiative with similar aims but no link to the English organisation of the same name, for almost four years before stepping down a few months ago.

Racism has long been present in Israeli club football. Beitar Jerusalem's fans are among the most notorious.

In 2013, ultras burned down their own club's offices in protest at the signing of two Chechen Muslims.

The most extreme Beitar fans have long taunted Israeli Arabs with chants of "may your village burn", while declaring themselves "forever pure".

However during his time at Kick It Out Israel, Segal saw racism against Arab players become more virulent and widespread.

Covid, during which fans radicalised online during lockdown, was one cause.

The election of Israel's far-right government at the end of 2022, legitimising views towards Palestinians that were previously seen as extreme, was a second.

"Before 7 October, people generally respected the Arab players," Segal said.

"Even though they had difficulties with them not singing the Israeli anthem, they respected them and they could talk about it.

"After 7 October though, we saw an increase in racist remarks.

"It was happening at small clubs where we hadn't seen it before. There were terrible songs - 'Let's kill all Arabs', 'There are no schools in Gaza because there are no children left'."

Football, inevitably, has become a focal point for emotion generated by the conflict.

Clubs which are usually bitter rivals came together to remember those killed by Hamas. Scarves were part of the commemoration at the site of the Nova music festival, where hundreds were killed.

Hapoel Jerusalem's club badge now features a silhouette of Hersh Goldberg-Polin, who was abducted and killed in captivity and whose parents have campaigned for peace through their grief.

"I wanted to continue. But sometimes there are reasons that are stronger than us that don't allow it."

Many in the Arab world would like more Palestinian prospects within Israel to follow the example of Ataa Jaber.

Jaber captained Israel's Under-21 side but switched nationalities, external to represent Palestine in June 2023, saying he could no longer separate sport and politics after seeing police evict Palestinian families from their homes in Israeli-occupied East Jerusalem.

Switching away from a high level of international football damaged his prospects of a move to Europe. At home, there were calls for his Israeli citizenship to be revoked. But, for Jaber, it was preferable to representing a country whose actions he deplored.

"To be Arabic in Israel, it's sometimes hard," he says. "Sometimes it is tough.

"Opportunities-wise, sometimes we are not on the same level."

"Time has to pass until you can touch certain areas, and at the moment you can see a lot of avoidance about talking about it, even mentioning it."

It may not. Translating youth-level promise to senior-level product is never simple.

"We saw Anan Khalaili and Hamza Shibli scoring against Brazil and that was our hope, that was the future. That was our future," says Einav.

"I'm not so optimistic, but people thought maybe it's really a magical thing that can help Israeli society in general.

The Amazon carbon sink is weakening as deforestation1,2,3,4,5, rising tree mortality6,7,8 and climate change disrupt the climatic conditions that sustain a humid tropical forest9,10,11. Forest loss reduces precipitation, increases temperatures and lengthens the dry season, driving higher carbon emissions1,2,3,4,11,12. Climate stress is stronger in more deforested regions, rendering forests progressively more flammable1,9,13. Large-scale climate events, such as El Niño, further amplify regional climate impacts9,14,15,16,17. Here we present an analysis of 15 years of Amazonian carbon fluxes derived from atmospheric CO2 measurements. We determine a carbon balance of 0.32 ± 0.08 PgC yr−1, with fires acting as the dominant source (0.40 ± 0.07 PgC yr−1) and forest uptake becoming increasingly unable to offset emissions. Furthermore, we investigate key drivers of the carbon emissions of Amazonia and identify three distinct year groups. Group 1 (2011–2014, 2017 and 2018) represents the role of the Amazon in the global carbon balance (0.11 ± 0.08 PgC yr−1) during wetter years with low human activity, such as deforestation, biomass burning and degradation. Group 2 (2019–2022) represents Amazon carbon emissions (0.43 ± 0.13 PgC yr−1) from human activities (mainly deforestation and degradation) during years with low climatic stress. Group 3 (2010, 2015, 2016, 2023 and 2024) has the highest carbon emissions (0.49 ± 0.07 PgC yr−1) and comprises warm and dry years (high climate stress), such as El Niño and the warming of the Tropical North Atlantic Ocean, associated with biomass burning.

Amazonia, the largest tropical forest in the world, has lost substantial carbon stocks due to large-scale deforestation and other land-use changes. Remaining forests in the region have offset some of this carbon loss through carbon sequestration processes. However, this capacity is declining, primarily because of increased tree mortality6,7,8,18 and changes in regional climatic conditions modified by deforestation1,3,9,13. The Amazon forest stores approximately 123 ± 23 billion tonnes of carbon (PgC) in vegetation and soils19,20. The interactions among the forest ecosystem, hydrological cycle and regional climate are complex, with atmospheric moisture recycling by evapotranspiration accounting for up to half of precipitation16. Direct human activities within Amazonia, including deforestation, forest degradation and biomass burning, have profoundly altered the carbon balance of the region and climate11. Forest loss reduces precipitation, raises temperatures and lengthens the dry season, delaying the onset of the wet season1,3,4,14,16. During periods of extreme climatic variability, such as El Niño or anomalously high Tropical North Atlantic (TNA) sea-surface temperatures (SSTs), these impacts are amplified through the interaction of accumulated forest loss, global warming, changing circulation and large-scale anomalies9,14,16.

Many questions remain about the main drivers of carbon emissions and variability in Amazonia. Studies based on modelling and bottom-up techniques show increased emissions during extreme climate (warm and dry) years, pointing to extreme events as the reason for increased carbon emissions. However, our direct atmospheric measurements show a more complex situation. Here, we use our 15-year history of Amazonian carbon fluxes derived from atmospheric CO2 measurements to answer important questions about the main factors driving increased carbon emissions and variability in the Amazon and whether they are caused by global extreme climate events or direct human activities (deforestation, biomass burning and degradation) in the Amazon.

In 2000, deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon represented a total area of 474,770 km2 (12.5% of the Brazilian Amazon, 3.8 × 106 km2). The Brazilian Amazon represents 58% of the Pan-Amazonia region (Fig. 1). By 2004, the PRODES programme21 of Brazil for monitoring native vegetation had recorded cumulative deforestation of 612,225 km2 across the Legal Amazon, equivalent to 15.5% of the original forest cover of the region. Between 2004 and 2012, the annual rate of deforestation fell 84% as a result of public policies designed to curb clearing22,23, reaching a cumulative loss of 697,319 km2 (18.4% of the total forest area). The rate began to rise again from 2013 to 2018, when total forest loss reached 19.5%, and accelerated sharply after 2019, reaching 791,835 km2 (20.9%) in 2022. Although the annual rate of deforestation declined strongly in 2023 and 2024 (30% and 47%, respectively) relative to the 2019–2022 period, cumulative forest loss still reached 807,301 km2 (21.3%). From 2010 to 2024, pasture area expanded by 19%, soybean plantation area by 193% and mining by 272% (ref. 24) to estimated values of 561,416 km2, 59,368 km2 and 3,956 km2, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1).

From 1979 to 2018, the changes in annual precipitation were not statistically significant (27 ± 69 mm); however, from 1979 to 2024, there was a reduction of 174 ± 69 mm, representing an 8% decrease. During the dry-season peak period (August–October), from 1979 to 2018, precipitation decreased by 17% (64 ± 19 mm), and from 1979 to 2024, by 20% (83 ± 18 mm). Between 2018 and 2024, Amazonia lost an additional 60,964 km2 of primary forest and 564,532 km2 of burned area, representing a significant increase in human activity impacting the region since 2019. Further discussion of changes in climatic conditions in Amazonia and the studied regions is provided in Extended Data Fig. 1, Extended Data Table 1 and Supplementary Discussion 1.

Regional analyses show that the most pronounced temperature increase occurred in the southeast region (ALF), with warming of 2.19 ± 0.21 °C during the dry-season peak and 1.37 ± 0.15 °C in the annual mean between 1979 and 2024. From 2018 to 2024, this region lost 37,930 km2 (62% of the total deforestation of the Brazilian Amazon) of primary forest. The most intense decline in precipitation happened in the northeast region (SAN). From 1979 to 2024, annual precipitation decreased by 304 ± 150 mm (12%), and during the dry-season peak, it declined by 37% (77 ± 23 mm). Over the same six-year period, SAN lost 15,023 km2 (25% of the total deforestation) of primary forest. More details are presented in Extended Data Fig. 1, Extended Data Table 1 and Supplementary Discussion 3.

This study includes 1,017 vertical profiles conducted between 2010 and 2024 at four Amazonian sites, whose upwind areas of influence represent nearly the entirety of Amazonia (Fig. 1 and Extended Data Fig. 2). Vertical profiles were typically performed twice per month, from 4.4 km above sea level (a.s.l.) to near the surface (about 0.3 km a.s.l.), sampling air at 12 heights in a descending helical trajectory at each of the four sites between 12:00 and 13:00 local time. Air samples were analysed for CO2 and CO at the Greenhouse Gas Laboratory (LaGEE/INPE), São José dos Campos, Brazil. The Amazon exhibits a dominant lower-tropospheric circulation pattern, with air masses originating over the tropical Atlantic Ocean, crossing the Amazon towards the Andes, then to the southern regions, and returning to the Atlantic in the southern regions. This circulation enables integration of both natural and anthropogenic processes by computing atmospheric mass balance across vertical profiles of sites. Here, we present Amazonian annual mean CO2 vertical profiles (VP; Fig. 2a), and the profiles differenced from the CO2 background (∆VP; Fig. 2b) for 2010–2024, calculated from the annual mean profiles at each site (Extended Data Fig. 3), using the same weighting methodology used for Amazonia carbon balance calculations1,13 (Methods). Most years show a positive ∆VP near the surface (Fig. 2b), indicating that Amazonia is a net carbon source rather than a sink and cannot offset regional anthropogenic emissions.

To better understand the variability in Amazonia annual carbon balance, we use k-means clustering analysis to group years, taking into account the following parameters: annual carbon balance (TCF), precipitation, temperature, deforestation (annual increment and accumulated), burned area (integrating all vegetation types)25, fire spots, Oceanic Niño Index26 (ONI) and TNA warming27. The cluster analysis identified the 15 years as belonging to three clusters: group 1 (2011, 2012, 2013, 2014, 2017 and 2018); group 2 (2019, 2020, 2021 and 2022); and group 3 (2010, 2015, 2016, 2023 and 2024). See the statistical analysis in Methods, Extended Data Fig. 4, Extended Data Tables 2 and 3, Supplementary Discussion 2 and Supplementary Table 1. Group 1 is characterized by wetter years and low human activities. Group 2 reflects a period with intense human activity—deforestation and degradation (biomass-burning, selective logging, mining and edge effects), and low climatic stress. Group 3 comprises years characterized by intense global climate events, such as El Niño and TNA events with associated biomass burning.

Group 1 (Fig. 2c, Extended Data Table 3a and Extended Data Fig. 5), representing the wettest years with low levels of human disturbance (deforestation and degradation), shows a near-neutral annual mean total carbon flux (TCF) of 0.11 ± 0.08 PgC yr−1 and ∆VP (VP – BKG) of 0.14 ppm, resulting from a carbon sink (−0.20 ± 0.12 PgC yr−1) that offsets 64% of fire emissions (0.32 ± 0.07 PgC yr−1). The annual deforestation and burned area were 6,389 km2 and 55,115 km2, respectively. This result indicates that Amazonia did not significantly contribute to the global carbon balance during wet years and with low human disturbance.

Group 2 years (Figs. 2c and 3, Extended Data Table 3b and Extended Data Fig. 5) were characterized by a period with higher levels of human disturbance and weak governance13. During this period, the annual deforestation rate rose by 97%, whereas the burned area increased by 25%, resulting in a TCF of 0.43 ± 0.09 PgC yr−1 and a ∆VP of 0.53 ppm, representing increases of 291% and 379% relative to group 1, respectively. The annual mean temperature was the same as in group 1, and precipitation was 134 mm (6%) lower (mainly due to the 2020 wet season), indicating low climatic stress during this period. In August 2020, a strong and prolonged La Niña event began and lasted until February 2023 (Fig. 4). During this period, carbon uptake gradually increased in the first quarter of each year (Fig. 4), offsetting an ever-greater proportion of anthropogenic emissions. As climate stress was very low during this period, it mainly represents the carbon emissions from human activity in Amazonia.

Quarterly mean TCF (black bars), ONI (cyan line)26 and TNA index (pink line)27 of Amazon. The positive dashed line indicates years with values exceeding 0.5, corresponding to extreme El Niño and TNA events.

Group 3 (Extended Data Table 3c and Extended Data Fig. 5) comprises years characterized by intense global climate events, such as El Niño and TNA warming. The mean ONI and TNA values for group 3 were higher than those for groups 1 and 2. Any years from groups 1 and 2 presented El Niño and/or warming TNA events. The El Niño events occurred in 2015–2016 (strong–very strong) and 2023 (strong; Fig. 3c), and the warming TNA events occurred in 2010 (moderate) and 2023–2024 (moderate–strong). We used the same ONI classification criterion28 for the TNA events (Methods). We also observed in Fig. 2c that the group 3 vertical profile showed an increase in the free-troposphere layer, unlike the other two; this is due to convective transport induced by the fire. Both El Niño and TNA warming contribute to reduced precipitation and increased temperatures in Amazonia. On average, group 3 received 308 mm (13%) less annual precipitation than group 1, with 20% less during the wet-season peak (January–March), and 17% less during the dry-season peak (August–October). The annual mean, wet-season peak and dry-season peak temperatures were higher than those of group 1 by 0.5 °C, 0.4 °C and 0.8 °C, respectively. These drought and warming conditions in Amazonia increased the flammability of the forest. We observed a 92% increase in burned area25 compared with group 1, whereas deforestation21 was 22% higher. TCF was 0.49 ± 0.07 PgC y−1, and ∆VP was 0.61 ppm, both of which were 325% higher than Group 1. However, for this group, NBE was −0.06 ± 0.10 PgC y−1 (nearly neutral sink), indicating that the main source of carbon is not the forest itself, but, again, human influence that promotes fires. Furthermore, recent climatic conditions interfere with the consequences of an intense El Niño year on the forest, as can be observed in 2023 and 2024 (Extended Data Tables 2 and 3 and Extended Data Fig. 7). FCF was 0.55 ± 0.10 PgC yr−1, 72% higher than group 1 and 53% higher than group 2, showing that fire emissions are an important characteristic of group 3. This finding highlighted the importance of clarifying the origin of fires, whether natural or anthropogenic. Therefore, a study was conducted to identify the relationship between lightning and forest fires in group 3. During the years of this group, it was found that lightning-induced ignition for fire events represented 1.6% and 1.0%, respectively, for the years 2023 and 2024 (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 6), allowing us to conclude that the emissions from group 3 originated primarily from human-induced fires, which were amplified by drought conditions. The years 2023 and 2024 were chosen to ensure gathering a more accurate cloud-to-ground lightning dataset, as higher-quality and more reliable data are only available from 2017 onwards.

These findings underscore the dominant role of direct human disturbance in Amazonia (Fig. 5, a schematic grouping summary), driving year-to-year changes in carbon fluxes and highlighting the urgent need for mitigation to prevent the approach of the region to a climatic tipping point29,30. Climatic stress amplifies the effects of human-induced fire, because drought conditions substantially increase forest flammability1,9,10,11,13,15,31,32,33.

The 2023 wet season exhibited the largest carbon sink over our study period of 15 years (Fig. 4 and Extended Data Fig. 7), which occurred after three consecutive La Niña years (Fig. 4). The 2023 Amazonia carbon balance was similar to that of group 1 (0.11 ± 0.09 PgC yr−1), because the wet-season sink offset 75% of the carbon emissions from deforestation and burned area, which were, respectively, 38% and 52% higher than in group 1. The annual mean temperature was similar to that of group 1, starting colder than normal and warming progressively as strong TNA and El Niño events became unprecedented drought and heat wave34,35 (Fig. 4).

While 2023 began after 3 years of La Niña, 2024 began in the middle of strong El Niño and TNA events, which made its outcome completely different. The year 2024 exhibited the highest carbon emissions (0.81 ± 0.09 PgC yr−1) in the 15-year record (Extended Data Tables 2 and 3 and Extended Data Fig. 7), 605% higher than that of group 1. It was also the hottest, driest and most strongly degraded by fire over the past two decades36. Annual temperatures were 1.2 °C higher than in group 1, and annual precipitation was 462 mm lower than in group 1. The burned area was 182% higher than in group 1, producing the most extensive burned area observed since 2010 in Amazonia (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Tables 2 and 3). Following the reinstatement of public policies to curb deforestation in 2023 (ref. 13), fire became a widespread tool for deforestation. In 2024, PRODES identified that 38% deforestation was caused by fire37. Most Amazonian wildfires are intentionally set, and severe droughts exacerbate their extent and intensity15,29,31,37.

More detailed subregional (RBA, ALF, SAN, TTC) analyses are provided in Extended Data Tables 2 and 3, Extended Data Figs. 2 and 3, Supplementary Discussion 3 and Supplementary Figs. 3–5.

The 15-year annual mean Amazonia carbon balance was 0.32 ± 0.08 PgC yr−1 (FCF, 0.40 ± 0.07 PgC yr−1; NBE, −0.08 ± 0.12 PgC yr−1). The pronounced interannual variability (−0.12 PgC yr−1 to 0.75 PgC yr−1) underscores the need for decadal-scale analyses to accurately define the role of Amazonia in the global carbon cycle, and it is also important to consider spatial representativeness, given the large variability across the subregions studied. Long-term deforestation and degradation have reduced annual precipitation by about 8% over the past 46 years and dry-season peak precipitation by about 20%, while increasing annual mean temperature by 1.13 ± 0.13 °C and dry-season temperature by 1.81 ± 0.18 °C. The grouping of years, in three distinct scenarios (Fig. 5), showed what Amazonia represents for the global carbon balance in each of these scenarios: (1) wet years, with low human activities (0.11 ± 0.08 PgC yr−1); (2) low climatic stress years with high human activities (0.43 ± 0.13 PgC yr−1); and (3) warm and dry years, associated with intense increase of fires, essentially human-caused (0.49 ± 0.07 PgC yr−1). It is important to note that groups 2 and 3 do not differ significantly in carbon emissions, and both are dominated by deforestation and biomass burning, respectively. It is also noteworthy that the NBE across all three groups is close to neutral, indicating that direct human activities are the main risks to the Amazon. Drastic fire reduction is therefore essential to avert a tipping point, especially as escalating climate stress amplifies human impacts, and vice versa. Based on the consequences of the very strong El Niño 2015–2016 in the southeast Amazon region, which has become an increasing source of carbon, similar consequences are expected after the strong El Niño and TNA events in 2023–2024 for Amazonia. These results highlight the urgent need to implement a zero-deforestation policy, strengthen controls on biomass burning and prioritize forest restoration programmes.

Here, we report CO2 and CO measurement results from the four Amazonian aircraft vertical profile sites of the CARBAM project38 (SAN: 2.86° S 54.95° W; ALF: 8.80° S 56.75° W; RBA: 9.38° S 67.62° W; in 2010–2012 for TAB: 5.96° S 70.06° W; and since 2013 for TEF: 3.39° S 65.60° W, and since 2022 for CZS: 7.00° S 72.50° W), detailed in refs. 1,13. The regions of influence for the four sites representing the northeast (SAN), southeast (ALF), southwest (RBA) and northwest Amazon region (TAB, 2010–2012; TEF, 2013–2021; CZS, 2022–2024) are shown in Extended Data Fig. 2c–h. Our samples were typically collected twice per month, totalling 1,017 vertical profiles over 15 years, in a descending spiral from 4,420 m to about 300 m a.s.l. In 2015, the vertical profile sampling was stopped in April at all sites and resumed in November at RBA. In 2016, vertical profiles were performed only at RBA and ALF. The vertical profiles were usually taken between 12:00 and 13:00 local time. Air samples were analysed by a non-dispersive infrared analyser for CO2 and by gas chromatography with HgO reduction detection for CO. The detailed analytical and sampling methods were presented in previous studies1,13.

We defined the Amazonia study area according to subregions from ref. 39 and biomes from ref. 40, in which the studied area in Amazonia was determined to include only the forest ecosystems subregions: Amazonia stricto sensu, Guianas and Gurupi, with a total area of 6,700,798 km2.

The annual mean VP for each site was calculated by first producing the monthly mean for each height and then the annual mean for each height. The annual mean ∆VP for each site and for Amazonia was calculated by subtracting the corresponding background (BKG) for each VP and each height (Fig. 2 and Extended Data Fig. 3). To calculate the Amazonia annual mean VP, we applied the same method used to obtain the Amazonia annual mean flux and balance. To scale to the whole of Amazonia, we divided it into three regions (see ref. 13 and Supplementary Fig. 5). To compose the VP for region 1 (SAN + ALF), we calculated the weighted mean CO2 concentration at each height, proportional to the area of each region. To calculate the VP for region 2 (RBA + TAB, 2010–2012; RBA + TEF, 2013–2021; RBA + CZS, 2022–2024), the same procedure was used as in region 1. And for VP of region 3, the remaining Amazonia area not covered by the regions of influence of the vertical profile, we used the same VP as region 2. To compose the VP for Amazonia, the weighted mean for each height CO2 concentration was calculated, considering the three regions for each height (Supplementary Fig. 5).

We used a column budget technique to estimate TCF, FCF and NBE, which consists of the difference between CO2 mole fraction measured in the vertical profile and the estimated background mole fraction41 (∆CO2), considering the travel time of air parcels along the trajectory from the coast to the site (equation (1)), following the methodology in refs. 1,13,42.

Background mole fractions were estimated according to the methodology described in ref. 41, using the geographical position of each air-mass back-trajectory from the HYSPLIT model43 when it intersects two virtual limits: (1) a latitude limit, from the Equator southwards at 30° W and (2) a line between two observation sites at Ragged Point, Barbados (RPB), and Ascension Island, the United Kingdom (ASC), from NOAA/GML. We used CO2 (ref. 44) and CO (ref. 45) concentrations from the NOAA/GML Carbon Cycle Cooperative Global Air Sampling Network to determine the background.

We calculated fire emissions according to the methods published in refs. 1,13. We used observed CO/CO2 ratios by site from 2010 to 2024 (CO/CO2, ppb CO per ppm CO2) from measured CO mole fractions. The CO/CO2 ratios were calculated from clearly identifiable plumes in the vertical profiles during the dry season along period study (39 VP ALF CO/CO2 = 60.7 ± 30.7 ppb ppm−1; 35 VP SAN CO/CO2 = 56.1 ± 20.4 ppb ppm−1; 44 VP RBA CO/CO2 = 67.7 ± 24.1 ppb ppm−1; and 19 VP TTC (TAB_TEF_CZS) CO/CO2 = 74.2 ± 29.1 ppb ppm−1), which represents how many ppb CO is emitted for each 1 ppm CO2 during burning process. To calculate CO2 from fire, we used the CO flux and subtracted it from natural CO emissions (equation (2)). After the step \({F}_{\mathrm{CO}}-{F}_{\mathrm{CO}}^{\mathrm{Natu}{\rm{r}}\mathrm{al}}\), we used the CO/CO2 ratio to estimate the amount of CO2 from fire. NBE represents all processes from natural sources and sinks and all anthropogenic non-fire emissions (or after fire, without CO emissions) (NBE = total − fire).

Flux uncertainties were estimated using Monte Carlo error propagation (1,000 iterations). CO2 measurement uncertainty was 0.03 ppm, and background (BKG) uncertainties were derived from residuals to the fitted CO2 smooth curve, according to ref. 1. Uncertainties in back-trajectory travel times13 were also included. For carbon fire emissions and NBE, uncertainties in CO/CO2 ratios, CO fluxes and a 25% uncertainty in natural CO emissions were considered. VP flux uncertainties were derived from the Monte Carlo ensemble. Monthly uncertainties were estimated using pooled standard deviation to account for intra-month variability within the annual mean flux, and period uncertainties were calculated using the quadratic mean.

An additional source of uncertainty is the limitation on sampling to 4.4 km a.s.l. Transit times of air parcels from the coast to sampling sites, based on HYSPLIT, can vary, with mean trajectory times of 2.6 days for SAN, 5.3 days for ALF, 6.6 days for RBA, 5.7 days for TAB, 5.0 days for TEF and 7.6 days for CZS. The convective process, together with long travel times of air masses, can represent loss of carbon sources and sink contributions from the surface, because our vertical profiles start at 4.4 km. Comparing the background concentration and the top of vertical profiles is one way to verify potential information loss1,13. According to the method we use, the flux is obtained by subtracting the measured CO2 mole fraction in the VP from the background concentration, accounting for the integration time (equation (1)). During the dry season, the loss of information is larger to positive (VP > BKG), because during the burning season (peak of the dry season), the top of VP starts with a higher CO2 concentration than BKG due to convective processes driven by biomass burning.

A previous study46 proposed a correction of our gas concentration measurements based on a comparison of laboratory analyses of samples collected on 12 vertical profiling flights above Manaus in 2017–2019. The in situ measurements were made with a cavity ring-down spectrometer (CRDS), and discrete flask samples (PFPs) collected on the same flights were analysed in the laboratory. Some of the continuous CRDS measurements differed from those determined in the laboratory from flask samples (PFP) collected on the same flights (Figure A4 in ref. 46). Based on linear regression of N = 158 paired CRDS-PFP measurements, ref. 46 reported that water vapour content explained only 13% of the variance in the CRDS-PFP differences (Figure A7 in ref. 46). Nevertheless, ref. 46 proposed applying this regression relationship to adjust all laboratory measurements. Applying such an adjustment would conflate multiple methodological differences between the two sampling and analytical systems. The regression in ref. 46 compares independent instruments sampling distinct air parcels under different pressures, flow rates and materials. The proposed correction assumes that the in situ CRDS measurements are accurate, that the laboratory analyses are biased, that discrepancies arise solely from water vapour, and that the regression relationship can be extrapolated to approximately 13,319 laboratory measurements collected over 15 years. For these reasons, the proposed adjustment was not applied in this study. LaGEE measurements used in this study are internally consistent across the full temporal and spatial domain.

An additional source of uncertainty is addressed in the calculation of fire carbon emissions and NBE. Over time, the forest is becoming drier and hotter. These changes promote a lower CO/CO2 ratio. The use of a single mean CO/CO2 ratio is another source of uncertainty, leading to lower fire emissions than those observed in recent years because the ratio tends to decline over time. It is likely that carbon emissions from fires were higher than our averages for 2023 and 2024 indicate, because both were drought years.

We used the missForest algorithm to impute missing monthly TCF and FCF data at the ALF, SAN, RBA and TAB_TEF_CZS (TTC) sites. These gaps resulted from sampling and logistical issues in the laboratory. The missForest method is a non-parametric imputation approach based on random forests47,48, and we implemented it in R language49 through the missForest package50. To estimate missing values for TCF and FCF1,13, we included the following monthly variables in the imputation process: temperature (T), precipitation (P), vapour pressure deficit (VPD), ONI, TNA index, EVI, burned area (BA) and fire spots (FS). The missing data gaps were imputed using average values calculated from 1,000 runs of missForest. The normalized root mean square errors obtained for FCTotal and FCFire were 0.0014 and 0.0012 for ALF, 0.0011 and 0.0010 for RBA, 0.0055 and 0.0053 for SAN and 0.0018 and 0.0016 for TTC, respectively. NBE missing data were calculated by subtracting the fire from TCF.

We define regions of influence as those areas covered by the density of back-trajectories51. We updated the calculations for the regions of influence used in our published CO2 papers1. In this study, the regions received two treatment steps. In the first step, we calculated back trajectories for altitudes lower than 1,300 m. As we have only four heights below 1,300 m, the density of trajectories could be reduced, compromising the representativeness. To better characterize atmospheric circulation over the quarters and years, we calculated trajectories at each sampling height (>1,300 m) twice a week, at the same locations and time of day, over the 15 years for each VP sampling site. A second treatment in the grid cells was done to increase the weight for the grid cells near the sample site. We normalized the weight for the region of influence to be from 0 to 1. First, the grid with a value of 1 was calculated by disregarding 2.5% of the grids with the highest values, thereby increasing the area of the grid region with a value of 1. It was considered a regular grid of 1° × 1° generated in the WGS 84 georeference system (EPSG:4326).

We used the quarterly regions of influence maps as spatial weighting functions for all studied parameters to determine how each parameter influenced the carbon flux and for the missing data imputation process (missForest), following ref. 4. We used the GPCP database (http://eagle1.umd.edu/GPCP_ICDR/GPCP_Monthly.html), v.1.3, for precipitation analysis (described in refs. 52,53,54), which contains daily data at a 1° × 1° latitude–longitude resolution. We used 2-m temperatures from ERA-5 that are monthly means of daily means since 1959, and which were used with a resolution of 0.25° × 0.25° latitude–longitude, obtained from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF)27,55. The VPD product is a measure of the indirect vapour pressure deficit in kPa (resolution of 2.5 arcmin) of monthly means of temperature and humidity, provided by the Climatic Research Unit Ts4.0 (ref. 56). The dataset was resampled to a 1° × 1° spatial resolution using the monthly mean. The EVI is a vegetation index that aims to estimate the fraction of photosynthetically active radiation (fPAR) from terrestrial vegetation. The EVI product used57 was the MANVI: Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) multiangle implementation of atmospheric correction nadir-solar (see the methods in ref. 13).

Two products of burned area (BA) were used: (1) for all annual burned areas, the BA from MapBiomas was used, integrating all types of vegetation that were burned (primary and secondary forest, pasture, agriculture and others), from the MapBiomas Fire Collection 4 (ref. 25; https://brasil.mapbiomas.org). MapBiomas Fire was validated with a global accuracy of 89% (ref. 33); (2) for monthly BA (used only for missForest calculation), MODIS products were used: MODIS Collection 6 MCD64A1 burned area product13,58.

The procedures to retrieve deforestation as geographic data built by PRODES/INPE21 based on a historical series of Landsat images provide deforestation annual increments in the Brazilian Amazon. Detailed information on the PRODES methodology is available and can be accessed at http://urlib.net/rep/8JMKD3MGP3W34R/443H3RE (ref. 59). The dataset accuracy is 93.5 (ref. 60). We used the data period from 2010 to 2024. Using QGIS software, a 0.25° × 0.25° grid was generated for the entire Brazilian Amazon, which was filled with the absolute values of deforested area calculated for each cell and each year of the series. The mean deforestation area was calculated for the period 2010–2024 within each grid cell. Annual deforestation data correspond to the deforested area mapped by the PRODES project in a given year for Amazon forests.

Monthly deforestation and degradation data correspond to either the deforestation alerts (clear cut, deforestation with vegetation, and mining) or degradation alerts (ordered selective logging, disordered selective logging, burns and degradation) captured by the DETER61 Amazon system in a given month. This dataset is produced daily by the visual interpretation of medium spatial resolution optical imagery of National Institute for Space Research (INPE) and, although not a full estimate of deforestation and degradation in the Amazon, can indicate variations in deforestation patterns over time33,62. We used the raw version of the dataset provided by INPE that included both password-protected data and data collected from August 2015.

Fire spots in Pan-Amazonia between 2010 and 2024 and burned area in Brazilian Amazon were retrieved from the BDQueimadas wildfire monitoring program of INPE63 (see the methods in ref. 13).

Warm (El Niño) and cold (La Niña) periods based on a threshold of ±0.5 °C for the ONI, 3-month running mean of ERSST v.5 SST anomalies in the Niño 3.4 region (5° N–5° S, 120° W–170° W), based on centred 30-year base periods updated every 5 years (ref. 26). To analyse the intensity of the events, we used the ONI classification anomaly28—for El Niño events, weak (0.5–0.9 °C), moderate (1.0–1.4 °C), strong (1.5–1.9 °C), and very strong (≥2.0 °C).

The TNA index was computed as the mean sea-surface temperature (SST) anomaly over the region 23.5° N–5.5° N and 57.5° W–15° W (ref. 64). Monthly SST fields were obtained from ERA5 (0.25°), and anomalies were derived relative to a 30-year climatological baseline (1971–2000), consistent with standard TNA calculations27. We used the same ONI anomaly classification to analyse the intensity of the TNA warming events.

Annual data (2010–2024) for the Amazon included TCF, precipitation (P), temperature (T), ONI, TNA index, fire spots (FS), burned area (BA), annual deforestation (DEF) and accumulated deforestation (DEF_acc). Variable distributions and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests (Supplementary Table 1), respectively. Although most variables met normality assumptions, robust statistical approaches were adopted because of the small sample size (n = 15) and potential deviations from Gaussian behaviour typical of environmental datasets (Supplementary Discussion 3). Principal component analysis was applied to standardized data, with the first two components considered to represent the dominant variability. The k-means clustering (k = 3) was performed in the principal component analysis space, with cluster characteristics summarized in a standardized heatmap. The optimal number of clusters was determined using the average silhouette width (Extended Data Fig. 4).

Group differences among clusters were assessed using Welch’s analysis of variance (Supplementary Table 1), which is robust to heteroscedasticity and unequal sample sizes. When significant differences were detected (P < 0.05), pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Games–Howell post hoc test (Supplementary Table 1), which does not assume equal variances and appropriately controls the type I error rate under these conditions. This approach provides a consistent and reliable framework for identifying differences among clusters in environmental variables (Supplementary Discussion 2). All analyses were conducted in R language49.

We obtained annual estimates of pasture, soybean cropland and mining area across Amazonia from the MapBiomas Collection 1024 dataset (30 m spatial resolution). Annual land-cover maps were used to isolate class-specific pixels (pasture, soybean and mining) and to compute their corresponding areas (km2) within the Amazonia carbon study area. This approach provided a consistent time series of land-use expansion and persistence for 2000–2024.

After 2017, in group 3, the years with accurate data were 2023 and 2024, when a more accurate cloud-to-ground lightning dataset was available. We used cloud-to-ground strokes from the Earth Networks Total Lightning Network (ENTLN)65. In South America, particularly in Amazonia, ENTLN lacks a dense sensor network comparable to that in other regions of Brazil. Even so, it operates with a stroke detection efficiency above 50% (corresponding to a flash detection efficiency above 80%) and a mean location accuracy below 1 km. Just for reference, in regions with high sensor densities, after the 2021 upgrade, the ENTLN reaches cloud-to-ground flash detection efficiency levels above 95% and median location accuracy below 100 m (ref. 66). Although performance depends on regional sensor coverage, ENTLN is considered one of the most advanced and reliable lightning networks globally67. Active fires used in this study were provided by the Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite on the Suomi National Polar-Orbiting Partnership satellite, with a spatial resolution of 375 m. The Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite 375 m active fire product provides geolocation accuracy of the order of one pixel (about 375 m), with typical sub-pixel geolocation uncertainties (about 70 m), ensuring reliable spatial representation of fire locations. All overpasses, day and night, were used from the Wildfire Monitoring Program of the INPE, available at http://www.inpe.br/queimadas/ (ref. 63). To assess lightning-associated wildfire among all cloud-to-ground lightning strokes, we identified probable lightning candidates that occurred up to 72 h (3 days) before active fire detection, using a fixed buffer radius of 1 km around each active fire and accounting for the location accuracy of both datasets. A detailed description of lightning candidate characteristics in Brazil is provided in ref. 68.

#1184. The part-timers who won three Wembley finals in a row

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

The part-timers who won three Wembley finals in a row

Question: Which two men's club sides have won three successive competition finals at the new Wembley?

You probably knew Pep Guardiola's Manchester City won three League Cups on the bounce between 2018 and 2020 before adding a fourth in 2021 â but another side did the silverware hat-trick first.

Whitley Bay, who play in the ninth-tier Northern Football League, won three successive FA Vase finals between 2009 and 2011 â an unprecedented achievement in non-league football.

The FA Vase, a national competition for amateur teams, was established in 1974; and in more than half a century The Seahorses are the only ones to win it four times â including those three on the trot.

And they did so under the eye of an unassuming IT manager from Sunderland, who used "quiet moments in the day" to plan historic cup success.

Ian Chandler, who had three spells at Whitley Bay as a player, became manager in 2004 and spent 10 years in charge.

It was no mean feat, as any non-league manager will tell you. Instead of focusing on work meetings, Chandler says he spent more time sorting hotel bookings or discussing scouting reports.

His team were inspired by missing out on Wembley in 2007-08, when Whitley Bay reached the FA Vase semi-finals. They lost 4-0 to Lowestoft Town in the first leg, and despite going 3-0 up inside 20 minutes of the return fixture at Bay's Hillheads Park stadium they were unable to overturn the deficit.

After that elimination, Chandler developed a superstition of never again giving pre-match interviews - as the last time he did so was before the 4-0 defeat.

Whether because of his renewed focus, or because of a desire among players to make amends, it steeled the group to make history.

"It's the hardest thing to keep a team together - as soon as players get noticed, they get picked up at higher level," Chandler tells BBC Sport.

"But after we got to the Vase semis, players wanted to stay and prove what they could do.

Ian Chandler played for Barnsley, Stockport and Aldershot before a successful non-league career

Despite being a lifelong Sunderland fan, Chandler admits basing his team on Kevin Keegan's Newcastle - with entertainment even placed above winning.

"There was a standing joke in the team, two or three at the back? One time we chased a game with two at the back," he says.

"The Premier League can be so boring; fans want to be entertained, that's what we promised."

Chandler was already a Whitley Bay hero when he was appointed manager by chairman Paul McIlduff, having scored the winner in the 2002 FA Vase final against Tiptree United at Villa Park.

After a slow start as the then-35-year-old with no prior managerial experience found his bearings, Chandler built a talented team which became the envy of non-league football nationwide.

"We played good football. We had pace on the wing, fantastic midfielders, and strikers who were deadly," says McIlduff, who has been Whitley Bay chairman since 2001.

"When we brought them in, it was about fitting the psychology of the dressing room. No-one thought they were better than anyone else, they played for each other."

A growing influence in the side was the 'Whitley Bay Maradona'.

Paul Robinson came through Newcastle United's academy before a career spent across various non-league clubs in the north-east â including Whitley Bay from 2007 to 2013.

Like several other players and staff, he worked with Chandler both in his day job at software company Sage and at Hillheads Park.

"He was my manager at work and football, I was sick of him," he tells BBC Sport. "But it helped get time off for matches."

And it meant he also had an understanding boss if his mind drifted at work.

"Zoning out happened regularly, picturing scoring and winning, what the opposition would be like - hoping they were rubbish so we could have an easy stroll," he says.

Perhaps Robinson's greatest Whitley Bay moment came in the 2010 FA Vase semi-final second leg, when his last-minute diving header in the driving rain secured a 6-5 aggregate victory over Barwell, sparking a pitch invasion.

"Everyone goes to the finals, you were there, you could enjoy it," says Robinson, who is now academy head at South Shields.

"But the rounds before were the bigger games. At home, we would get 2,000 or 3,000 at every game. You felt like a real player. You associate those memories with 90s FA Cup you never think it will happen in non-league."

Chandler played in a 2-0 win over Preston in round two as they reached the FA Cup third round for the first and only time in the 1989-90 season, and they also progressed to round two the following campaign.

Those seasons of success - made The Bay a feared side for non-league clubs across England, and meant they were ready for the big games in a way most amateur sides are not.

"One quarter-final against Biggleswade [in 2009], they turned up in suits and ties," says Robinson. "We thought, this is their day out, their cup final. We won 5-2.

"By the end, teams were turning up afraid of us. We had it won beforehand."

Paul Robinson scored the fifth goal in Whitley Bay's 6-1 win over Wroxham in the 2010 FA Vase final

Off the pitch, the team's success had an influence on the whole of Whitley Bay. A working-class town near Newcastle, which had its heyday as a Victorian-era holiday destination, sporting success brought local pride to the north-east coast.

And like any lower league club, that success could only be supported by dedicated volunteers, who went into overdrive before the Vase finals.

They would go door to door across shops in Tynemouth, North Shields and Whitley Bay to raise support. A van selling tickets was parked outside Woolworths on the high street.

"There was such excitement around the whole of Whitley Bay - every shop had things in the windows, the blue and white stripes," says one of the volunteers, Jenn Robinson.

She got involved because her late husband Mike was a club board member and if she wanted to see him she "had to go around there".

"Everyone wanted to come and watch, we got so many local sponsors," adds Jenn.

"I still do buffets and things for players and long-standing fans. We have a game tonight, and I'm making the sandwiches now."

Newcastle United helped out with selling tickets for the finals, while North Tyneside Council provided security services as thousands flocked to Hillheads Park for big cup games.

But even the best laid plans can go awry.

"We organised a train down to London, the guarantee was that it would go straight to Wembley," says McIlduff. "The first time it worked, second time they were taken to Kings Cross - we had to organise taxis up to Wembley."

By hook or by crook, The Seahorses army made it three times to the national stadium. Some even got royal treatment.

"It was absolutely surreal, and a delight," Jenn says. "The first Wembley final we played at, it had just opened. My husband being on the board, we sat in the royal box, which was fabulous.

"One of the security guys showing me to our seats said, 'Miss Robinson, this is Prince William's seat'. He hadn't even sat in it at that point."

Thousands of fans travelled from Whitley Bay to London across the three finals

Chandler, whose son Benjamin was a mascot at all three finals, said it was an overwhelming experience at times for the players too.

"The first year was crazy, everything was brand new," he says. "The first year the bus driver scraped the side up of the bus driving into the ground.

"The second year we tried to take everything in, all the lads had their mobiles. Then the third year, it was just a case of can we do it, win three in a row."

And three times, Whitley Bay rose to the occasion.

After beating Glossop North End 2-0 in 2009 in front of 12,200 supporters, Whitley Bay returned a year later to thrash Wroxham 6-1.

The opening goal was scored by prolific striker Paul Chow after only 21 seconds. At the time this was the quickest goal in a Wembley final and was only surpassed 13 years later when Ilkay Gundogan scored for Manchester City inside 13 seconds in the FA Cup final.

The hat-trick was completed in 2011 with a frantic 3-2 win over Coalville Town, who equalised twice before Chow was again the hero with an 86th-minute winner.

In total, nearly 30,000 fans were in the stands across the three finals.

After each final, Whitley Bay celebrated in The Green Man pub just 15 minutes' walk from the famous Wembley arch.

"In the first year we just went back to the bar and it was an anti-climax," Robinson says. "The second and third years the club got their act together and we rented a room; families, friends and fans were invited, they were brilliant nights.

"In 2010, we actually spent 10 days together - the week of the final we spent as a squad preparing, then we went to Marbella for five nights. Best 10 days of my life!"

The 2010 trophy lift was perhaps the most emotional though, as the players were joined by former team-mate Mark Taylor. He had played in the previous year's final, before being diagnosed with motor neurone disease, forcing an early retirement.

"Mark used to come on the away trips after he got diagnosed, he just wanted to be Mark - we had a good laugh everywhere we went," says Chandler. "He's still knocking about, abusing me on Facebook. He was the core of our second and third year."

Whitley Bay lift the trophy in 2010, joined by former team-mate Mark Taylor (left)

Success ensured the lives of Chandler and his team were never quite the same again. Reporters turned up to Sage to speak with the manager and his players; even Chandler's mum was interviewed by BBC Radio Newcastle legend Paul Dixon.

Chandler - and his wife - even got up close and personal with managerial icons.

"I was invited to the annual sports awards in Manchester. There were people there like Sven Goran Eriksson and Sir Alex Ferguson [and] numerous others," he says.

"I had my wife with me and Sven came over gave her a peck on the cheek - she wasn't aware of who it was until I told her later that evening."

Robinson admitted that going back to work at an IT company after Wembley glory was a "massive comedown".

"One year I got a standing ovation when I walked into the office, but after that it was just back to normal," he says.

"People wind you up about it - 'are you talking about Wembley again? Give it a rest Robbo!"

Like all good things, Whitley Bay's golden era came to an end. Chandler departed in 2014 after a dip in form; the club have not won a major honour since the 2011 FA Vase and remain in the ninth tier.

But the memories in the football-mad north-east remain.

"Me and my wife went out on a Sunday at a pub in Durham just a few weeks back, and this guy came up out the blue and asked, 'Are you Ian Chandler?'" he laughs.

"Then he started talking about that team; in the north-east, everyone follows football. It was so lovely."

It is not easy when the Premier League is full of fans who love seeing hard tackles, aggro between opponents, and end-to-end football. 'It is really difficult,' Alonso conceded. 'You know that if you concede one, the energy is coming, and you need to control the momentum. You can have a mental preparation. You need to have composure to deal with those moments.

'To say "Come on guys, we know what we have to do, we have prepared for this and the other moment will come' for me is fundamental." That is from the people on the pitch.

'We are expecting a good atmosphere, electric, with good energy, knowing that in a derby this passion is really in the air in the stadium. We need to use it in a proper way because this is all about those games where you feel that excitement.'

Mike Penders will make his first Premier League start in goal with Emiliano Martinez sidelined. Alonso backed the 21-year-old to handle the pressure and compared him to Thibaut Courtois. Chelsea's manager added he phoned the Real Madrid goalkeeper in the summer to receive intel on his Belgian compatriot Penders, saying: 'Thibaut even said himself that he could see resemblances with Mike.'
