#720. NWSL drops controversial criteria around ‘Rodman Rule’ in settlement with NWSLPA

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Any player will be eligible for High Impact Player status

Requirements had included top 100, marketability lists

Sides also resolve grievance on Rodman contract refusal

The NWSL and the NWSL Players Association have reached a settlement to drop the restrictive criteria around the controversial High Impact Player (HIP) rule, allowing any player to earn the league’s highest salaries.

The NWSL introduced the rule late last year, allowing teams to spend an additional $1m above the salary cap to sign star players. Players with the designation had to meet one of eight criteria, ranging from US national team minutes and Ballon d’Or recognition to NWSL Best XI honors to media lists, including the Guardian’s Top 100 and a ranking that ordered athletes by marketability.

The NWSLPA opposed the mechanism and filed a grievance, arguing that the classification of players was a violation of federal labor law and the existing collective bargaining agreement. About 100 players had qualified for the designation based on the criteria – a total that, at the time the rule was introduced, included the likes of Trinity Rodman and Sophia Wilson but would have excluded such players as two-time NWSL MVP Sam Kerr and US star Catarina Macario.

Under the settlement, which was announced Friday, the eligibility criteria were eliminated. Beginning 1 January 2027, clubs will be able to designate any player as a High Impact Player, so long as their salary equals at least 12% of the base salary cap. The league can also set a limit on the number of HIP players per club.

The two sides settled six grievances in total, including one over the rejection of a contract proposal between the Washington Spirit and Rodman. Another involved the league’s footwear policy, which had dictated what brands players could wear during games.

“This settlement is certainly evidence of the NWSLPA’s strength and commitment to enforcing the terms of the Collective Bargaining Agreement,” NWSLPA director Meghann Burke told ESPN, which was the first to report news of the settlement.

“Disagreements are a natural part of any mature labor relationship, particularly in a league that is growing and evolving as quickly as ours,” NWSL executive vice-president Bill Ordower said in a statement. “What matters is how you work through them. This agreement gives the players, clubs and league greater clarity and certainty moving forward, while protecting the integrity and competitive balance that is fundamental to the NWSL.”

The initial version of the HIP Rule was introduced by the league last December. It allowed teams to spend $1m above the salary cap – for the 2026 season, set at $3.7m – to keep star players amid a wave of talent departing for Europe, where teams are not bound by salary caps.

The rule was ratified by the league’s board of governors and went into effect on 1 July. It became known informally as the “Rodman Rule” because its introduction coincided with the Washington Spirit star’s prolonged contract saga this past offseason.

Rodman had received lucrative offers from teams in Europe that the Spirit could not have matched under the NWSL’s salary cap rules. In November, before the HIP rule was introduced, the Spirit attempted to re-sign Rodman to a deal that would have averaged more than $1m annually, but NWSL commissioner Jessica Berman blocked the offer and said it violated the league’s rules because of its backloaded structure. The NWSLPA filed a grievance on Rodman’s behalf, calling the refusal a “flagrant violation” of player free agency rights.

Weeks later, the league introduced the HIP rule. Rodman re-signed with the Spirit in January on a record deal worth more than $2m annually.

The union grievance over the initial refusal of Rodman’s contract remained open after she signed the new contract. While teams are not required to disclose who is signed using the HIP mechanism, several players who would have been eligible under the criteria have since been signed or re-signed by teams in the league, including Denver’s Lindsey Heaps, Orlando’s Barbra Banda and Gotham’s Rose Lavelle.

The league will still have the ability to reject contracts for salary cap circumvention, but it must notify the union of the decision and provide an explanation within 72 hours. The league will also be required to provide salary cap and revenue updates to its clubs throughout the year.

The settlement also addressed the league’s footwear policy. Throughout this season, some players have received warnings and fines for wearing cleats from brands that were not official sponsors. The league will now honor pre-existing endorsement deals for most players and withdraw previous fines.

On Sept. 30, NASA formally concluded OSIRIS-REx, the historic first U.S. mission to pluck a pristine piece of an asteroid and deliver it safely back to Earth.

The van-sized OSIRIS-REx spacecraft — whose name is short for Origins, Spectral Interpretation, Resource Identification, Security-Regolith Explorer — visited and sampled Bennu, an ancient space rock holding pristine remnants from the dawn of our solar system more than 4.5 billion years ago. Among those cosmic relics are raw organic building blocks, the kind that may have seeded life on Earth.

"I have known this day was coming for a long time, but it is still difficult to see it arrive," OSIRIS-REx Principal Investigator Dante Lauretta, who's based at the University of Arizona, said in a post on LinkedIn. "I feel enormous pride in what we accomplished together, and real sadness that this journey has come to an end."

For the mission team, the farewell capped a decade of high-stakes engineering, on-the-fly troubleshooting and nail-biting close calls that ultimately delivered to Earth the largest asteroid sample ever collected in space, one that held secrets from the early solar system.

Long before launch, the team faced obstacles that nearly thwarted the endeavor — chiefly, two rejections from NASA — before finally securing the green light for the $1 billion project under the New Frontiers Program in 2011.

When OSIRIS-REx launched on Sept. 8, 2016, on a two-year journey across the inner solar system, Bennu was little more than a point of light in telescopes. Scientists expected the asteroid’s surface to be a smooth, sandy sphere where OSIRIS-REx could seamlessly touch down. Much to their surprise, however, when the probe arrived at the asteroid in late 2018, it revealed Bennu to be a treacherous, loosely bound amalgamation of jagged boulders.

"Our expectations about the asteroid's surface were completely wrong," Lauretta said in an Oct. 7 statement from the University of Arizona. "There was no obvious place to collect a sample anywhere."

When OSIRIS-REx touched down on a small crater named Nightingale, Bennu offered so little structural resistance that scientists said its surface reacted more like fluid than a hard, rocky shell. The spacecraft's sampling head sank directly into the asteroid, stopping after burrowing 20 inches (51 centimeters) deep by firing its thrusters at the right time to pull away, with the precious sample in hand.

Then came another nail-biting moment: The sample collector lid got jammed in a partially open position because so much material was scooped up, allowing fragments of the precious cargo to escape. In a race against time to prevent further loss, the mission team quickly stowed the sampler head directly inside the return capsule, securing the cosmic treasure for its journey back to Earth.

Even the final return to Earth, on Sept. 24, 2023, kept the team on edge: A parachute failed to deploy properly during reentry, forcing a tense but ultimately safe landing in the Utah desert.

"Successful exploration missions are not those that avoid surprises; they are those that are deliberately designed to adapt to them," Lauretta said in the statement, quoting a line from the mission's final report to NASA.

"That certainly describes OSIRIS-REx," he said. "Again and again, the mission presented us with circumstances we had not expected, and again and again, this team found a way forward."

The mission's triumphs began long before the samples reached Earth. For example, the probe set a Guinness World Record when it slid into orbit around the 1,610-foot-wide (490 meters) Bennu, which became the smallest object ever circled by a spacecraft.

The scientific payoff that followed included remote-sensing work that confirmed Bennu's surface is coated in carbon-bearing compounds. Those observations also revealed that Bennu was once part of a much larger, water-rich parent asteroid that shattered in a catastrophic collision in the main asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter.

The returned sample — which weighs 4.29 ounces (121.6 grams) here on Earth, more than twice the mission's requirement — continues to thrill and occupy scientists.

A wave of research papers, now topping 200, continues to piece together the composition and history of the ancient space rock. Among other findings, Bennu's pristine material has revealed amino acids and sugars that, on Earth, are essential for life, alongside a history of exposure to liquid water that could have served as the "broth" for these compounds to interact.

More discoveries are likely to come. Most of the returned Bennu sample is being preserved for future generations of scientists, who will study it "with questions and instruments we cannot yet anticipate," Lauretta said in the statement.

Though its OSIRIS-REx chapter is closed, the spacecraft itself is far from retired. Renamed OSIRIS-APEX (OSIRIS-APophis EXplorer), the probe is using its remaining propellant to head toward Apophis, another near-Earth asteroid and a different type of space rock from Bennu. It is scheduled to arrive in 2029.

#717. Trump's diesel deal with Russia is unlikely to significantly lower global fuel prices despite expectations of rapid relief

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

As the world's largest diesel exporter, the US benefits from high prices. At home, however, consumers are feeling the strain of rising living costs. Why Donald Trump's deal with Russia is unlikely to change that.

With or without a deal with Russia, diesel prices on global markets are unlikely to fall significantly anytime soon.

That is the assessment of experts interviewed by DW regarding the latest fuel-supply agreement between US President Donald Trump and Russian President Vladimir Putin.

"The main reason for high diesel prices is the war in Iran and the simultaneous disruption of shipping through the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab al-Mandeb Strait," said Samina Sultan, a foreign trade expert at the German Economic Institute (IW) in Cologne.

The situation has led to production outages and made fuel shipments "more expensive, less secure and more difficult," she said.

Trump's deal with Russia also has an additional geopolitical dimension, Sultan said: "It causes the wars in Ukraine and Iran to become completely intertwined."

Things are looking up, though. Roughly 13.5 million barrels of crude oil are once again flowing through the Strait of Hormuz a day, according to shipping and commodities analytics firm Kpler.

But for refined products such as diesel, volumes remain far lower at around 677,000 barrels per day, compared with 3.6 million barrels before the war.

Carsten Fritsch, a commodities analyst at Commerzbank, also attributes the price increases to the wars.

"If nothing changes in Ukraine and the Middle East, I see very few ways to resolve these supply bottlenecks," he said.

In a social media post on October 9, Trump announced that he would temporarily suspend some sanctions on Russia and begin importing Russian diesel.

He wrote "diesel prices for Americans and, indeed, the world, will be COMING DOWN, IN RECORD NUMBERS, AND FAST!" in a mix of lowercase and all caps.

Russia confirmed the arrangement. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov told Russian state news agency TASS on October 13 that supplying diesel fuel was "in the interests of Russia, the United States and global energy markets."

Diesel prices in the US and many EU countries have risen sharply over the past 12 months.

According to the US Energy Information Administration (EIA), prices in the US have climbed by about 60% and now stand at $6.30 per gallon, equivalent to $1.66 (or €1.49) per liter of diesel.

But even if Russia were able to flood global markets with cheap diesel, prices would not fall nearly as much as they recently rose, something all of the experts contacted by DW agree on.

Many refineries around the Gulf region have suffered disruptions or damage, while transporting fuel via shadow fleets and securing insurance remains costly, cumbersome and risky.

Another key shift is that Russia is no longer the world's largest diesel exporter. In fact, it is facing its own fuel shortages at home.

The US is now the world's biggest diesel exporter. In 2025, it exported an average of 1.26 million barrels of diesel per day, accounting for roughly 15.4% of global diesel exports.

At the same time, Russia fell to second place with a 9.5% share, and that was before major Ukrainian drone attacks on its refineries.

Besides the new US-Russia diesel agreement, Trump also recently floated the idea of US diesel export restrictions. But the idea was abandoned because it could lead to even higher diesel prices in the country, according to Samina Sultan.

"There simply is not enough diesel storage capacity in the United States," Sultan said. "Production would have to be reduced, and then prices would rise again."

If the US were to impose export restrictions on diesel, buyers in Latin America and the European Union would be hit particularly hard. Since Russia invaded Ukraine, EU countries have increasingly imported diesel from the US instead of Russia because of sanctions.

"An export ban merely redistributes the shortage; it does not solve it," said ING chief economist Carsten Brzeski.

"Global prices would continue to rise, while refineries would scale back production as margins shrink," he added.

Export restrictions aside, few people consider that the US-Russia diesel deal will have any big impact.

According to calculations by the platform The World Data, the promised shipment of 2.2 million barrels of diesel from Russia would not even cover a single day's US diesel demand of roughly 4 million barrels.

Even if all of the diesel volumes announced by Trump were combined, totaling 35.8 million barrels, they would only cover about nine days of US domestic demand.

"I CANNOT OVERSTATE HOW MUCH OF A NOTHING BURGER THIS IS," Canadian oil market analyst Rory Johnston wrote in an all-caps post on X shortly after the announcement.

Russia would normally export almost 10 times that volume if its refinery fleet was not being targeted by Ukrainian drone attacks, he added.

Canada also supplies diesel to the United States. Roughly 80% of all US diesel imports come from its northern neighbor.

Given Trump's verbal attacks on Canada and the ongoing trade dispute, analysts view the prospect of Canada supplying significantly more diesel to the US as highly unlikely.

#715. Modern neuroscience is rediscovering a surprising idea freud had 130 years ago

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

What if the human brain works in ways that Sigmund Freud began describing more than 130 years ago? A new scientific analysis suggests that some of his ideas about the mind share surprising similarities with one of the most influential theories in modern neuroscience.

Published in the scientific journal Entropy, the paper explores connections between Freud's original theories, later developments in psychoanalysis, and a modern approach to brain research known as the prediction paradigm.

This approach describes the brain as a prediction machine. Rather than simply responding to information from the outside world, the brain is constantly anticipating what will happen next. It then compares those expectations with incoming information from the senses and works to reduce any differences between what it predicted and what actually occurred.

Scientists believe this ongoing process plays a central role in how people perceive their surroundings, make decisions, behave, and regulate emotions.

Researchers Erik Stänicke, Bendik Hovet, and Line Indrevoll Stänicke, together with colleagues, argue that this modern understanding of brain function has important parallels with ideas that psychoanalysts have been developing for more than a century.

"For over 130 years, psychoanalysis has developed psychological theories about how predictions take place at a subjective level, which cognitive neuropsychology is now studying at a physiological level."

How Freud's Ideas Connect to the Predictive Brain

Although neuroscience and psychoanalysis approach the mind differently, the researchers suggest that both may be describing related processes.

Neuroscience examines the physical mechanisms behind thought and behavior, often using mathematical models to explain how the brain processes information. Psychoanalysis, which grew out of Freud's work, focuses more on unconscious mental processes, emotional experiences, and the ways past relationships influence present behavior.

In other words, one field investigates what happens inside the brain, while the other explores how those processes are experienced by the individual.

A particularly interesting connection involves the psychoanalytic concept of projection and the neuroscientific concept of prediction.

In psychoanalysis, projection involves attributing feelings, intentions, or qualities to someone else. The researchers suggest that this resembles how the brain uses previously learned expectations to interpret new experiences.

"When we attribute qualities, intentions or feelings to other people, our brain shapes our experience of the world in line with established expectations," says Stänicke.

For instance, someone who has repeatedly experienced rejection may become especially sensitive to signs that other people are rejecting them. Even an ambiguous remark or facial expression might be interpreted negatively because past experiences have shaped what the person expects.

Over time, these expectations can influence how someone approaches unfamiliar situations and forms new relationships.

"This corresponds to the neuroscientific distinction between changing one's own predictions, perceptual inference, and the attempt to make the world conform to them, namely active inference."

Perceptual inference refers to the way the brain interprets sensory information and updates its expectations in response. Active inference, by contrast, involves taking actions that help bring experiences into line with predictions or reduce uncertainty.

Together, these concepts help explain why people do not always experience the world as it objectively is. Their expectations can influence what they notice, how they interpret events, and how they respond.

Why the Brain Holds On to Negative Expectations

The researchers believe the similarities between Freud's ideas and modern neuroscience could offer valuable insights into mental health disorders, particularly those involving deeply established patterns of thinking and behavior.

Both approaches portray the mind as constantly seeking stability and predictability. This tendency is related to homeostasis, the process through which a system maintains a relatively stable internal state.

In the predictive brain model, reducing uncertainty is an important part of maintaining that stability. The brain relies on familiar expectations to make the world easier to understand, even when those expectations are inaccurate or no longer helpful.

"Psychoanalysts refer to the tendency in the mind to recreate familiar relational patterns, even when these are poorly adapted," says Stänicke.

This could help explain why people sometimes repeat harmful patterns in relationships or continue interpreting situations negatively despite evidence that challenges their assumptions.

"Rigid and persistent symptoms, such as paranoid ideas or an internalized critical voice, may be stable but not very flexible prediction models," says Stänicke.

"For example, there may be people who automatically expect criticism, rejection or hostility from others, and therefore interpret situations through this filter despite the fact that reality does not warrant it."

Such expectations can become deeply entrenched because they make experiences feel familiar and predictable. Even when the resulting interpretation is distorted, maintaining an established mental model may feel less uncertain than changing it.

According to Stänicke, this offers a possible explanation for why some psychological symptoms can persist for years and why changing longstanding patterns of thought and behavior often requires considerable time.

The connection is particularly interesting because it suggests that some mental health difficulties may involve more than consciously held beliefs. They may also reflect deeply learned expectations that automatically shape emotional responses and interactions with others.

How Psychotherapy Could Help Change Deeply Rooted Patterns

The researchers also highlight an important distinction between what people consciously believe and what they have learned through experience.

"In addition, both models give us insight into how parts of our expectations of the outside world are not only anchored cognitively, but in procedural memory that is expressed in relational ways of being," he says.

Procedural memory is a type of long-term memory involved in learned habits and skills. It allows people to perform familiar actions without consciously thinking through every step.

In the context of relationships, the researchers suggest that a similar form of learning may help explain why certain emotional reactions and patterns of interaction become automatic.

Someone might intellectually understand that other people are not necessarily hostile or critical, for example, yet still instinctively respond as though rejection is inevitable.

These expectations are not simply thoughts that can be changed through reasoning. They can also be embedded in habitual ways of reacting to people and navigating relationships.

This distinction may help explain why psychotherapy sometimes needs to address personal relationships and emotional experiences rather than focusing only on conscious thoughts.

"Therefore, psychotherapy sometimes has to work relationally. For example, new experiences in the relationship between therapist and patient can gradually help to change entrenched relational patterns."

Through repeated experiences that challenge familiar expectations, patients may gradually develop different ways of interpreting situations and relating to other people.

Bringing Freud's Psychoanalysis and Neuroscience Together

The researchers argue that connecting psychoanalysis with modern predictive brain theory could benefit both fields.

Neuroscience may provide a biological framework for understanding processes that psychoanalysts have long described through subjective experience. At the same time, psychoanalytic theories may offer more detailed explanations of how expectations are experienced emotionally, interpreted personally, and expressed through relationships.

The proposed similarities do not establish that Freud's broader theories were correct. Instead, they suggest that two very different traditions of studying the mind may offer complementary ways of understanding some of the same psychological processes.

By examining both the brain's physical mechanisms and the individual's inner experience, researchers hope to develop a more complete understanding of human behavior and mental health.

"Bringing these two fields together can open up for a more holistic psychology, in which both neurological mechanisms and subjective experience are included. In this way, we can understand subjectivity in a more scientific manner."

#703. Nord Stream trial opens in Germany, defense seeks dismissal

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Serhii Kuznietsov is accused of attacking civilian infrastructure in the 2022 sabotage of the Russian gas pipeline to Germany. His lawyers called Nord Stream a military target, and said their client is sworn to secrecy.

The trial of a former Ukrainian special forces officer, Serhii Kuznietsov, opened in the northern German city of Hamburg on Wednesday amid considerable public and press interest.

Kuznietsov is accused of leading the September 2022 sabotage operation that put the undersea Nord Stream 1 and 2 gas pipelines linking Russian to Germany out of action. The pipelines remain inactive four years later, with no sign of plans to ever reactivate them as Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine heads into its fifth winter.

Prosecutors accuse the Ukrainian of participation in a war crime by targeting civilian infrastructure, but his defense lawyers on Wednesday argued that the case should be dismissed entirely on multiple grounds.

A team of three lawyers representing Kuznietsov questioned the premise of the trial in court.

One of Kuznietsov's lawyers, Nicola Canestrini, argued that it was not valid to try the officer on war crimes charges for an attack on civilian infrastructure.

He argued that the pipeline, which provided half of Germany's natural gas imports prior to Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine, was ultimately a "Russian weapon," given the importance of energy revenues for funding Moscow's military.

"Is it unlawful to destroy an aggressor's infrastructure without harming any human beings?" Canestrini asked the court.

After the charges were read out, the judge asked Kuznietsov if he wanted to comment, and he said that he did not.

His lawyers argue that this silence extends beyond not speaking in court and say this should be the main grounds for dismissal.

They say Kuznietsov is sworn to secrecy by his home government in Ukraine and so is unable to discuss his case or potential exonerating evidence with anyone, including his legal representatives.

"He is not permitted to speak. His two immediate superiors told him that he does not have authorization to make any statements — not even to his lawyers," defense attorney Jes Meyer-Lohkamp told the court. In German law, Meyer-Lohkamp argued: "Anyone who cannot speak out cannot be convicted."

DW's Matthew Moore was on hand in the court in northern Germany on Wednesday.

There is huge interest in this court case, Moore reported. No sooner had the court doors opened at 07:30 local time in Hamburg than the first journalists, observers and protesters began arriving.

A small pro-Ukraine protest held up a banner saying, "Russia is a terrorist." One participant from Germany told Moore they wanted to remind the world of "the war crimes taking place against the Ukrainian people."

Inside, a Ukrainian student told DW that he and others had made the journey from Kyiv to show support for the defendant, Serhii Kuznietsov.

"Serhii Kuznietsov is a hero," the student said. In the eyes of the German prosecutors, it is another matter. They accuse him of carrying out a war crime. If found guilty of his part in the gas pipeline bombing, Kuznietsov faces a lengthy prison sentence.

The case has been highly politically awkward for Germany — a staunch supporter of Ukraine despite investigators pursuing a trail that led to Kuznietsov and other suspects.

Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has repeatedly denied any Ukrainian involvement in the operation, and had also resisted Kuznietsov's extradition to Germany after his arrest in Italy last year, arguing that there was no case to answer.

German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul declined to comment on the trial on Wednesday, saying that the most important thing for now was for the case to proceed with no political interference or restrictions.

"We will deal with any outcome, and we will, of course, take it into account in our political calculations," he told a press conference in Berlin.
