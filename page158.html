#513. Too much RNA can starve cells of energy, scientists discover

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Scientists have uncovered a surprising way that viruses can interfere with the body's cells. When too much RNA accumulates inside a cell, it can damage the structures that produce energy, potentially leaving the cell struggling to function normally.

A new study led by researchers at the Texas A&M College of Veterinary Medicine and Biomedical Sciences (VMBS) found that excess RNA produced during poxvirus infections can impair mitochondria, the tiny structures responsible for generating most of the energy cells need to survive.

Published in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, the research reveals an unexpected connection between RNA buildup and cellular energy production. The findings could have implications far beyond viral infections, potentially helping scientists better understand aging-related diseases and improve RNA-based medical treatments, including mRNA vaccines.

"Scientists have long known that RNA degradation helps control protein production and remove defective RNA," said Dr. Zhilong Yang, a professor in VMBS' Department of Veterinary Pathobiology. "Our study reveals another important role: it helps cells maintain the energy they need to function properly."

Cells constantly produce and break down RNA as part of their normal activities. This cleanup process, known as RNA degradation, helps regulate protein production, eliminate faulty RNA, and control immune responses. The new findings suggest it also performs another essential function by protecting the cell's energy supply.

RNA plays several important roles in keeping cells functioning. Messenger RNA (mRNA), for example, carries genetic instructions that tell cells which proteins to produce. These proteins are needed for nearly everything cells do, from maintaining their structure to carrying out essential chemical reactions.

Another type, double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), frequently appears during viral infections. Its presence can serve as a warning signal, alerting the immune system that a virus may have entered the cell.

Viruses often generate large quantities of RNA as they reproduce inside infected cells. Ordinarily, cellular cleanup systems help prevent RNA from accumulating to harmful levels. However, when RNA production exceeds the cell's ability to break it down, the excess material can interfere with mitochondria and reduce energy production.

Scientists already understood that excessive double-stranded RNA could provoke potentially harmful immune responses. What surprised the researchers was discovering that too much ordinary messenger RNA could also damage mitochondria.

"MRNA is a normal component of our cells, but it seems like with many good things, if we get too much, that can become a problem for the cell," Yang said. "When too much RNA builds up, including mRNA, it can damage the mitochondria and interfere with the cell's ability to produce energy."

This creates an unusual challenge for viruses. Although viruses depend entirely on the cells they infect to supply energy and manufacture proteins, producing excessive amounts of viral RNA can damage the very machinery they need to reproduce.

According to Yang, poxviruses, a group that includes the viruses responsible for smallpox and mpox, appear to have ways of managing this problem. They use RNA cleanup mechanisms to remove excess RNA, helping infected cells remain functional long enough for viral replication to continue.

"If the cell is not healthy, the virus would not replicate well," Yang said. "The virus needs to keep RNA levels balanced inside the cell."

Scientists Discover RNA Can Directly Damage Mitochondria

One of the study's most unexpected findings emerged when researchers investigated exactly how excess RNA affects mitochondria.

Initially, the team suspected that RNA buildup was damaging these cellular powerhouses indirectly by activating the immune system. Certain types of RNA are known to trigger defensive responses, which can place additional stress on infected cells.

However, experiments revealed that the damage did not require an immune response.

"When we isolated only the mitochondria and added RNA, we still saw mitochondrial damage," said Dr. Djamal Brahim Belhaouari, the study's first author. "That was the moment we realized this could be a previously unknown cellular response to RNA."

The researchers also observed that mitochondrial problems developed before substantial immune activation occurred. Furthermore, the damage did not depend on the immune pathways normally activated during infection.

Together, these results indicate that excess RNA itself can interfere with mitochondrial function, rather than simply causing damage through the body's response to a virus.

The precise mechanism remains unclear, but the researchers have proposed a possible explanation involving electrical charges.

Mitochondria produce energy by maintaining an electrical and chemical difference across their inner membranes. This difference helps drive the production of ATP, the molecule cells use as their primary energy source.

RNA carries a negative electrical charge. The researchers suspect that when unusually large amounts accumulate near mitochondria, they may disturb the electrical conditions required for efficient energy production.

"We think the negatively charged RNA may accumulate around mitochondria and disrupt the electrical balance needed for energy production, although how it happens is unknown," Yang said. "This is a new concept that we are continuing to explore."

What the Discovery Could Mean for Aging and Disease

Although the experiments focused on viral infections, the findings raise questions about whether RNA buildup might contribute to other health problems.

Abnormal RNA accumulation has been observed in several conditions, including certain cancers, neurodegenerative diseases, and disorders associated with aging. Neurodegenerative diseases involve the progressive deterioration of nerve cells, as occurs in conditions such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease.

Because mitochondria are essential for maintaining healthy cells, disruptions to their energy production could potentially contribute to cellular dysfunction in some of these conditions.

The researchers suggest that excess RNA may represent one previously unrecognized source of mitochondrial stress. However, further studies are needed to determine how broadly this mechanism operates and whether it directly contributes to these diseases.

"We think this has quite broad translational implications," Brahim Belhaouari said. "Too much double-stranded RNA or mRNA that cannot be degraded effectively may contribute to disease processes in some contexts."

Potential Implications for mRNA Vaccines and Future Treatments

The discovery could also provide useful information for scientists developing medicines that rely on RNA.

RNA-based therapies, including mRNA vaccines, work by introducing RNA instructions into cells to produce specific proteins. Understanding how cells respond to RNA, process it, and eventually break it down is important for designing these treatments.

By identifying another way excessive RNA can affect cellular health, the findings may help researchers optimize future RNA-based therapies and vaccines. The study does not establish that existing mRNA vaccines cause mitochondrial damage in people.

"Understanding this mechanism could help researchers design and optimize future RNA-based therapeutics and vaccines by better understanding how cells respond to large amounts of RNA," Yang said.

More broadly, the research suggests that controlling RNA levels is important for more than maintaining accurate protein production or managing immune activity. It may also be essential for keeping mitochondria functioning and ensuring cells have enough energy.

For the scientists, the most striking result was that even a molecule cells depend on every day can become harmful when it accumulates beyond normal levels.

"Scientists already knew that too much double-stranded RNA can be harmful," Yang said. "What surprised us was finding out that too much mRNA -- something cells normally need every day -- can also damage mitochondria and interfere with the cell's ability to produce energy."

Ukraine wants its own satellite-communication system, which shouldn't be surprising given the events of the past week or so.

The Ukrainian military just signed a letter of intent with two Finnish companies — the telecom giant Nokia and satellite maker ICEYE — to look into developing a sovereign satellite-communications network for Ukraine.

"Ukraine needs secure, sovereign and high-performance satellite communications systems that it controls," ICEYE cofounder and CEO Rafal Modrzewski said in a statement today (Oct. 22). "We have a proven track record of delivering sovereign missions at speed to help build that capability. ICEYE has proudly supported Ukraine since 2022, and we remain committed to continuing that support today."

Ukraine relies heavily on satellite-based communications and data to defend itself from Russia, which invaded the nation in February 2022 and continues to attack it with ground troops, missiles and drone strikes.

But Ukraine does not own those satellites. It primarily uses the services provided by SpaceX's Starlink megaconstellation in low Earth orbit (LEO) and Starshield, a military-grade, jamming-resistant version of Starlink employed by the U.S. government and its allies.

Starshield in particular is crucial to Ukraine, as it allows the nation to operate fast-moving drones over large distances in war zones. Ukraine has gotten very good at drone warfare, employing these little robots both in offensive and defensive capacities.

On Oct. 14, however, Ukrainian soldiers and officials reported a severe throttling of their access to Starshield services — a speed limit on terminal-carrying drones, which caused many of them to fall out of the sky.

It's not entirely clear who's behind the throttling. Some U.S. officials have apparently said the order came from Elon Musk, but the billionaire SpaceX founder and CEO put the ultimate responsibility on the Trump administration.

"Starshield systems are owned and controlled by the US government. Any limits on speed or usage are decided by the US military and ultimately POTUS," Musk said on Oct. 14 via X, the social media platform he owns.

Whoever made the call, it certainly wasn't Ukraine. And the Starshield throttling — which apparently is still in effect — has doubtless reinforced the nation's desire to control its own satellite-communications infrastructure.

"Nations need communications they can rely on in every situation. Satellite networks extend and protect connectivity when networks on the ground are disrupted," Mikko Hautala, Nokia's chief geopolitical and government relations officer and chairman of Nokia Defense, said in the same statement.

"This makes them a key part of any secure communications system," he added. "Through this Letter of Intent, together with Ukraine and ICEYE, we will explore how trusted, sovereign satellite communications can strengthen Ukraine's resilience and give it control over the connectivity it depends on."

Nokia and ICEYE announced on Oct. 1 that they plan to work together to help nations develop such sovereign satcom systems. The partnership with Ukraine follows on from that earlier agreement.

ICEYE has already been aiding the Ukrainian defense effort. The Finnish company and its U.S. subsidiary jointly operate a fleet of Earth-observing satellites in LEO, which study our planet using synthetic aperture radar. Ukraine uses data from the ICEYE constellation for intelligence and planning purposes.

This relationship has put ICEYE — or one of its satellites, at least — in Russia's crosshairs. A Russian inspector satellite called Kosmos 2612 recently made an extremely close approach to ICEYE-X36, which is operated by ICEYE US.

The goal of the unsanctioned rendezvous is unclear. Russia may have wanted to assess the satellite's capabilities or interfere with it in some way. Or perhaps the nation simply wanted to send a message, letting ICEYE (and Ukraine) know that it could mess with satellites if it wanted to.

#510. Hugging Face’s New AI Biology Model Lets You Chat With DNA

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

The AI platform Hugging Face on Thursday released a new open model for understanding DNA that can run locally on a laptop.

Developed in collaboration with researchers at Zhongguancun Academy in China and the University of Naples Federico II in Italy, the model is trained on genomic sequences and natural language so that people can ask questions about the effects of DNA changes on gene activity and health and get conversational answers in return.

Lewis Tunstall, a senior research scientist at Hugging Face, says the tool is meant to help researchers design new drugs and genetic therapies, and it could potentially aid patients in interpreting their own genomes.

“We've unlocked a new capability in language models, which is fundamentally about being able to chat with DNA,” he says of the new model, dubbed Carbon-X. Using natural language means even nontechnical users “can actually ask questions about DNA that wouldn't be accessible through any other tool.”

Known as essentially GitHub for AI models, Hugging Face provides a library of readymade AI tools and a platform for running primarily open-weight and open-source AI applications for developers, researchers, businesses, and people who want to experiment with AI without building everything from scratch. In September, Nvidia agreed to acquire the company for nearly $13 billion. (Hugging Face was also at the center of a major hack by OpenAI’s models earlier this year.)

DNA is more like computer code than natural language. Instead of 0s and 1s, though, it uses As, Cs, Gs, and Ts to encode information. A human genome contains 6 billion of these letters. While the human genome has been sequenced end to end, scientists still don’t understand how most of it works.

Now, AI models are helping researchers decipher some of those mysteries. Last year, Google DeepMind released AlphaGenome, which predicts the effects of DNA sequences and genetic mutations but does so in a numeric format. It also requires more compute power. Evo 2, a model developed by the Arc Institute and Nvidia, along with academic partners, speaks the language of DNA and can generate entire genomes from scratch.

Carbon-X integrates both DNA and natural language in a conversational interface. Think of it like a ChatGPT for DNA.

The team started with an existing language model and expanded its vocabulary to read DNA in groups of six letters. They then continued training on 300 billion chunks of data, or tokens, that combined DNA sequences, English text, and examples linking DNA to biological descriptions. The DNA sequences came from mammals (including humans), other vertebrates, invertebrates, plants, fungi, and protozoa. Tunstall says the team didn’t train the model on viruses because they didn’t want it to be used for potentially dangerous applications.

Users won’t be able to upload full human genomes. The model was trained to be able to ingest DNA sequences of roughly 50,000 base pairs, so users will only be able to query small parts of the genome at a time.

One of the ways scientists could use Carbon-X is to help understand whether a genetic mutation is benign or disease-causing, a key step in developing a targeted drug or genetic therapy. If an intervention targets the wrong mutation, it could be totally ineffective or, worse, cause unintended harm.

Researchers face restrictions when it comes to uploading identifiable or sensitive patient data to frontier AI models, so Tunstall says his team intentionally designed a small model with 14 billion parameters. That will allow it to run locally on the user’s hardware, rather than rely on cloud-based models. No data is sent to Hugging Face or any other company, he says.

The developers of Carbon-X caution that they haven’t validated the model yet with actual wet lab experiments, so they can’t say how accurate its predictions are.

#508. Millions take fish oil for brain health. A two-year study found no benefit

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Millions of Americans take fish oil supplements in hopes of protecting their memory and keeping their brains healthy as they age. These products have become a major business, with Americans spending more than $1 billion each year on supplements containing omega-3 fatty acids. These essential nutrients help build and maintain the connections between brain cells that support memory, learning, and other cognitive functions.

But a new study suggests that getting more omega-3s into the brain through supplements may not provide the protection many people expect.

Researchers at Keck Medicine of USC found that even when high doses of omega-3s successfully reached the brain, they did not produce measurable improvements in memory, thinking abilities, or protection against brain shrinkage.

The findings, published in eBioMedicine, come from a two-year clinical trial involving older adults considered to be at increased risk of developing Alzheimer's disease. Despite taking omega-3 supplements daily, participants showed no significant cognitive advantage over those who received a placebo.

"We all wish there was a silver bullet for preventing Alzheimer's, but our findings showed that fish oil supplements do not appear to protect brain health," said Hussein Naji Yassine, MD, director of the USC Center for Personalized Brain Health and lead investigator of the study. "While omega-3s play an important role in forming brain cell connections needed for cognition, our results do not support fish oil supplements as a preventive measure against Alzheimer's."

A Two-Year Study Tests Fish Oil's Brain Benefits

To investigate whether omega-3 supplements could protect the aging brain, researchers enrolled 365 adults between the ages of 55 and 80. All participants rarely ate fish, a major dietary source of omega-3 fatty acids, and were considered at elevated risk for Alzheimer's disease.

Nearly half (47%) carried APOE4, a gene variant associated with a substantially higher risk of developing Alzheimer's later in life. Although having this variant does not mean someone will necessarily develop the disease, it is the strongest known genetic risk factor for late-onset Alzheimer's.

Participants were randomly assigned to receive either a daily fish oil supplement or a placebo, an inactive treatment used for comparison. The supplements provided 2,000 mg of docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), an omega-3 fatty acid that is especially important for the brain.

DHA is a major component of brain cell membranes, helping maintain the structure and function of neurons. It also supports communication between nerve cells, making it an important nutrient for normal brain activity.

The study was double-blind, meaning neither participants nor researchers knew who was receiving the active supplement during the trial. This approach helps reduce bias and makes it easier to determine whether any observed benefits are actually caused by the treatment.

Omega-3 Levels Rose, but Memory Did Not Improve

Before evaluating whether the supplements improved brain health, researchers needed to answer a fundamental question: Were the omega-3 fatty acids actually reaching the brain?

To find out, they measured DHA concentrations in cerebrospinal fluid, the clear liquid that surrounds and cushions the brain and spinal cord. Examining this fluid can provide information about substances entering and circulating within the central nervous system.

After six months, DHA levels in the cerebrospinal fluid had increased by an average of 17% among participants taking the supplements. This provided evidence that the omega-3 fatty acids were reaching their intended destination.

However, getting DHA into the central nervous system was only part of the goal. The more important question was whether those higher levels would translate into better brain health.

Researchers assessed participants' memory and other cognitive abilities at the beginning of the trial and again after two years. Despite the increase in DHA, those taking fish oil performed no better on the cognitive tests than participants receiving the placebo.

Brain imaging revealed similarly disappointing results.

The supplements did not prevent shrinkage of the hippocampus, a region deep within the brain that plays a central role in forming and organizing memories. The hippocampus is particularly vulnerable to changes associated with aging and Alzheimer's disease, making its size an important indicator researchers use when studying brain health.

Taken together, the results suggest that simply increasing DHA availability in the brain is not enough to produce measurable cognitive benefits in this group of older adults.

Why Fish Oil Supplements May Work Differently Than Food

The unexpected findings have raised a new question for researchers: If omega-3s can reach the brain, why don't higher levels necessarily improve its function?

Yassine and his colleagues are now investigating how the brain absorbs, processes, and uses these nutrients. Their earlier research suggests that omega-3s may be more beneficial when consumed as part of a broader dietary pattern rather than taken in isolation as a supplement.

One possibility involves the Mediterranean-style diet, which emphasizes foods such as fish, vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts, and olive oil. This eating pattern naturally supplies omega-3 fatty acids alongside other nutrients and has been associated with a lower risk of Alzheimer's disease.

The researchers suspect that the combination of nutrients in a balanced diet, along with an individual's overall health, may influence how effectively omega-3s support the brain.

"We're focused on better understanding how the brain processes omega-3s and whether factors, such as poor health, dietary pattern, genetic risk and age, may change the brain's ability to effectively absorb and use omega-3s," said Yassine. "We are working to develop medications that may help the brain better utilize these nutrients to preserve cognitive function."

Understanding these differences could eventually help scientists identify why certain people might respond better to omega-3s than others and whether new treatments could improve the brain's ability to benefit from these essential fats.

Importantly, the findings do not mean omega-3 fatty acids are unnecessary or that eating fish offers no health benefits. Instead, they indicate that taking high-dose DHA supplements alone did not provide measurable protection against cognitive decline or hippocampal shrinkage in the population studied.

Healthy Habits Remain Key to Alzheimer's Prevention

Although the trial focused specifically on fish oil supplements, the researchers emphasize that protecting brain health requires a much broader approach.

Rather than relying on a single supplement, they recommend maintaining healthy habits throughout life, including regular physical activity, sufficient high-quality sleep, and a nutritious, balanced diet.

These behaviors support overall health and may help reduce factors associated with cognitive decline. Physical exercise, for example, promotes cardiovascular health and blood flow, while sleep plays an important role in memory formation and normal brain maintenance.

"Staying healthy throughout life remains the most powerful tool we have for reducing Alzheimer's risk, including regular exercise, quality sleep and a balanced diet," said Yassine. "Living a healthy lifestyle is the brain's equivalent of getting regular car maintenance and high-quality oil changes. The brain is more likely to lose greater function if health issues in other parts of the body go unaddressed, in the same way that car engines stop working if regular maintenance is skipped."

While these lifestyle factors were not directly tested in the trial, the researchers believe they offer a more promising foundation for long-term brain health than depending on fish oil supplements alone.

The results also highlight an important distinction: A nutrient can be essential for healthy brain function without providing additional protection when consumed in large doses as a supplement. Understanding why that happens may open new avenues for research into Alzheimer's prevention and the nutritional needs of the aging brain.

On Oct. 22, 1992, the space shuttle Columbia launched into space with the ashes of Gene Roddenberry, the creator of "Star Trek," aboard.

During mission STS-52, NASA astronaut and shuttle commander James Wetherbee brought a small container with a portion of Roddenberry's ashes into space as part of his personal effects at the request of Roddenberry's widow, Majel Barrett. It was Roddenberry's lifelong dream to go to space, but he never got to go while he was alive.

But thanks to his wife, a little bit of Roddenberry finally made it to space almost exactly one year after his death. NASA and his family didn't tell anyone about it until almost two years later in 1994.

Wetherbee said he was "honored to be asked" by Barrett to carry Roddenberry's ashses on the spaceflight. "I'm just glad I was able to bring a little happiness to the family," he said, according to Newsweek.

The launch of Roddenberry's ashes into space on NASA's STS-52 mission aboard the space shuttle Columbia marked the world's first "space burial," but it wouldn't be the last.

About five years after Roddenberry's memorial flight, in 1997, the space memorial company Celestis began launching a series of "burial" flights by flying capsules with human cremains on rockets . In fact, more of Roddenberry's ashes flew on Celestis' "founders flight" in April 1997 aboard an air-launched Pegasus rocket that took off from the Canary Islands in Spain. The ashes of Roddenberry's wife and of Star Trek actor James Doohan have also flown in space, among others.

Celestis has gone on to launch human cremains on many suborbital and orbital flights, including some on SpaceX Falcon 9 rockets. The company even launched the ashes of the late astronomer Eugene Shoemaker to the moon in 1999 on the Luna 1 flight, with another set of cremains launched into deep space on the Enterprise mission in 2023.
