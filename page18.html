After three games on Tuesday night, the rest of the NBA teams opened their 2026-27 seasons on Wednesday night. And with it, we finally got a look at a host of star players with their new teams in regular-season action on the heels of a rather eventful summer across the league.

After grading LeBron James's opening act with the 76ers, here are the report cards for some other notables after their first games in different digs.

The biggest name to change teams this offseason (non-LeBron division). Once again, Pat Riley got his man. But even after acquiring Antetokounmpo, Riley hinted that they were still looking to add another major piece, indicating that the Heat needed to improve a roster that could clearly use more floor spacing, shot creators, scoring and depth.

That's certainly how it looked in the Heat's ugly 25-point home loss to the Timberwolves in the season opener. Antetokounmpo had 35 points in 30 minutes, but that still wasn't enough to keep the Heat in the game. To the extent that there were any positives for Miami, at least Giannis looked like Giannis -- including airballing a free throw and being a problem in the open court (especially when the refs were lenient with traveling violations; more on that in a moment when we get to LaMelo Ball's debut).

Antetokounmpo played just 36 games in his final season with the Bucks, the result of a combination of injuries and a late-season tank job. But prior to that, he finished in the top five in the MVP voting in seven straight seasons. He'll be 32 in December, but the Heat are betting he's healthy and has plenty of top-tier basketball still to offer.

They'll need all the heavy lifting he can muster if Miami wants to join the contenders in the loaded Eastern Conference.

The "C" stands for comedy. Look, there's no denying that Ball is a skilled basketball player. In an easy win over the Heat, Ball had 21 points (on 7-for-17 shooting, including 5-for-13 from deep) and six assists.

He looked comfortable next to Anthony Edwards, who went off for a game-high 45 points without breaking much of a sweat. Ball can hit shots from all over the court and he's a fantastic passer. But there's also a reason why the Hornets moved on from Ball, and they were right to do so. During his time in Charlotte, he demonstrated tantalizing talent, but that often came in the service of individual highlights and at the expense of team success. And while he made jaw-dropping plays with the Hornets, he also frequently left you scratching your head.

It's not so much that Ball took, oh, all the steps after his gather in what was pretty plainly an egregious travel that went uncalled. It's more that he couldn't help laughing about it and pointing to the crowd afterward, as though he'd just drained an impressive bucket. With Ball, it's impossible to be sure if he's in on the joke or completely unaware that he's doing a bit at all times.

That's a hot take to be sure. Now we'll see whether the Wolves can handle it or if they eventually get burned.

Following what we'll charitably label an eventful final year in Los Angeles, Leonard is back in Toronto. He's an instant upgrade over Brandon Ingram at both ends of the floor, and when paired with Scottie Barnes, the Raptors now boast two of the best two-way wings in the league.

His first game back with his old club didn't put too much of a strain on Leonard. He should thank the Bulls for not putting up too many roadblocks for his reentry. In a blowout 135-92 home win, Leonard had 23 points, six rebounds, two assists, four steals and a block in just 24 minutes. He shot only 5-for-17 from the floor but made all 11 of his free throws.

Remember him? After being traded from the Lakers back in February 2025, Davis has played just 29 games -- all of them in Dallas. When the Mavericks flipped him to the Wizards last season, Davis was true to his nickname and stayed in street clothes without stepping on the floor for Washington. Until Wednesday.

In a 128-116 win over the Bucks, Davis reminded everyone just how good he is, going for 34 points, 12 rebounds, four assists, two steals and a block. He made 11 of 20 shots and hit a 3-pointer. Best of all, he played 34 minutes. Yes, it was against the Bucks, who figure to be awful this season, but AD didn't make the schedule and knocking the rust off against Milwaukee isn't the worst thing given how long it's been since he played.

Since arriving in Washington, Davis has been a popular trade candidate in rumors and whispers around the league. The Wizards have insisted they are not interested in moving him, despite putting contract extension negotiations on hold this summer. It's hard to blame the organization for wanting to see how he fits first -- and how much he'll even be available -- before committing big money to him. Davis's talent is undeniable, but he'll be 34 in March and a litany of injuries over the course of his career has repeatedly kept him on the sidelines. He's played 56 games or fewer in six of the last 10 seasons.

But hey, at least he was out there on Wednesday, playing with Trae Young and the Wizards. The crowd seemed to appreciate it.

When the Trail Blazers traded for Morant, the nearly universal question became "how is that going to work?" In the opener, the answer was "pretty well."

According to a report in The Athletic in early October, new Blazers coach Micah Nori didn't believe it made sense to start Damian Lillard and Morant in the same backcourt. That thinking didn't even last through the first game.

The Blazers started Morant on Wednesday in a runaway 124-83 home win over the Suns. In 23 minutes, Morant went for 14 points (on 6-for-8 shooting), five rebounds, eight assists and three steals with just one turnover. It's the smallest of sample sizes, but the Blazers will obviously take it.

The main concern with adding Morant was that Portland already had an overcrowded backcourt with Lillard, Jrue Holiday, Scoot Henderson and Shaedon Sharpe (who is expected to miss extended time with a knee injury). The situation was further complicated by the Blazers unlocking Deni Avdija last season and realizing the team worked better when the offense ran through him. Throwing another high-usage player like Morant into the mix seemed inadvisable, especially when he's used to having the ball in his hands and isn't a good shooter (31.1% from deep for his career).

Against the Suns, Morant hit a pair of threes. Even more encouraging, he showed the kind of effort the Blazers will need from him if this odd mix is going to work over the course of a long season.

#3317. Buccaneers vs. Panthers: 3 keys to a crucial Week 7 matchup

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

The Tampa Bay Buccaneers have reached a point in their season where simply being competitive isn't enough. At 1-5, they need to start finishing games, and Sunday's NFC South matchup against the Carolina Panthers offers a chance to get back on track against the division leader.

Tampa Bay enters Week 7 after a 26-19 overtime loss to the Pittsburgh Steelers, a game in which the Buccaneers led 16-3 into the fourth quarter before watching it disappear. Now they'll face a Carolina team that has won two straight games and developed one of the NFL's most productive offenses.

According to the Buccaneers' official Week 7 game preview, Carolina ranks second in total offense at 404.4 yards per game and fourth in scoring at 29.2 points per game. Tampa Bay, meanwhile, ranks 24th in total offense at 292.7 yards per game and 25th in scoring at 19.8 points per game.

The biggest storyline heading into Sunday is Baker Mayfield's expected return after missing three games with a dislocated right thumb. Mayfield practiced fully all week, and head coach Todd Bowles expects him to start, though the veteran quarterback is officially listed as questionable.

Undrafted rookie Jalon Daniels deserves credit for stepping into a difficult situation and helping Tampa Bay earn its first victory against Dallas. However, the Buccaneers have been limited offensively while asking an inexperienced quarterback to recognize blitzes, adjust protections, and make decisions against NFL defenses.

Mayfield's experience should give offensive coordinator Zac Robinson more flexibility, but his return won't solve every problem. Tampa Bay ranks 30th in passing offense at 164.8 yards per game and 28th in red-zone touchdown percentage at 47.4%. The Buccaneers also rank 30th in sacks allowed per pass attempt, making pass protection an important concern against Carolina.

That challenge grows harder with right tackle Luke Goedeke ruled out with a calf injury. Wide receiver Emeka Egbuka will also miss Sunday's game with concussion and foot injuries, leaving Tampa Bay without one of its leading receiving threats.

While Tampa Bay has struggled through the air, its rushing offense has been more productive. The Buccaneers rank eighth in the NFL with 127.8 rushing yards per game, and Bucky Irving leads the team with 452 rushing yards.

Carolina has been vulnerable against the run, allowing 132.2 rushing yards per game, which ranks 27th in the league. The Panthers also give up 5.12 yards per carry, giving Tampa Bay an opportunity to establish its running game rather than relying entirely on Mayfield's arm.

Irving's ability to create yards after contact could be particularly important. A productive running game would help keep Tampa Bay out of obvious passing situations, reduce pressure on its offensive line, and give Robinson more opportunities to use play-action.

Carolina will also be without outside linebackers Jaelan Phillips and Thomas Incoom, who were ruled out Friday. Their absences could give Tampa Bay more opportunities to attack the edges of the defense.

Carolina quarterback Bryce Young has taken a significant step forward this season. In five starts, Young has thrown for 1,549 yards and averages 309.8 passing yards per game, the highest mark in the league entering Week 7.

The Panthers lead the NFL in passing offense, averaging 295.6 yards per game, with Tetairoa McMillan emerging as Young's top receiving threat. McMillan has 32 receptions for 471 yards, while running back Chuba Hubbard has contributed 426 rushing yards and six touchdowns.

#3307. MLB Lockout 101: In-depth guide to likely work stoppage and what it means for free agency, 2027 season, more

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

Major League Baseball's players and owners are in the process of negotiating a new collective bargaining agreement, or CBA. The CBA is the accord that governs the working relationship between the players, as represented by their union, the MLBPA, and the clubs. It covers pretty much every aspect of that relationship that you can imagine, all the way from the overarching salary and payroll structure to daily meal money and days off during the season.

Not surprisingly, it's the economic platform of the game that's the toughest to negotiate because that is always, in essence, a fight over money, or how the league's substantial revenues will be divided up between teams and players. That's keenly relevant right now because players and owners are sparring over whether or not MLB will have a cap on salaries moving forward. The owners have renewed their push for a salary cap after not aggressively (or at least not this aggressively) pursuing one since the 1994-95 players' strike.

Matters are such that the owners are expected to implement a lockout of the players after the current CBA expires at 11:59 p.m. ET on Dec. 1 of this year, or a little more than a month after the World Series ends.

There are two kinds of labor stoppages in MLB: the strike and the lockout. A strike is when the labor side – players as represented by their union, in this instance – puts a halt to operations. A lockout is when the management side – team owners and the commissioner's office doing their bidding, in this instance – initiates the stoppage. In plainspoken terms, a strike is a refusal to work, and a lockout is a refusal to permit work to be done. For those on the outside, the result is the same, in that there's no Major League Baseball during a lockout or strike.

When a lockout is in force in MLB, as is expected to be the case after this CBA expires, nothing happens on the major-league front. And we do mean nothing.

There's an almost literal element to a lockout in that anyone on the 40-man roster of any MLB club will be locked out of any team facility for the duration of the stoppage. That also means no access to team trainers or team physicians or members of the coaching staff. Beyond that, there's a transaction freeze in place starting the moment the lockout begins (more on that in a moment).

Additionally, MLB is expected to remove player images and videos from the league's official website, MLB.com, and player images and even names would be removed from articles on MLB.com. A league memo acquired by The Athletic also forbids contact between players and anyone on the club side, at least to the greatest extent possible. The latter extends all the way down to a recommendation against sending holiday cards to players who have been locked out.

Bottom line: A lockout means the sport at the highest level is on hold across all fronts until further notice. "Further notice" almost certainly means when a new CBA is agreed to in principle and then ratified by both sides.

As just noted, a lockout means a transaction freeze, which means no players on any 40-man rosters can be traded and no major-league free agents can be signed for as long as the lockout lasts. It's worth noting that in the run-up to the last lockout of 2021-22, we saw a swarm of free agent signings just before the lockout began. Again, there's roughly a month between the end of the World Series and the expiration of the CBA, so there is some calendar available for those looking to make trades or signings (once the five-day exclusive negotiating window closes and qualifying offers are extended) before the freeze sets in. We could see that flurry of moves again, particularly in light of proposed changes to contract structures that would limit lengths and dollar amounts. Would players like Tarik Skubal, who are eyeing mid-nine-figure contracts, want to get their deals done quickly and hope they're grandfathered in?

That said, there's a lot more uncertainty about what the game's financial structure will look like after this CBA negotiation concludes, at least relative to the last time we did this. That may have a chilling effect on the trade and free agent markets leading up to the lockout date. Clubs don't like mass uncertainty when it comes to making major roster decisions, and they may prefer to see what the league's salary and revenue sharing systems will look like for the next half-decade (the term of each CBA these days) before committing to signings and trades.

If the lockout spans a meaningful amount of time, then that means a great deal of usual winter business will be put on hold. The winter meetings and the major-league portion of Rule 5 Draft are typically held in the first half of December, so those could be imperiled. Last time out, the major-league portion of the Rule 5 Draft was indeed canceled, although the November Rule 5 protection deadline had already passed. If the lockout stretches into January, then the exchange of arbitration figures between eligible players and their teams will be delayed. Get well into late January and early February without an agreement, and arbitration hearings and the spring training schedule could be shortened or imperiled altogether. Needless to say, local businesses and elected officials in Florida and Arizona would very much not like that. That, though, could be the least of our worries.

It very well might. It's too soon to say, but there's the real potential for disruption and, perish the thought, maybe even cancellation of the 2027 season. The latter is of course the worst-case scenario, and there's no reason to think there's a strong threat of that possibility, at least this far out. During the last lockout, though, the two sides weren't fighting over quite as much territory, and we very nearly lost games from the regular-season schedule. Lest anyone forget, the last time the owners vigorously pushed for a salary cap, we wound up losing the World Series.

There is a wrinkle of note that may make a worst-case scenario less likely. If the lockout drags on long enough to cancel 60 or more games from the 2027 MLB season, then the fallout could be significant. The Athletic recently reported that if the lockout extends into late May or so and costs at least 60 games, then most of the league's national broadcast and streaming contracts would be extended for another year, through 2029. That matters because Manfred and the league hope to bundle and centralize team streaming rights and share those revenues equally, and the current timeline would theoretically allow that to happen at the same time the national contracts are up for renegotiation in 2028. Tacking on another year to those national contracts would push back the significant windfall that owners and the league are expecting from that new media rights structure. This amounts to a major incentive, at least on the league side, to agree to a new CBA before June arrives.

Just players on the 40-player roster will be affected by the lockout, as mentioned. While that includes some minor leaguers, the vast majority of minor-league players are not on the 40 and thus will not be impacted. Minor leaguers now have their own CBA independent of MLB players, and that CBA is still in force. Thus, they're not being locked out. The minor-league season will proceed as normal, albeit minus those prospects on their respective 40s, just as it did during the 2021-22 lockout. So, yes, there will be affiliated professional baseball going on even if the lockout stretches into the 2027 regular season.

To hear commissioner Rob Manfred tell it, the lockout is designed to hasten negotiations between the two sides and speed up the process of forging a new CBA that, assuming it arrives in time, will cover the 2027-31 MLB seasons. Manfred cited this very reason leading up to the lockout of 2021-22, and during the drumbeat phase of this approaching lockout, he said this:

"In a bizarre way, it's actually a positive. There is leverage associated with an offseason lockout, and the process of collective bargaining under the NLRA (National Labor Relations Act) works based on leverage. The great thing about offseason lockouts is the leverage that exists gets applied between the bargaining parties."

After implementing the 2021-22 lockout, Manfred said the drastic decision was designed to "jumpstart the negotiations and get us to an agreement that will allow the season to start on time."

Actions, though, hold sway over words, and after locking out the players, MLB owners didn't make their first CBA proposal to the players for another six weeks or so.
