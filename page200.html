The civil rights arm of the U.S. Department of Education has dramatically scaled back investigations involving schools and colleges since the second Trump administration began, even as complaints continue to pour in, a ProPublica analysis of newly obtained records found.

The analysis shows how severely the department’s Office for Civil Rights has retracted from its mission, launching an average of about 100 new investigations a month — 1,839 total from Inauguration Day in 2025 through July 2026. The OCR launched an average of 310 new investigations each month in 2024 during the Biden administration, according to data previously obtained by ProPublica.

Under President Donald Trump, the OCR abandoned the public-facing list of schools it was investigating and the reasons for those investigations, including claims of discrimination based on race, sex and disability. ProPublica sued the department to obtain internal case data. The government subsequently provided records of the OCR’s actions in the first 18 months of Trump’s return to power, and we are publishing the data so the public can search the cases.

The decline in investigations is especially dramatic for cases involving sex discrimination. More than 200 new complaints were filed with the office related to schools’ handling of sexual violence — rape, assault and abuse. But the Trump administration launched investigations in only 29 of them, finding fault with a school district only once, according to the newly obtained data. It’s typical for there to be at least 60 sexual violence investigations opened each year.

The department addressed fewer of these cases despite continued concerns about the persistence of the problem — as demonstrated by the intense public scrutiny over how Cornell University addressed a student’s report of a 2024 gang rape.

Beyond that, the OCR launched only two investigations into discrimination based on sexual orientation.

“They’re barely doing anything to protect women and girls, including survivors of sexual violence,” said Shiwali Patel, an attorney who previously worked at the OCR and is now with the National Women’s Law Center. “They just don’t care about women and girls.”

The organization and more than 120 other advocacy, civil rights and education groups sent a letter on Tuesday to Congress’ ranking education committee leaders urging them to hold the OCR accountable for “its failure to enforce civil rights law and protect students from sexual violence.”

With OCR’s online database idle since Trump took office, the only way to know where the agency has launched investigations has been through selective department press releases, when OCR employees provided internal data to reporters or from schools and colleges that acknowledged that their institutions had become targets of the investigations.

The data ProPublica received as a result of our lawsuit can now fill that gap on the more than 1,800 civil rights investigations that the department initiated from Jan. 20, 2025, to July 29 of this year. The database also reflects the 24,000 new complaints logged through May and the nearly 17,000 new and old cases the department closed — almost always by dismissing them.

The agency is dismissing complaints more frequently than in prior years, the records show. And fewer than 700 cases opened by the administration were resolved with a finding of wrongdoing.

The data does not include investigations that had been opened before Trump retook power but that are pending decisions by OCR — a backlog of more than 10,000 cases.

In response to questions from ProPublica, an Education Department spokesperson blamed the Biden administration for leaving office with 19,000 unresolved cases, which the Trump administration has worked to address.

“Spare us the crocodile tears as the Trump Administration is working to restore OCR to its original purpose: enforcing the law to ensure all students can receive an education free from discrimination,” spokesperson Sabin Sidney wrote.

He added that the administration “treats any form of discrimination on the basis of race or sex as illegal.” And he criticized the prior administration for endorsing policies that allowed transgender girls and women to compete in women’s athletics and use women's restrooms.

The data disclosure comes as the administration is moving forward with efforts to shut down the department and move the OCR under the Department of Justice. Disability rights advocates and others have been critical of the move, while the administration says it will lead to more efficiency and do a better job protecting students’ rights. The majority of cases before the OCR involve students with disabilities, as has been the case historically.

In the early months of Trump’s second term, Education Secretary Linda McMahon fired hundreds of employees and closed seven regional OCR offices, leaving the department severely diminished. Its remaining civil rights workers have described untenable case loads and pressure to dismiss complaints.

The remaining attorneys “are navigating pure chaos,” said Rachel Gittleman, president of the American Federation of Government Employees Local 252, the union that represents OCR staff. “They are being asked to use the tools they have used for their entire careers not for good, not to protect students, but to harm students. You see it in the case dismissals, the cases that target trans students or people of color.”

In examining the new records, ProPublica found the extent to which the agency has shifted its focus, as the OCR places a new emphasis on complaints that men faced discrimination. The agency launched 172 investigations into alleged “discrimination against males,” compared with 104 cases of alleged “discrimination against females” in the period examined.

It also has investigated allegations that campus programs or scholarships excluded men. For example, it investigated a complaint against Skyline College in California that had been filed in 2023 but that President Joe Biden’s administration had not acted on, according to records from the university. The complaint alleged that Skyline discriminated against men by listing scholarships on its website that are exclusively for women and for promoting a program for women.

The civil rights office opened the investigation in mid-April, and two weeks later the community college agreed that it would remove the scholarships from its website and add language that the program was open to all students. A Skyline College spokesperson did not respond to questions from ProPublica.

At the same time, the agency chose to open fewer than 370 new investigations involving racial discrimination, even though about 5,000 complaints were filed.

Of those new investigations, about 11% involved allegations of discrimination against white students. Over the summer, the department announced investigations into five medical schools’ admissions policies.

“They are not allocating their resources to racial discrimination complaints, to sexual assault complaints, to the types of complaints that really often speak to systematic discrimination and problems,” said Johnathan Smith, managing director of education at the nonprofit National Center for Youth Law.

That organization also has sued the Education Department for similar information obtained by ProPublica; the lawsuit is pending.

We received two data files from the U.S. Department of Education — one file contained Office for Civil Rights cases that were initiated through a complaint or had a resolution from Jan. 20, 2025, through July 2026. The other listed investigation notices sent to institutions from Jan. 20, 2025, through April 9, 2026. Most Trump administration investigations started with a complaint, but if an investigation was initiated internally rather than through complaint, it appeared in only the latter file. As a result our tallies do not include internally initiated cases after April 9.

The two files list different issue descriptions to refer to the same cases, and we utilized issue descriptions from both datasets in our analysis. For example, “sexual violence” appears only in the complaint file descriptions, while “discrimination against males” appears only in the investigation notices file.

#4170. What Is Driving the Global Resurgence of Mangrove Forests?

📅 22 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Mangroves on the island of Mauritius in the Indian Ocean. Adam Burton

After decades of losses, mangrove trees are gaining ground on coastlines around the world, often in places they have never grown before. Paradoxically, scientists say, warming temperatures and the destruction of inland forests are helping to power the revival.

From the bayous of Louisiana to the backwaters of the Irrawaddy in Myanmar, from the lagoons of West Africa to the billabongs of northern Australia, mangroves are making a dramatic and unexpected comeback.

What a difference a couple of decades can make.

In 2007, ecologists from six countries asked in a letter published in the journal Science whether we would soon be living in “a world without mangroves.” One of the world’s most distinctive, biodiverse, and carbon-rich ecosystems was in rapid retreat in the face of encroaching agriculture, construction of sea walls, ports, and marinas, and rising sea levels. The mangroves seemed doomed.

But in recent months two separate studies have painted a very different picture. A high-resolution analysis of satellite imagery published in Science in June found that the stunted, salt-tolerant trees are now undergoing an “unexpected expansion,” healing after decades of destruction.

At the same time, a report on the State of the World’s Mangroves, published in September by a group of NGOs under the umbrella of the Global Mangrove Alliance, found that “mangroves are now increasing in extent, year on year,” said coauthor Mark Spalding of The Nature Conservancy.

It’s not that the destruction of mangroves has ended. But the Science study finds that new mangrove growth between 2010 and 2023 covered approximately 3,200 square miles, outpacing losses of around 2,400 square miles. Worldwide, mangrove cover is now back to the levels last seen in the 1980s.

The new findings are the surprising outcome of the most detailed analysis yet attempted of satellite imagery of mangroves over the past four decades, says Pete Bunting, a remote sensing analyst at Aberystwyth University in Wales, who worked on both studies. It shows that previous assessments had provided “a substantial underestimation of mangrove gains” over the past 15 years, says the Science study’s lead author Zhen Zhang of Tulane University in Louisiana.

“For the first time, these unique ecosystems are expanding at a faster rate than they are declining,” says Catherine Lovelock, a coastal ecologist at the University of Queensland, who was not involved in either study.

However, not all ecologists are celebrating.

For one thing, mangroves are recovering in some places thanks to environmental degradation elsewhere. For another, says Norman Duke, an ecologist at James Cook University in Queensland, Australia, and lead author of the 2007 letter, mangrove quantity should not be confused with quality. Lost old-growth mangrove forests, which harbor the most wildlife, are being replaced by less biodiverse and resilient new stands, he says. Contrary to the new studies, “the world’s mangroves are not bouncing back.”

A coral reef around the roots of mangrove trees in Raja Ampat, Indonesia. Wendy Mitchell / Mangrove Photography Awards 2026

Mangroves are unique trees, evolved with specialized roots that anchor in shallow, intertidal waters with slow currents, no frost, and plenty of sediment. Mangrove forests are critical biodiversity hotspots, supporting everything from barnacles to tigers, and are vital fish nurseries for the oceans. For coastal communities, they yield rich harvests of crabs and shellfish and form a protective barrier, absorbing winds and tidal surges during storms, and even moderating the energy of tsunamis. They also store large amounts of carbon — typically four times more than nearby tropical rainforests.

Zhang has recorded the biggest revivals in the river estuaries of northern Australia and river deltas in India. In each case, the new growth extends across more than 450 square miles. But the most surprising turnaround has been in Southeast Asian countries, which were once the scene of rapid mangrove destruction. Since 2010, they have accounted for more than 40 percent of global gains.

Myanmar is “still the most severely deforested country among major mangrove-holding nations,” says Zhang. But since 2010, its mangroves have grown in area by 10 percent. Indonesia tells a similar story. After major losses in past decades, due primarily to a boom in shrimp and fish aquaculture, mangroves have been stable since 2005, when the boom began to abate. The country remains home to around a quarter of the world’s mangroves.

So, what is going on? Why the dramatic upturn for a global ecosystem once considered all but doomed by human invasion and rising seas? The new remote-sensing analysis, despite its greater resolution, allows only tentative conclusions. “We don’t fully understand what is driving these gains,” says Bunting. His mapping cannot distinguish planted trees from natural recovery, for instance. But there is growing evidence that natural regeneration is powering the resurgence.

Sometimes, mangroves are recolonizing areas previously taken by humans but since abandoned. During the late 20th century, coastal communities across the tropics ripped out millions of acres of mangroves in East and Southeast Asia alone to make way for ponds where they raised fish and shrimp for lucrative international markets. But many of these ponds soon became unproductive through disease, pollution, or poor management, and were abandoned.

But such recolonization is only part of the story. “Some 65 percent of mangrove gains are in new places where mangroves have not grown before,” the State of the World’s Mangroves report finds. Sometimes climate change is helping extend the range of mangroves polewards. On the Mississippi Delta, they are overtaking salt marshes as warmer winters eliminate mangrove-killing frosts, restricting habitat for birds and foraging mammals that need open ground.

False-color satellite images show the growth of mangroves at the mouth of the Matamuhuri River in Bangladesh between 1990 and 2023. Source: Landsat imagery. Courtesy of Pete Bunting

The majority of the gains, however, “seem to occur in river mouths and deltas and are being driven by sediment coming off the land,” says Bunting. On reaching the ocean, the sediment settles, creating mudbanks that mangroves swiftly colonize. The unsettling paradox is that, in many cases, mangroves along coastlines are returning thanks to the destruction of their forest counterparts inland. The loss of trees and their roots destabilizes soils, causing erosion and the flow of sediment into rivers.

That is what has happened along the Yangon estuary in southern Myanmar. Part of the giant Irrawaddy Delta, the estuary stretches some 25 miles from the ocean to the country’s biggest city, Yangon. The estuary lost more than half its mangroves between 1988 and 2011 as rice farmers moved onto its shores and replaced mangroves with rice fields, erecting dikes to protect them from the salty water. But the mangroves have since made a dramatic comeback. By 2023, their coverage had increased almost tenfold to more than 13,000 acres, according to Phyu Phway Thant of the Myanmar Maritime University in Thanlyin, who has mapped the growth from satellite imagery.

The rice fields have not gone. Instead, almost all the growth was mangroves colonizing new mudbanks created from sediment dislodged by upstream sand mining and deforestation, which was driven by illegal logging and the expansion of agriculture along tributaries of the Yangon River.

This is not unusual. The growth of mudbanks in many estuaries has coincided with an increase in sediment brought down by rivers to coastal waters over the last 20 years, says the State of the World’s Mangroves report. Other examples include parts of the giant Amazon Delta in Brazil, the shores of peninsular Malaysia, and the North Island of New Zealand, which is at the southernmost limit of potential mangrove habitat.

“Rising sea levels should in theory restrict mangrove growth, as seas invade the intertidal areas where they grow,” says Lovelock, but at the mouths of many rivers, sediment deposition is creating land faster than the seas are engulfing it, creating new habitats that mangroves swiftly colonize. And once established, the dense networks of mangrove roots trap more sediment — enough, the State of the World’s Mangroves report concludes, to keep pace with current rising tides in many places.

Of course, this positive feedback loop can be confounded by upstream dams, which drastically reduce the amount of sediment being carried toward the ocean, stopping the growth of intertidal mud flats, and causing deltas to be drowned.

Shrimp farms in Prachuap Khiri Khan province in Thailand have displaced mangrove forests. Ingram Publishing

For this reason, there are serious concerns among ecologists about the fate of East Africa’s largest expanse of mangroves, on the delta of the River Rufiji in Tanzania. In recent years, new mangrove stands have grown on mudflats in the outer delta, says Bunting. But that growth could now abruptly halt. In August, the Tanzanian government completed the Julius Nyerere hydroelectric dam some 100 miles upstream. The environmental group WWF has estimated that the dam will trap most of the 16.6 million tons of sediment flowing down the river each year — initiating a cycle of delta retreat and mangrove loss.

The growth of new mangrove stands has been accompanied by a growing awareness among some governments and coastal communities of their value. This is especially true in Indonesia and other countries bordering the Indian Ocean that were hit by a tsunami in 2004 that killed more than 200,000 people. Villages that had kept their mangroves were much better protected than those that had converted them to fishponds.

A resulting fashion for planting new mangroves has had mixed results, however. Tens of millions of dollars have been spent on planting mangroves across the tropics in recent years, according to the NGO Wetlands International. But “the majority of these restoration projects have failed,” it said in a recent report. More than 80 percent of seedlings have died or been washed away by the tides.

In response, some conservation groups have begun helping communities manage coastal landscapes to encourage natural regeneration. On the north shore of Java, villagers have erected some 10 miles of near-shore brushwood barriers to create calm water where seedlings cast off by mangroves have become established.

And in the West African state of Guinea-Bissau, Wetlands International is recruiting locals to encourage the natural return of mangroves to abandoned rice fields. “We open up dikes and clean the old natural canals” to allow water carrying mangrove seedlings from nearby stands to return, says Abdoulaye Ndiaye, who runs the program. Nature does the rest. More than 6,000 acres have been restored since 2015.

Meanwhile, the greener landscapes seen in satellite images do not necessarily indicate that biodiversity has recovered, says Uday Pimple of King Mongkut’s University of Technology in Bangkok, who has been doing extensive field research in the mangrove swamps of Thailand.

In Phang Nga province in western Thailand — an area where tin mining, charcoal burning, and shrimp farming previously decimated coastal mangroves — he found that 90 percent of mangrove recovery since the 1980s has been through the natural regeneration of a range of mangrove species, creating biodiverse forest. But restored mangrove forests in Trat province in eastern Thailand, which appear identical on satellite images, were largely planted with a single species of mangrove, resulting in much less biodiversity.

A man plants mangrove seedlings in Banda Aceh, Indonesia. Hand-planted mangroves rarely reach maturity. Chaideer Mahyuddin / AFP via Getty Images

Clearly, the prognosis for mangroves remains contested. Bunting says, “It is quite possible that the future trend will be similar to the last 15 years,” with gains continuing to exceed losses. And in a study of the likely fate of mangroves in a world 2 degrees Celsius warmer, Daniel Friess, a colleague of Zhang at Tulane University, found reasons to believe the trees could thrive. Because, at least in some places, they seem well able to keep pace with rising sea levels. Because increased rainfall will often increase soil erosion inland and sediment deposition along shorelines, giving mangroves even more room to expand. And because warmer temperatures should push mangroves further outside the tropics, replacing many more salt marshes in Australia and the southern U.S., for instance, for better or for worse.

But Duke argues that the new mangrove stands colonizing the tropics may not be as resilient as their predecessors to the more severe weather triggered by climate change. Or as useful in capturing and holding onto carbon. “They are immature stands, not fully developed in either biomass or biodiversity,” he says. That will leave them ever more vulnerable “as damaging [weather] events become more severe and more frequent.” Recent research appears to support his pessimistic presumption.

Field studies in China’s Hainan province found that mature mangrove forests recovered much more quickly from tropical cyclones, with their high winds, strong rainfall, and storm surges, compared with younger forests. The researchers stressed the importance of protecting old-growth mangroves rather than relying on new stands to replace them. Without such a strategy, humanity may yet face what Duke feared: “a world without mangroves.”

Two things worth knowing straight away. EDSA Day is a working day again, so 25 February is a normal Thursday for most people. And All Souls’ Day stays a special non-working day, which is what turns Undas into a four-day weekend.

Count only the breaks that need zero leave, and 2027 gives you seven long weekends. The last one is easy to miss because it spills into 2028.

That is 24 days of long weekends on the calendar before you touch your leave credits. The Holy Week and Undas breaks are the strongest of the lot because they run four days, which is enough for a short trip abroad without feeling rushed.

The year end break is the sleeper. Rizal Day on Thursday and the Last Day of the Year on Friday sit right before New Year’s Day 2028, which falls on a Saturday. Four days off, no leave, and it lands when half your barkada is home from abroad anyway.

This is where the year gets good. Three breaks each turn into nine or ten days with just three days of leave.

#3748. Pluralistic: Adversarial interoperability is a team sport (21 Oct 2026)

📅 21 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Pluralistic: Daily links from Cory Doctorow

Enshittification is particularly galling because (in theory, at least), digital technology should be really hard to enshittify.

That's because the only digital computer we know how to build is the "Turing-complete, universal von Neumann machine," which is to say, a calculating engine capable of computing every valid program. In some deep, important, foundational sense, every computer is the same as every other computer. Sure, they vary in terms of speed, storage and RAM, but given enough time and electricity, every computer can run every program.

This has profound implications for enshittification. Say a tech boss takes away a feature you value (as when Sonos pushed an update that removed nearly all the functionality from its "smart" speakers), or adds a feature you hate (like when Chamberlain blocked third-party apps from controlling its garage doors so it could force its customers to view seven ads before they could open or close their garages). At that point, a programmer can write an "interoperable" program that restores the good feature or blocks the bad one.

This program can be costlessly and instantaneously distributed to every affected technology user, and those users will likely pay something for it. After all, enshittification is a way to shift value away from technology users to tech companies, which makes the case for you paying for disenshittification technology that wrestles that value back.

All of that means that, in a state of nature, companies that are contemplating enshittification need to worry that their greed will tempt new companies to enter the market and poach their customers and suppliers. This alone might be enough to frighten companies off from enshittificatory gambits, and if companies press ahead regardless, then they open market opportunities for better companies that can costlessly, instantaneously usurp the original manufacturer's relationship with their entire affected customer base.

That's how it should work. But in 1998, Bill Clinton signed the Digital Millennium Copyright Act, and Section 1201 of the DMCA establishes a felony (punishable by a five-year prison sentence and a $500,000 fine) for reverse-engineering and modifying technology without the manufacturer's permission. Then, the US Trade Rep spent the next 25 years making sure that every US trading partner had an identical (or even worse!) "anticircumvention" law:

https://pluralistic.net/2025/05/26/babyish-radical-extremists/#cancon

The result is a grotesquely asymmetrical digital world, in which companies are free to use the flexibility of digital computers to downgrade or brick your digital devices, but where you commit a crime if you avail yourself of a countermeasure to get more out of something you own. This is the core technological factor at play in enshittification.

Enter AI. Thanks to vibe-coding, everyday people are rolling their own software for their devices and building the tools to shatter the digital locks that manufacturers use to block disenshittification. An excellent article by 404 Media's Jason Koebler runs down some of these hacks, including drop-in replacements for smart TV software that blocks the endless ads and surveillance that have made TVs all but unusable:

https://www.404media.co/reverse-engineer-anything-is-the-new-wild-west-of-ai/

These AI tools mix several techniques to disenshittify users' devices. One major component is automating the installation of existing alternative firmware for these devices that have been (possibly illegally) created by hobbyists and uploaded to various code repositories. Installing these alternative OSes is typically a gnarly task, requiring the user to do a lot of complex, fiddly, highly technical work to bypass their devices' digital locks.

In a world without anticircumvention law, a company that sold alternative OSes could hire a ton of programmers and UX developers to smooth over this process, but this is beyond the reach of a typical hobbyist or even a community of hobbyists, especially if they must operate in the shadows, under the threat of civil and criminal liability.

There's a useful analogy here to the trajectory of desktop versions of GNU/Linux. The first time I tried to install Linux (on an original iMac, in the late 1990s), the installation process included a multi-week post-installation wrangle that saw me (often unsuccessfully) compiling key modules from source code and then painstakingly configuring them to get the computer's graphics and I/O working reliably.

Fast forward 30+ years and installing Linux is trivial, thanks to highly capitalized companies like Canonical (makers of Ubuntu, the flavor of Linux I've used for nearly 20 years now). Installing Ubuntu today merely requires that you download the OS to a thumbdrive, reboot your computer using the drive as a startup disk, and click "install." Canonical's installer automatically hunts down the correct software for your hardware and configures it. Canonical writes and contributes to a lot of parts of the Linux OS, but the most important work it does is the fiddly, painstaking, labor- and capital-intensive work of perfecting that installer so it Just Works.

Much of what a vibe-coded jailbreaking tool does is stand in for Canonical, but for hobbyist OSes for smart TVs and speakers and many other devices. These LLMs patiently research every weird error and peccadillo and parse through message boards and logfiles detailing other hobbyists' struggles to get the code working and methodically apply and adapt those solutions until your device Just Works.

Some of these vibe-coded interop tools go farther than that. According to Koebler's coverage, some device owners had their coding assistants scratch-build entirely new, heavily customized OSes for their devices, complete with bypasses that allow this new code to be installed despite the manufacturer's countermeasures which are designed to prevent this. Delightfully, many of these users have created software that revives their bricked, discontinued devices. This poses the possibility of a world in which manufacturers no longer have the last word in your use of your own property: the mere fact that the manufacturer lost interest in a device and killswitched it does not mean that you must consign that valuable device to a landfill to become immortal, toxic e-waste.

All of this is good news. Some of the most sophisticated jailbreaking tools (such as those on the super viral "Reverse Engineer Anything" Github page) run locally, on your own computer, without invoking the "frontier models" that the big, awful AI companies have created:

This is good even if you're just modifying your own devices to get them to more closely conform to your own use-case. LLMs are genuinely good at coming up with the abstruse and obscure configuration lines and other incantations to get your technology working. Recently, I used a local model to fine-tune the power-management on my new laptop in ways that go beyond the options that are exposed through user-friendly graphic interfaces. The LLM spat out a grub "GRUB_CMDLINE_LINUX_DEFAULT" line that (again) Just Worked.

The last time I had to do this, I spent days painstakingly trouble-shooting options by trial-and-error. Using a chatbot to fill in the gaps left by decisions about which configuration options to place within reach of regular users and which to reserve for power-users has made my computer much more useful to me for the things I want to do, like writing and making collages.

The reality is that it's just not practical for technology developers to turn every conceivable function of your computer into a simple-to-use, accessible tool. A large part of technology design is about leaving things out (or, as my favorite Eno Oblique Strategy goes, "Be the first person to not do something that no one else has ever thought of not doing before"). No matter how thoughtful and diligent a technology designer is, they will never fully anticipate the needs and exigencies of their users. That's why the motto of the disability rights movement is "Nothing about us without us."

The users of technology know more about their use-cases than can ever be captured through a requirements gathering exercise and user testing. One of the best and proudest legacies of technology is the lineage of "user-friendly" programming tools that let nontechnical people design their own applications that exactly met their needs, from Hypercard to Visual Basic to Scratch, a lineage that vibe-coding tools are arguably part of:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/09/25/other-people/#right-there

But these tools are not perfect (nothing is). For all that Hypercard lets you do many things that would otherwise require a skilled programmer, it was also always limited relative to, say, writing MacOS code in Objective C. Your Hypercard program might do exactly what you needed it to, but slowly, and sometimes unreliably, and with serious limitations, such as a relatively low ceiling for the number of records you could add to a database.

Vibe-coded personal apps aren't necessarily subject to these limitations, but they come with some significant risks. Vibe-code can have serious security vulnerabilities that expose the user to things like identity theft or sextortion. It's conceivable that a vibe-coded garage-door opener app might make it possible for attackers to open your garage door when you're not home (or worse, when you are!). Depending on your risk-tolerance and expertise, these might be acceptable prices, or they might be too high.

But it's important to clearly identify the problem here: if the alternative OS you install on your smart TV exposes you to privacy-invading hack attacks, the problem is the defect in that software. The problem isn't that you had the temerity to rip out the manufacturer's own software and replace it with code of your choosing. You bought that TV, it's yours, and you don't owe the manufacturer an obligation to expose yourself to its own, built-in, intentional surveillance and invasive ads.

Here's a parallel: you have the right to put any shoelaces in your running shoes. Sure, Nike might prefer you to lace up your Jordans with its expensive OEM laces, but that's Nike's problem, not yours. Now, Nike might argue that by using no-name laces, you run the risk of having a lace break while you're running, which could also break your ankle. That's certainly a possibility: shoelaces do break, and Nike might be justifiably proud of the quality of its laces. But if your generic laces snap (and so does your ankle), the problem is that the shoelaces you chose were defective. The problem is not that you chose someone else's laces.

Your stuff is yours. You can put anyone's milk on your cereal, not just the milk that Kellogg's endorses. If the milk you choose is sour and you have to pour your cereal down the toilet, that's too bad, but it doesn't mean that Kellogg's should have the right to shame you for having the temerity to choose the milk you preferred, and it certainly doesn't militate for DMCA-style enforcement to coerce you into using Kellogg's official milk in the name of protecting you from food wastage.

I raise this because there's a live debate over this subject, which is well represented in the latest 404 Media podcast:

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UbG1qwxMtU0

In the podcast, Koebler and his 404 co-founder Joseph Cox express misgivings about various possible uses of "slop interop." Koebler discusses the fact that John Deere sells the same physical tractor at many different price-points, depending on which software a farmer chooses, and raises the possibility that a farmer might vibe-code software that replicates the high-end functionality without paying for it. Koebler discusses two possible downsides to this: first, that the vibe-coded software might be defective and expose the farmer to risk; and second, a question about whether it's fair or just for a farmer to get those additional functions of their tractor without paying for them.

I think the first objection is entirely legitimate. The software you run on your expensive tractor should be secure, both for your own benefit and for the security of our food supply. That's actually a pretty good reason to ditch John Deere's own software, which is very insecure:

https://pluralistic.net/2022/05/08/about-those-kill-switched-ukrainian-tractors/

But I think the second question – whether it's legit to get this functionality without paying John Deere for it – is spurious. Sure, if John Deere provides you with that functionality, then they can send you the bill for it. But if you provide yourself with that functionality, using software you procured independently of John Deere, then John Deere has no right to get paid for it, and they certainly shouldn't have the right to stop you.

That tractor is yours. The fact that John Deere manufactured it is irrelevant. If you pay a contractor to build a house for you and later on pay a different contractor to add another bathroom to it, you don't owe the first contractor anything. It's your house (and if that bathroom has a leaky toilet, that's a matter between you and the second contractor, and not a reason to force you to only use your original contractor).

The law defines a narrow slice of functions that manufacturers get to control: those ideas that a patent examiner deems "novel, useful and nonobvious." It's legit (and good) for commercial operators to copy and improve the ideas of their rivals and competitors. It's why skilled tradespeople cluster together in the same geographic area.

Take Clerkenwell, the neighborhood in London where precision machining was invented by the jewelers and machinists who clustered there during the Longitude rewards. The reason all those competitors sought such close promixity to one another was because they wanted to copy the new techniques their rivals were developing (even if that meant that their rivals would be able to copy them):

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude_rewards

If you're old enough to remember the advent of spreadsheets and the rollout of Visicalc, Lotus 1-2-3 and Excel, you'll know that every time a software vendor invented a new spreadsheet feature, all the other ones immediately copied it. This wasn't bad for spreadsheets: it was good for spreadsheets, just as copying your competitors was good for precision machining in Clerkenwell.

Everything from GNU/Linux (a feature-for-feature clone of AT&T Unix) to SAMBA (a plug-compatible clone of Microsoft's SMB) to iWork (file-compatible, feature compatible clones of MS Office's apps) came about through this "adversarial interoperability." What's more, the heyday of this kind of adversarial interop was also the heyday of disenshittification, the era in which any anti-feature installed by your existing vendor could be countered by a disenshittifying patch from a new one:

https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2019/06/adversarial-interoperability-reviving-elegant-weapon-more-civilized-age-slay

The question of whether vibe-coded software is any good is separate from the question of whether it's good to replace the software in your devices, even if you replace it with software that has features that the manufacturer would normally charge a premium for. You are under no obligation to arrange your commercial affairs to the financial benefit of manufacturers, and anyone who claims to be surprised that someone they sold a device to used it to benefit themselves at the manufacturer's expenses is either disingenuous…or unserious. You bought it, it's yours.

But just as this vibe-coded adversarial interop has drawn both valid and spurious criticism, it's also drawn excitement that is partially justified and partially overblown.

When it comes to reviving bricked devices or rooting out the surveillance and ads from your smart TV, vibe-coded interop tools are genuinely exciting. These are the kinds of enshittification that can truly be remediated with individual jailbreaks.

But the real, meaty examples of enshittification can't be addressed on an individual level – they require systemic fixes. If you jailbreak your iPhone so that it can use alternative app stores, that fact is not sufficient to cause those alternative app stores to come into existence. If we want to stop Apple from levying a $100b "app tax" on the digital economy, it's not enough for individuals to vibe-code their own iPhone patches. We need to repeal the law that bans jailbreaking iPhones, so that a would-be alternative app store can raise capital and pay programmers and tech support staff and salespeople to assemble rival catalogs of software and help customers figure out how to get up their phones to use it.

The same goes for jailbreaking your printer so it accepts generic ink. Sure, this may work for you if you're up for refilling your own cartridges, and I'm sure that you can find someone on Alibaba who will sell you ink by the gallon. But the mere fact that a few users have secretly and privately jailbroken their inkjets won't conjure up a generic inkjet cartridge manufacturing sector. For that to happen, we need to make jailbreaking printers legal, not just possible.

Indeed, if we want to improve the quality of all that jailbreaking code the chatbots are writing and circulating, we need to be able to put those jailbreaks in public repositories for public inspection and public critique. It's possible for some of the code produced by an LLM to be slop, and for other code to be fine, and for yet a third tranche of code to be remediable slop that can be made robust and reliable through auditing and refactoring. You can't figure out which is which if you have to keep that vibe code a secret on pain of a long prison-sentence.

I think there's good cause to be excited at the prospect of getting your devices working better, or just working at all. I think that even if the code stops working after a while, that's still fine – either you got more use out of your device than you would have without the new code, or maybe you just make more code that runs for a while for you, and then you do it again.

These individual improvements to your devices and technology are worth celebrating. But they are no substitute for systemic intervention. If we're going to have a new, good internet, a post-American internet that truly disenshittifies America's defective tech exports and replaces them with robust, transparent digital public goods, we need to make reverse-engineering legal again. The agency charged with regulating Colorado’s first-in-the-nation legal recreational marijuana market potentially put consumers at risk of using contaminated products, a new state audit found.

The report pointed to inconsistent oversight by the Marijuana Enforcement Division and loose state rules around testing marijuana products or contaminants. As a result, it found, nearly 75% of marijuana processed for eventual sale from 2023 through 2025 evaded testing for pesticides.

The audit, released Monday, identified a host of issues, including regulators’ failure to meet regular inspection schedules or to conduct sufficient random checks at dispensaries for contaminated products. It also found that delayed agency warnings to the public allowed potentially contaminated products to remain on shelves for months, sometimes years.

The audit’s conclusions echo several findings of an investigation this year by The Denver Gazette and ProPublica into the Marijuana Enforcement Division’s lapses in policing contaminants entering the legal marketplace.

In one case highlighted by the newsrooms, it took 15 months for a manufacturer to discontinue production of a marijuana sleep aid pill despite consumers warning regulators that the product was harming their livers. The pills had herbal extracts that researchers say can cause liver damage.

Auditors said they did not identify evidence of “widespread health and safety issues” but did find that deficient regulations still left consumers vulnerable.

The audit found that unsafe products had remained on the market for as long as three years before the Marijuana Enforcement Division issued a public warning. The auditor’s review of the agency’s health and safety advisories found that, on average, from 2023 through 2025, contaminated products that were the subject of an advisory remained on shelves for more than seven months. That’s the same number of months the news organizations found contaminated products stayed on shelves before warnings were issued.

The audit also found that it took the agency three and a half months, on average, in 2025 to issue an advisory once it launched an investigation into potential contamination. Even after investigators determined an advisory was needed, it still took the agency a month on average to issue one.

“These issues limit MED’s ability to meet its core objective to identify product safety risks and to help ensure that licensed marijuana businesses meet product safety requirements,” the audit stated. “As a result, there is an increased risk that marijuana products sold in Colorado may have contaminants or that the potency listed on the labels is inaccurate.”

We reported that a top Marijuana Enforcement Division official acknowledged in a meeting with industry representatives that the amount of chemically converted hemp being sold as marijuana is greater than the agency has publicly disclosed.

State regulators announced a plan to crack down on companies that illegally sell cheaper and potentially hazardous hemp products as marijuana, citing potential tax avoidance and “serious risks to public safety.”

Colorado regulators discussed a proposal to do away with the current testing system that allows marijuana manufacturers to choose their own samples. Instead, independent labs or contractors would collect the samples.

The Denver Gazette and ProPublica reported that intoxicating hemp has seeped into Colorado’s marijuana market because the state had failed to adopt safety measures. Tests found signs of hemp in vapes sold at marijuana dispensaries.

After hemp companies figured out how to convert CBD into the high-inducing compound THC, Colorado became one of the first states to ban chemically converted hemp products, first with regulatory advisories and then with legislation.

President Donald Trump signed the Farm Bill, which legalizes hemp. Congress’ intent was to help farmers along with people who believe hemp’s high levels of the nonintoxicating compound CBD can help with seizures, pain and sleep.

Colorado became the first state to allow retail sales of marijuana for recreational use.

Colorado allows marijuana businesses to test far less frequently than required by state guidelines if they can show consistent compliance with testing rules. The reduced testing program results “in a significant portion of the marijuana product being sold in Colorado not undergoing testing to ensure it is safe,” the audit stated. Manufacturers also can rely on other testing exemptions, such as for certain marijuana-derived skin and body products, but there is “limited MED oversight” of how manufacturers determine they qualify for an exemption.

Auditors reported that a survey of MED investigators found that more than half said there are or may be widespread marijuana product safety issues in Colorado’s regulated market.

In a statement released Monday night, the Marijuana Enforcement Division emphasized that state auditors found no evidence “to support claims of widespread health and safety issues” with marijuana products for sale in Colorado, but the agency agreed the audit identified areas for improvement. It said that among other things, it would be assessing exemptions to tests currently allowed under state laws and regulations.

The state auditor’s review “allows us to take a critical look at how we can continue to carry out our work, consistent with our commitment to integrity, safety, and continuous operational improvement,” Dominique Mendiola, senior director of the MED, said in a statement.

The auditor’s report highlighted a criticism her agency has repeatedly sidestepped: the absence of independent collection of samples for safety testing. The Denver Gazette and ProPublica found that more than half of states with regulated marijuana markets rely on private labs or an independent third party to collect samples. Colorado marijuana businesses are free to collect their own samples submitted for testing.

The enforcement division’s own investigators told auditors that, as a result, Colorado relies largely on an honor code to ensure the safety of marijuana sold in the state’s dispensaries.

Auditors said that allowing businesses to select their own samples “increases the risk that businesses do not pick samples that are representative of the overall quality of their marijuana product.”

MED regulators at the start of the year said they planned to pursue an overhaul that would have required private lab personnel to collect samples, but they abandoned that effort after manufacturers said it would increase their costs. On Monday, Kyle Lambert, the enforcement division’s deputy senior director, told legislators that the agency opted against an overhaul this year, chiefly because he said it would be a geographic challenge to do away with self-sampling for rural businesses.

“It was decided now wasn’t the right time to propose that as a policy change,” Lambert said, adding that the agency still might revisit the issue.

State Sen. Byron Pelton, a Republican from Sterling, pushed back, saying it was a loophole he thinks the agency should consider closing.

“If I’m a shady character, I know which products I’m going to go sampling and which ones I’m not,” he said. “That’s a pretty gray area to me.”

The audit also questioned the agency’s reliance on businesses voluntarily recalling contaminated marijuana products, saying that “some of these recalls did not succeed at stopping the businesses from selling potentially unsafe product.”

Just last month, The Denver Gazette and ProPublica reported that a Colorado manufacturer voluntarily flagged 49 of its popular marijuana vapes for recall, nearly six weeks after a third-party lab notified the company and regulators that it detected methylene chloride, a chemical the Environmental Protection Agency has sought to ban for most uses because it can cause cancer. The MED issued a health and safety advisory a day after the publications first reported the manufacturer had warned dispensaries to stop selling the vapes, which consumers had been purchasing for over a year.

#3652. The Accidental Activists of Data Center Alley

These would-be rebels in Northern Virginia joined the fight against data centers mostly to stop the encroachment on their homes and exclusive private communities.

I am hiking on a nature park that cuts through 45 miles of Northern Virginia, crisscrossing grassy terrain, wetlands, wooden bridges, and goldenrod, and then a dozen promontories offering the most unnatural of sights—windowless, hulking block-long data centers and their attendant transmission lines and substations. I heard “the hum” of the centers before I saw them; their sound resembled a horde of cicadas on meth. I felt nervous, and somewhat overwhelmed, as I counted roughly 20 of them in just the two miles I walked on the Washington and Old Dominion Railroad Trail in Loudoun County.

Here is the country’s thickest concentration of data centers—233 centers in all, over 3 percent of the county’s overall land mass. Data Center Alley, as it’s called, is a harbinger of what is to come in this country if tech hyperscaling continues unchecked. But Loudoun County is not only an example of tech infrastructure running amuck. It’s also an example of a semi-successful battle to press pause.

The week I visited in September, local activists had just secured a significant victory when the county’s Board of Supervisors voted 7–1 to impose a 12-month pause on board action for data centers and electrical substations. It was one win for the broader opposition, who were cross-partisan, unlikely dissidents in their 50s and 60s, who joined the fight mostly to protect their own homes and exclusive private communities. Some of them even refused to say a bad word about AI, claiming the fear of data centers in popular imagination was “overblown,” even though they themselves had been organizing against them as they encroached on their homes.

One of these accidental activists is Jessica Medeiros, 55. I met her in front of her home, in the Oak Trail Square development in Sterling. She works with juvenile offenders by day, as a pretrial officer in nearby Prince William County. In her off hours, she is just another empty nester of two grown children, hanging with her boyfriend, enjoying frequent outdoor runs in the area and five-times-a-day walks with her dog. Well, that was before the gargantuan blue Vantage VA2 data center at the end of her street, four city blocks long and wide, rose up.

That Vantage VA2 center happens to be one of the worst ones out there. For one thing, it isn’t connected to the electrical grid, meaning its energy comes from on-site or nearby power plants. These plants are primarily powered by natural gas; the VA2 center uses gas turbines and backup emergency diesel generators. This reliance means that such hubs risk being louder than normal, filthier than normal, and ultimately worse for the environment than normal. As a case in point, on the morning we met, Medeiros pointed out where the soot from the day usually falls.

More and more developers have sought power plant equipment like turbines and generators to elide regional power grids, which are now stressed under the data center’s electricity requirements.

To me, these data centers are a physical embodiment of both the decline of the rule of law and the rise of the tech oligarchs. They are, of course, part the national conversation around AI and have become a center of the activism against it. In the first half of 2026, the Data Center Watch project by AI security company 10a Labs found that at least 75 data center projects worth $130 billion were disrupted by local groups in the first quarter and at least 45 projects worth $68 billion in the second. The Data Center Opposition Report found that 640,000 people are in anti-data center Facebook groups in the United States. Gen Zers have joined groups like New Jersey’s Climate Revolution Action Network, or CRAN.

“To me, this center…is just indicative of what is happening in our country, because the money and the power are taking over the lives of citizens,” says Medeiros, a petite blonde who describes herself as having the energy of a puppy, with the swift gait of a runner, despite a recent foot injury. She was rapidly walking around the perimeter of a building that looks like a post-lobotomy IKEA. It has got the same cheerful cobalt exterior but no furniture, customers, or meatballs inside, only racks and heat. “Last week, Trump eliminated the, what was it? Oh yeah, the emissions level requirements for these power plant. For people like us, that basically screws us. We don’t have those protections anymore.” (The Trump EPA had recently exempted data centers from publicly disclosing their emissions.)

Medeiros and her neighbor Greg Pirio, 75, a government contractor who specializes in foreign aid, are two of a number of neighbors who have thrown themselves into protesting the Vantage plant, attending community meetings and town halls, doing press, creating systems for monitoring noise and air quality, and petitioning their local representatives.

Medeiros’ voice is a rasp, now, as well, which she attributes to the centers.

One of the key culprits is the fine particulate matter these centers create.

But that’s not their only threat. Their constant whining noises should be understood as noise pollution, which the World Health Organization has found to cause high blood pressure, heart disease, and sleep disturbances. (Two of the people I spoke to mentioned sleep disturbances since the centers opened near their homes.) Hearing damage is a possibility if the roar of the center goes above 85 decibels—and the constant sound, all day, near schools has also been connected to lower educational outcomes, according to a forthcoming study from researchers at Brown University. Google’s browser will flash two types of warnings based on whether the site is deemed dangerous or just suspicious.

In a blog post published Tuesday, Google outlined three new measures designed to help Chrome users browse the web more safely and securely. The new measures apply to Chrome both on computers and mobile devices.

First up is protection against suspicious websites. Chrome has long warned you about dangerous sites that are known to be malicious. Now, the browser will also notify you if a site is deemed suspicious. The warning that appears on the screen may specifically tell you that the site doesn’t seem right, as it’s similar to other sites associated with phishing, malware, and scams. You can click a Learn More link to delve further.

If you’re concerned about the site and want to follow Google’s advice, just close it or click the Return to safety button to go back to the previous page. If you’re certain the site is safe, you can mark it as safe and use it normally.

Google rates a website as suspicious by comparing it with other deceptive or misleading sites. Gemini also plays a role here, as the AI analyzes the site’s content, the brand it represents, the site’s intent, the domain’s reputation, and other factors to derive a scam likelihood score.

With this update, Chrome can now distinguish between suspicious sites and dangerous sites by flashing different warnings. The alert for a dangerous site paints the screen red and strongly recommends that you leave the page. The alert for a suspicious site is more subtle, giving you the option to still visit and use the site if you’re certain it’s legit.

“Suspicious site warnings notify users when a page exhibits deceptive patterns commonly associated with known malicious sites,” Google said in its blog post. “These new warnings are distinguished by format, iconography, color, and text, to make it easy for users to quickly and confidently make the best choice for themselves based on the context. By tailoring the warning UI in this way, we are able to safely expand our coverage to display a higher volume of protective warnings.”

Google has also beefed up Chrome’s overall scam-detection capabilities. Last year, the company added Gemini’s AI smarts to Chrome to thwart tech support scams. Here, the scammer behind a malicious site tries to convince you that you’ve been infected by malware, only to try to sell you security software or gain remote access to your computer.

Now, this same type of AI-powered security will alert you against a wider range of online scams, including fake antivirus and phony browser updates. For this, Google uses a server-side Gemini model with more advanced capabilities. The model analyzes the content of the page, the reputation of the domain, and other signals to reach a final conclusion.

To protect your privacy, the company promises that the amount of data shared with the back-end server is minimized and is shared only when the site triggers high levels of scam signals. Any page content sent from your computer or mobile device isn’t logged, remembered, or used to train the AI, Google added.

ClickFix is a type of social engineering scam that tricks you into manually copying and running malicious commands on your own computer, ostensibly to fix a technical issue that doesn’t exist. To take advantage of the new security defenses in Chrome, you need to be running the browser in Enhanced Protection mode. This is the strongest level available in the program’s Safe Browsing process. To enable this in Chrome on the desktop or mobile, head to Settings and select Privacy and security. On the desktop, click the option for Security; on mobile, tap the option for Safe Browsing. Then choose the setting for Enhanced Protection.

You should also turn on Enhanced protection for your Google account. For this, sign in to your Google account page on the web. Select the option for Security & sign-in. Scroll down the page and select the option for Enhanced Safe Browsing for your account. Then just turn on the switch.

#4347. Whitsundays Long Weekend: What Filipinos in Australia Should Plan

📅 21 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Most Filipinos in Australia save their annual leave for one trip, usually in December. Three weeks home, a long list of relatives, and by the time you land back in Sydney or Melbourne, the leave balance is empty and long weekends are all you have left for the rest of the year.

The three days never feel like enough to plan a proper trip around, so most of those weekends get spent at home. The Whitsundays answer that better than almost anywhere else in the country. A group of 74 islands off the north Queensland coast, with water clear enough to see coral from the surface. No passport or visa complications. You book a flight, a room and one boat trip, and the planning is finished.

Three days also happens to be the right length for a Whitsundays tour. Fly out Friday morning, spend Saturday on the Great Barrier Reef, and you are home by Sunday evening with a holiday behind you and your annual leave still sitting there untouched.

Because Hamilton Island holds the whole trip. Three days is a tight window, and none of it goes on transfers, because nothing here is further than a short walk or a free shuttle.

There is nothing foreign to manage either. No currency to exchange, and your phone plan and Medicare card still work. A three-day Whitsundays holiday does not feel like a rushed version of a real one. And the best part? You only have to do minimal planning.

Any long weekend works, but some are better value than others. School holidays push flights and rooms up. Skip them if your dates can move.

Fly to Hamilton Island. It is the only island here with a commercial airport, so the whole journey is one flight and a ten-minute shuttle to your room.

There is a cheaper option. Fly to Proserpine on the mainland, take a coach to Airlie Beach, then a ferry to the island. That adds about three hours each way, so it is not worth it on a long weekend.

There are two bases. Hamilton Island is the easier one, Airlie Beach is the cheaper one, and for a long weekend the easier one usually wins.

Hamilton Island is a resort island, so there is no travel left once you land. Rooms run from apartments with kitchens up to resort rooms at Catseye Beach. There are no private cars here, so everyone walks, takes a golf buggy, or catches the free shuttle that loops every ten to fifteen minutes. Hamilton Island cruises leave from the marina, a short walk or one shuttle ride from your room. With kids or older parents along, this is the one to book. Rooms, beaches, and the marina sit close together, so you don’t need to pack and travel all day. If your parents are flying in from the Philippines, our guide on how to get an Australia tourist visa for Filipinos covers their application.

Airlie Beach costs noticeably less, with more hostels, self-contained units and restaurants per block. The catch is a ferry every time you want to be on an island, which eats into three days.

With Hamilton Island as your base, a Whitsundays island tour needs no transfers. A day to arrive, one for the reef, one for the island itself. Book the reef day first, since it leaves at a fixed time, then fit the other two around it.

You will land mid-morning or early afternoon, so keep this day light. Drop your bags and spend the afternoon close to where you are staying.

This is a long day, and it runs from early morning to early evening. Eat a proper breakfast, apply sunscreen and bring a hat.

In-water guides go out with each group, so first-time snorkellers are never on their own. Children from five years old can come. There is no alcohol on board, since the vessel is not licensed. Save that for dinner.

The last day belongs to the island itself. A hike, a long breakfast and a buggy. Work backwards from your flight time. The airport is ten minutes away; the hike up Passage Peak is not.

Every figure below was checked in October 2026, and flights and rooms shift with the season, so read those two as a base rather than a quote. Book the flights, room and the cruise early.

Put the two room rows side by side and Airlie Beach comes out a couple of hundred dollars a night cheaper, which seems practical till you add a ferry crossing each way, every day. On a three-day trip, that saving buys less than it looks like, because the thing you run short of here is time, not money.

Most long weekends end in a small regret for the beach you never reached, the lookout you ran past and all the other spots you missed in the rush. Hamilton Island is small, but there is more on it than its size suggests and three days cover it properly. You fly home knowing the weekend paid you back.
