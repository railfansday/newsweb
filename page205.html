#4197. Arctic Wildlife Research Suffers as Geopolitical Competition Grows

📅 15 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

A polar bear on sea ice in the Arctic Ocean. Sebnem Coskun / Anadolu via Getty Images

As national security and the hunt for rare minerals come to dominate Arctic agendas, governments are slashing support for wildlife research. Scientists say the loss of monitoring is unprecedented and comes as climate change dramatically reshapes life in the Far North.

Decades ago, one of the world’s largest subpopulations of nesting eider ducks spent its summers on tiny Mitivik Island, near the mouth of Hudson Bay. But as concern grew, in the 1990s, over their steep decline globally, the Canadian government set up a rudimentary research station near the island in the hopes of forestalling a collapse.

The modest investment paid enormous dividends over the next 30 years as Inuit partners from nearby Southampton Island, who hunt common and king eiders for food and feathers, worked with scientists from all over the world to solve the mystery of where the birds spent their winters and the causes of, and remedy for, their initial decline. In the field for months at a time, researchers also gained critical insights into how climate change, disease, diminishing sea ice, and polar bears were affecting bird populations.

As remarkably successful as the pioneering research station was, though, Canada’s Environment and Climate Change department quietly shut it down in 2024. By then, the number of nesting eiders had crashed again, from a high of 8,000 pairs to just a few hundred.

Today, federal research continues in only three of the 14 migratory bird sanctuaries in the Arctic. Hundreds of other wildlife research endeavors in the circumpolar world, including in Greenland and the U.S., have also been shuttered in recent years.

Like many other agencies and programs operated by Environment and Climate Change Canada, the Canadian Wildlife Service has suffered from the high cost of conducting research in the Arctic, the increasing likelihood of polar bear encounters as sea ice recedes, and a series of crushing funding cuts. In the most recent blow, the Canadian government in 2025 committed to reducing the Environment and Climate Change workforce by 10 percent.

In the past, funding to monitor wildlife in the Arctic often had to be justified by its relevance to oil and gas development and how roads, pipelines, and oil wells would affect animals such as migrating caribou, according to Larry Hinzman, an expert in Arctic change at the University of Alaska, Fairbanks. But as the search for Arctic oil and gas began to ramp down in the late 2000s, due to low natural gas prices and breakthroughs in fracking for oil and gas that spurred new production in the south, climate change became a priority.

The current circumpolar decline in funding, Hinzman said, is not just about rising costs. It stems from governments shifting priorities once again, this time from climate change to national security issues and the search for rare minerals. According to the Harvard Kennedy School’s Belfer Center for Science and International Affairs, geopolitical tensions are also undermining the work of the Arctic Council, an international forum comprising the eight Arctic countries that plays a key role in monitoring environmental changes in the region.

Wildlife technician Uli Steiner tags a king eider netted on Mitivik Island before research efforts were halted in 2024. Grant Gilchrist

While support for research in the Arctic wanes, Canada is investing $405 million to advance the construction of a new highway to the Arctic coast to facilitate resource development and connect remote communities, part of a $40 billion plan to assert sovereignty over its Arctic territory.

Research in Alaska has been particularly hard hit by the suspension of hundreds of climate-based collaborations with Russia following its invasion of Ukraine, and by the closure last year of the Arctic Research Consortium of the United States after the National Science Foundation eliminated its operating grants. Between 2021 and 2024, the number of U.S. research publications on climate change in Greenland fell by 32 percent. And in July the Greenland Institute of Natural Resources, the territory’s leading environmental research center, temporarily halted any new collaboration with U.S. partners when President Donald Trump threatened to take over the island.

“There have always been ebbs and flows in the support for environmental monitoring in the Arctic,” said Hinzman. “But what we are seeing now is unprecedented. It’s heartbreaking because we need to know more than ever how to respond to a warming Arctic that is affecting almost every facet of life.”

The decline in Arctic research comes at a time when at least 96 Arctic and sub-Arctic mammal and bird species may be responding to warmer winters, earlier spring snowmelt, and profound landscape changes by migrating earlier in the year and traveling farther afield, sometimes in new directions.

But dozens of studies have shown that these journeys don’t always lead to more food. And they often expose birds to additional parasites and pathogens and put them at greater risk of predation. The new migrations also happen at a time when critical stopover sites — like the Salton Sea, in California — are being badly degraded by drought and development.

Polar bears and eiders are among the many animals that appear to be struggling with rapidly warming northern marine environments, from the Baltic to the Canadian Arctic Ocean to the eastern seaboard, stretching from Newfoundland to Maine. But without researchers in the field, it’s difficult to predict how or whether these species may adapt.

The situation was far different when eiders began their mysterious worldwide decline in the 1990s. Then, researchers working in Mitivik, in the East Bay Migratory Bird Sanctuary, which would later be named the Qaqsauqtuuq Migratory Bird Sanctuary, implanted satellite transmitters into the abdomens of eiders and learned where they were overwintering. The findings led to tighter regulations on eider hunting in Canada and Greenland that dramatically boosted eider numbers.

That is, until they crashed again due to disease and polar bear predation. In 2005, Grant Gilchrist, the founder of the research station, and his colleagues identified an outbreak of avian cholera — which is rare in the Arctic and likely linked to global warming — that would kill as many as 3,000 nesting females in 2006 and about half the population over the eight seasons it spread through the nesting colony.

U.S. soldiers conduct cold-weather training near Fairbanks, Alaska, in February, after President Trump threatened to invade Greenland. Victor J. Blue / Bloomberg via Getty Images

Gilchrist was also on hand to witness how receding sea ice, brought on by climate change, forced polar bears onto land just when the eiders were sitting on their eggs. “The bears were like grazing cows leisurely moving through a pasture, stopping to feed [on eggs] whenever they were hungry,” he said. And herring gulls, which can’t force an eider off its nest, learned to eat the eggs that the bears had missed while the eiders were away.

Canadian Wildlife Service scientist Jennifer Provencher and her colleagues discovered that even though the eiders nesting in Mitivik hosted larger-than-expected communities of gastrointestinal worms and flukes, which benefit from warming temperatures, the parasites reduced the ducks’ exposure to contaminants such as mercury and lead. The parasites appear to absorb and clear contaminants from eider tissues. This is significant because mercury and lead, which are leaching from melting permafrost in many parts of the Arctic, can result in reproductive failure in ducks, and they can be harmful to humans who rely on ducks for food. The Inuit have higher levels of mercury than the general Canadian population.

Other researchers found that eiders, like some other animals exposed to deadly disease, eventually developed an immunity to avian cholera.

“Had we not been there, we would never have been able to document any of this,” said Gilchrist, who says he has little doubt that most of what Mitivik researchers discovered is happening in other parts of the Arctic.

In the past, Indigenous hunters have reported dramatic changes in the Canadian Arctic, such as sharp declines in ivory gull numbers; orcas moving into the region and killing narwhal and bowhead whales; and grizzly bears migrating north, mating with polar bears, and producing hybrids. But it is almost impossible to say what is going on in the vast areas that are too far afield for Inuit hunters without putting scientists in the field. In Arctic Canada, there are approximately 150,000 people living in an area that is about half the size of the Lower 48 states. Only a fraction of these people lives above the Arctic Circle.

Environment and Climate Change Canada did not respond to questions about funding cuts and the cessation of research in Mitivik. In a statement, the department said it is “committed to the long-term conservation of biological biodiversity and abundance,” noting that experts are working on new research programs with territorial governments and Indigenous organizations, and with new technologies to help track and understand birds and other animals, including polar bears.

Still, Andrew Derocher, a University of Alberta scientist who has conducted research over the past 40 years on polar bears in Norway, Canada, and Alaska, notes that support for polar bear research in Canada is at an all-time low. Determining how polar bears are faring in Canada — which is home to 13 of the world’s 19 polar bear populations — is becoming increasingly difficult, he says, if not impossible, because of a lack of government funding.

Eiders found dead after an avian cholera outbreak on Mitivik Island in 2006. Grant Gilchrist

“We’ve pretty much stopped any research beyond the long-term monitoring in Western Hudson Bay and a project in James Bay” funded by a private foundation, he says. “There is almost no other research on polar bears in Canada now.”

Ornithologist Mark Mallory spent 12 years conducting research at migratory bird nesting sites across the Canadian Arctic while working for the Canadian Wildlife Service, but he left that job for a research chair at Acadia University in part because of the high cost of conducting research in the polar regions and the endless struggle to secure consistent funding. Mallory is still very much interested in what is happening in the Arctic, but his years in the field and as co-editor-in-chief of the journal Arctic Science have given him a broader perspective on the challenges scientists face when trying to figure out how a warming climate is affecting wildlife.

“No credible scientist would deny that not enough is being invested in long-term monitoring,” he says. “But the bigger problem is not enough is being done to act on what we know is happening… Do we really think 10 or 20 more years of data showing ranges shrinking north, or clutch sizes reducing, or skinnier species is going to change anything in the minds of politicians or the public?”

Gilchrist appreciates Mallory’s frustrations with governments that refuse to act on climate change, but he — like many Arctic researchers — still believes that long-term monitoring with Inuit partners is essential. “Somebody should still be out in nature monitoring wildlife and the natural environment, and quantifying changes that are occurring or not occurring,” he said.

He doesn’t know whether the eiders of Mitivik Island will eventually return or whether they have found safer areas to nest. Because of the high costs of operating at Qaqsauqtuuq and the increased odds of running into polar bears, Gilchrist is now focusing his research at a site on Baffin Island, where it is cheaper to work and where there are fewer bears.

#4110. Spooky Lava Lamps – Halloween Science

📅 15 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Are you ready to brew up some bubbly science this Halloween? Our spooky lava lamps are a fun alternative to a traditional pumpkin and a fantastic way for kids to learn about density and chemical reactions! Using just water, oil, food colouring, and an Alka-Seltzer (or effervescent vitamin tablet), you can create a science activity kids will remember for a long time!

I also have a pumpkin lava lamp that’s perfect for a Halloween evening if you place a torch behind it!

Fill the bottle or jar a quarter full with water.

Top up to the (near) top with the vegetable oil.

The oil and water should separate into two layers, water at the bottom and oil sitting on top. Add about 6-8 drops of food colouring once they have separated.

Pop in half an Alka-Seltzer tablet and watch the bubbles form.

Add more Alka-Seltzer bit by bit to keep the bubbles rising and falling.

Another idea is to use small plastic cups made to look like pumpkins or ghosts for a mini version.

Water and oil don’t mix because water is made of polar molecules. A water molecule has a positive electrical charge on one end and a negative charge on the other. Opposite charges attract, so water molecules stick together because the positive end of one water molecule is attracted to the negative end of another.

Oil molecules are different; they are non-polar, meaning their charge is more evenly spaced out. This means oil is not attracted to water. Oil is hydrophobic (water-fearing) and tries to stay as far away from water as it can.

Oil sits on top of the water because it is less dense than water.

When you add Alka-Seltzer (made of citric acid and sodium bicarbonate), it reacts with water to form carbon dioxide gas and sodium citrate. As the carbon dioxide bubbles rise, they carry bits of coloured water with them. When the gas escapes at the surface, the coloured water drops sink back down, creating a lava lamp effect.

#4147. COP17: Tracking progress towards the UN’s 2030 nature targets

📅 15 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Countries will meet in Armenia this month to carry out the first global review of progress towards meeting key nature targets by 2030.

These targets – and the wider ambition to halt and reverse biodiversity loss around the world – were agreed in the landmark Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework in 2022.

But the ambitions will not be achieved in time for the 2030 deadline unless action “accelerates rapidly”, a key UN report found.

It noted that nations are falling short on 22 of 23 nature targets, which focus on restoring ecosystems, increasing nature finance and reducing harmful subsidies.

This report and other findings will inform the global progress review taking place at the COP17 biodiversity summit in Armenia over 19-30 October.

#4117. How to Extinguish a Candle with Carbon Dioxide

📅 14 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Can you extinguish a flame without touching it? This fun classic science activity is always a crowd pleaser. Carbon dioxide is heavier than air, so when it’s released near a candle, it replaces the air (which contains the oxygen needed for fire to burn) and puts out the flames!

WARNING – This activity involves fire and should be supervised or performed by adults

There are two easy ways to create enough CO2 to put out a small flame. You can create it with a baking soda and vinegar reaction or collect CO2 in a balloon placed on top of a bottle of fizzy water.

Simply place a balloon quickly on a bottle of fizzy water when it’s first opened. If you swirl and gently shake the bottle, the CO2 will collect in the balloon and inflate it.

An unopened bottle of fizzy water ( club soda or sparkling water )

Place the tea light on a heatproof surface and light it.

Remove the lid from the carbonated water bottle and quickly place the balloon over the opening.

Gently swirl and shake the bottle to release the trapped gas. You’ll see the bubbles rising, and the balloon will start to inflate.

Once the balloon stops inflating, carefully remove it from the bottle, pinching the end so no gas escapes.

Hold the balloon over the tea light and slowly release the carbon dioxide. The tea light should go out.

Carbon dioxide is released when vinegar and bicarbonate of soda react.

Place the tea light on a heatproof surface and light it.

Pour the vinegar into the jug and then add the bicarbonate of soda. You should see bubbles of gas (carbon dioxide) straight away.

Carefully tilt the jug over the tea light so the CO2 is near the flame. It should go out!

CO2 fire extinguishers are used for electrical fires. Fire needs oxygen to burn, but when carbon dioxide is sprayed over the fire, it covers the flames in a blanket of carbon dioxide, which starves the fire of oxygen and puts it out.

One advantage of using CO2 is that it doesn’t damage expensive equipment like water or foam.

When CO2 gas shoots out of a fire extinguisher, it’s so cold that it can turn into ice crystals.

#4085. How to tell if a startup has a moat

📅 13 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

👋 Hey there, I’m Lenny. Each week, I share deeply researched product, growth, and career advice. For more: Lenny’s Jobs | Lenny’s Podcast | Lennybot | How I AI | Become an AI-Native Builder and my other favorite AI/PM courses.

Moats! Does anyone have one anymore? As AI makes it easy to build anything, this is one of the biggest questions on people’s minds right now. Especially people trying to decide where to work. I teamed up with my frequent collaborator, the incredibly smart and insightful Dan Hockenmaier, to answer this question: How do I know if a given startup has a moat? The answer is a lot more interesting, and straightforward, than you’d expect. Let’s get into it.

There is a pervasive belief that startups are a lottery ticket. It’s impossible to pick winners, so it’s just a roll of the dice.

Not only can startups be a great learning experience and a lot of fun, but they can also provide economic rewards comparable to (or greater than) the “safe” route into big tech.

However, for this to be true, you must rigorously evaluate the startup you’re considering joining. Some of this is obvious: Does it have a strong team? Do customers love the product? Is it growing fast? But perhaps because it seems harder to measure, people often skip one of the most important criteria: Does it have a moat?

A company can check all your boxes and look like a rocket ship. If it doesn’t have a moat, though, it’s going to stall out. It won’t be able to keep growing once competition gets fierce, and it’s unlikely to reach a major liquidity event. That can mean wasting years of your life.

Moats are more important than ever in the AI era. Anyone can create a product; they can do it faster than ever before, and everyone is building the same things. But only some of these products will win.

Of course, assessing a startup’s moat isn’t an exact science. The good news is, there’s a simple framework that could meaningfully increase your odds of picking a winner or avoiding a flame-out, and you can apply it in just an hour after receiving an offer. The process will also teach you a lot about the business.

The first step is to figure out what kind of moat a startup could have. This can seem daunting, especially given how frenetic the conversation is on the topic right now. The term “moat” is being used much too loosely, as a stand-in for basically anything that gives a company an edge.

In reality, only a small subset of what people call moats actually drive the vast majority of long-term value.

They’re what I’ll call compounding advantages: not just a temporary edge, but a self-reinforcing flywheel that gets stronger and stronger over time, ultimately making a company very hard to compete with.

There are only four compounding advantages that really matter. Every major tech company has at least one. If you can’t see the potential for any in the startup you’re evaluating, that’s a red flag.

1. Switching costs: The more deeply embedded a product is in the way a customer works, the harder it is for that customer to switch. This increases retention and revenue per customer, which can be reinvested into the product.

2. Network effects: The more users a product has, the better it gets for everyone. This increases engagement and attracts more users.

3. Scale economies: The more volume a company has, the larger and more efficient its operations can be. This lowers unit costs, funding lower prices or a better product at the same price, further driving up volume.

4. Data effects: The more usage a product has, the more data it generates. This data can be used to improve the product and increase usage.

What about all of the other moats people talk about? Can they be compounding advantages as well?

A common one is speed of execution. It’s true that speed is critical for startups. But a company that has to sprint forever is not a good business. Ultimately, it will misstep, slow down, and competition will catch up. Speed gives a company more shots on goal as it tries to build a great product and a great business, but it must eventually find something more durable.

Another favorite is distribution. It does matter more than ever: building products has gotten easier, but getting them to customers has not. But distribution is not a compounding advantage either. It helps create the raw ingredients for one, but it is not one itself.

Finally, we have brand. The idea is that when everyone is building the same thing, the most resonant brand will win. But for startups, brand works more like customer acquisition spend: Put money in, get growth out. It’s not something that will effectively keep others from competing long-term. Brand can become a true compounding advantage (as with Coke or Nike or Apple), but it just takes too long to really matter for most startups.

Speed, distribution, brand, a great founding team, and lots of funding—these are all fuel. They get the car moving in the right direction. The four compounding advantages are what it looks like when the car starts rolling downhill on its own.

Your job is to identify which kind of compounding advantage a startup has the potential to build. It won’t be in full force yet (it takes a long time to develop), but it should be pretty clear which path they are on. That’s because it’s not something you can “bolt on”; it’s not some clever competitive tactic or a tweak you can make to your business model.

A compounding advantage is the core loop at the center of the business through which you add more value for customers. Defensibility emerges as a byproduct of turning the flywheel over and over.

So as you evaluate a company, don’t focus on what they say they are or what their “strategy” is. Focus on what they’re actually doing. What is the team working on every day? What’s on their roadmap? Which teams are large and influential inside the company?

Switching costs are sometimes viewed negatively: as something that keeps customers trapped or a reason clunky legacy software never gets replaced.

But think about what it takes for a product to get into this position in the first place. It first has to become so deeply embedded into the way a customer works, so essential to their business, that they can’t remove it. That requires creating a product that delivers enormous value.

Mike Vernal recently wrote something of a manifesto for anyone building this kind of business today:

If you are building a venture-backed software company, I think the answer is once again “do it all.” Just build the whole thing—your moat is the totality of what you’ve built and completely owning the buying center.

Some have argued that AI is making switching costs irrelevant. AI does make the actual migration faster and cheaper, but that is not where most switching costs actually come from. The bigger cost is what I’ll call the “workflow tax”: all of the time the team spends learning a product and building it into how they work, and how disruptive it is to do that over again. AI also helps teams build products much faster, which could allow them to become even more embedded across a wider range of surfaces.

At the core of these companies is a machine to both deepen and broaden customer engagement. The roadmap is rich with efforts to better serve existing use cases or create ways to serve new ones. They’ll often be focused on integrations and working with other parts of their customers’ stack. The team should be heavily tilted toward product and engineering, with an emphasis on shipping rapidly.

The classic examples are large incumbents with broad, deeply embedded products in SaaS, fintech, and healthcare, like Salesforce, Datadog, ADP, and Epic. But there are many earlier-stage companies that are currently on a path to creating something similar.

Rippling is a great example. Parker Conrad is building what he calls a “compound startup.” They started with employee onboarding and expanded rapidly across HR services, talent, finance, payroll, and business intelligence, all operating on shared infrastructure. This is the kind of product that gets embedded so deeply it rarely gets ripped out.

Network-effect businesses are totally different—so different, in fact, that most product features and the UI itself matter very little. How is it that Craigslist can still get so much traffic when it looks like this, in 2026?

Because what matters most is the network. The value of a network-effect business increases with each additional user. It’s all about having all of the people customers want to interact with, all of the content they want to consume, or all of the products they want to buy.

Network-effect products are common in industries like social (LinkedIn), gaming (Roblox), and marketplaces (Amazon).

The emphasis of a network business is less on building out the surface area or feature set of the product, and more on growing the network and making it easier and easier for that network to interact.

For example, during my years at Faire (a marketplace connecting local retailers and independent brands), the vast majority of our roadmap was focused on just a few things:

Getting more brands and making sure we had all of their products

Improving the search and discovery algorithm to recommend the right products

Reducing risk so retailers could try more products (free shipping, net terms, bigger credit limits)

Every decision gets filtered through one lens: “Does this strengthen the network?”

Businesses with scale as their compounding advantage are all about making it possible to offer the same product at a lower cost or a higher-quality product at the same cost.

These businesses are often highly capital-intensive: They make a big investment, such as in a production facility, warehouse, or LLM training run, and figure out how to get it to pay back. And then they do it again, but this time try to do it bigger and faster.

They’ll often have larger operations, engineering, and finance teams. And they will usually be focused on expansion: into newer, higher-scale operations and into new geographies and categories. Scale businesses are commonly found in industries like manufacturing (SpaceX), retail (Costco), media (Netflix), and now in the frontier labs.

Zipline (the autonomous delivery robot company) is a good example of a startup where scale economies are starting to emerge. They benefit from scale in two ways: by manufacturing more drones to reduce unit costs and by increasing delivery density to improve their utilization rate. The result is a cost per delivery that has fallen from $300 at launch to $12 today. Anyone who wants to compete with them will first have to go through the expensive part of that curve.

The final type of compounding advantage has historically been relatively rare, but AI is making it much more common.

These are businesses with data at the core. It’s the input that makes the product higher-quality, more efficient, or work for more use cases. And by using the product, customers generate even more data, which further compounds this effect.

In these companies, you’ll often see a large effort to collect and sanitize data, and to build algorithms and products on top of it. ML and data science are usually large and influential teams.

One good example is Stripe Radar. They use transaction data across all merchants to get better at detecting fraud for each of them. Stripe has processed over 70 trillion data points and $1.9 trillion in transaction volume. 92% of all transactions use a card it has seen before. The result is a product that a company with access to less data can’t match.

Many AI products have the potential for this compounding advantage because of how much they can be improved with more data. Cursor is a clear example: with each accept or reject from users, the model gets better at understanding what good code looks like. Many vertical AI products have the potential to do the same thing for other domains like law, healthcare, and customer service.

📅 13 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Pluralistic: Daily links from Cory Doctorow

Every morning, I revisit my blog headlines from this date, going back 25 years, 20 years, 15 years, 10 years, 5 years, and one year. I publish the most interesting ones in a section of my newsletter that I call "object permanence," and I think it's the most useful part of my day.

"Object permanence," of course, is a reference to the stage in an infant's development when they acquire the ability to connect events in the past to events in the present, which is why it's the point at which babies cease to be amazed by peek-a-boo. I've been blogging for more than a quarter century, and these daily trawls through the things I considered important enough to share are an unexpectedly powerful way to connect the events of the past to the events of the present.

Take cybersecurity, a subject that is filling today's headlines, primarily due to a spate of poorly understood (and criminally poorly explained) incidents in which AI companies' hacking tools broke into various servers on the internet, sometimes trashing them:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/09/12/god-in-the-box/#llms-are-fake

In my discussions of this subject, I've been at pains to tease apart two different aspects of these incidents: first, that it's wrong to speak of bots "going rogue" here (they're doing what they're designed to do); but second, that the alarming thing about these incidents is that they illustrate how deeply unserious our approach to information security has been, and remains.

This is important context, and context is what gives us the object permanence we need to both understand and address real problems, like the two related facts that a) our IT systems are poorly constructed and indifferently maintained; and b) the people who are supposed to be fixing this keep making it worse.

The worst culprits here are governments, though the private sector is also terrible (in truth, our cybersecurity nightmare is something of a private-public partnership from hell). Governments are deeply compromised when it comes to digital security because they have an irreconcilable conflict of interest: on the one hand, they want to defend their countries' administrative systems, firms and households from digital attacks. But on the other hand, they really want to be able to launch attacks on their adversaries, both foreign and domestic.

Worse: because governments don't know who those adversaries might be (or might become at some later date), the state security apparatuses of the world's governments keep insisting that all working computer security must be dismantled, so they can spy on all of us in order to figure out whom among us is a Bad Guy, and then "travel back in time" through each newly discovered "bad guy's" communications to identify their confederates and detail their nefarious plots.

This is an old, old, old fight. It was arguably the first policy fight of the digital era: the fight over whether we could have working encryption at all. Old hands call this era "the crypto wars" (from when "crypto" meant "cryptography"), when the NSA classed working encryption code as a "munition" and criminalized civilian access to it. That was a very hard-fought fight, and it was won decisively by the Electronic Frontier Foundation and our just-retired executive director Cindy Cohn, who, as a young lawyer, litigated the "Bernstein case" that established that code was a form of expressive speech protected by the First Amendment. If you're interested in learning more about this, you should read Cindy's newly published memoir, Privacy's Defender (all profits go to EFF):

https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262051248/privacys-defender/

Before Cindy came on the scene, the crypto wars were being fought primarily by the "cypherpunks," a motley gang of computer security enthusiasts of many political stripes who were unified by their conviction that the most important aspect of security was defensive. The fact that the NSA wanted to be able to read the private messages and files of their adversaries (the "Four Horsemen of the Infocalypse": terrorists, drug traffickers, child pornographers and organized crime) must not be allowed to trump the fact that any encryption that the NSA could pierce could also be pierced by bad guys: corporate spies, state-sponsored spies, griefers, petty criminals, stalkers, identity thieves and other creeps.

This argument was so indisputable, it's a tautology: "You cannot make a computer user more secure by making their computers less secure." We don't know how to make a computer that only "good guys" can break into, but which "bad guys" bounce off of. Either we make computers more secure, or we don't.

In the decades since the crypto wars, governments have embarked upon an uninterrupted program to weaken the security of our computers, with catastrophic effect. The most spectacular example is unquestionably the spy agencies' doctrine called "NOBUS," which stands for "no one but us"; as in "No one but us is smart enough to find this bug, which means that we can withhold its discovery from the manufacturer, which will allow us to weaponize this bug and use it to attack our enemies. Because no one but us is smart enough to find this bug, none of those enemies will ever discover it, weaponize it, and use it to attack the people who pay our salaries, who expect us to keep them safe."

This turns out to be both catastrophically wrong and catastrophically dangerous. A series of leaks of computer vulnerabilities hoarded by both the NSA and CIA under NOBUS confirmed that these bugs were independently rediscovered by criminals and state actors at the rate of about 20% per year. Worse, though, is that these bugs don't need to be independently rediscovered, because the NSA and CIA both leaked them.

The highest-profile leak was an NSA-hoarded vulnerability in Windows they codenamed "Eternal Blue." Eternal Blue leaked in 2017, whereupon cybercriminals hybridized it with some otherwise undistinguished ransomware to create Wannacry, a devastating piece of malware that took down hospitals, banks and oil pipelines, as well as the entire city government of Baltimore and the British Library:

https://cdn.sanity.io/files/v5dwkion/production/99206a2d1e9f07b35712b78f7d75fbb09560c08d.pdf

But NOBUS isn't the only example of spy agencies making our computers less secure in the name of making us more secure. Perhaps the most egregious example of that are the Bundestrojaners, malicious software purchased by the German government, whose customs agents secretly installed it on the computers of unsuspecting people during border inspections. This "lawful interception" software was hugely invasive: it could grab your screen, plunder your hard drive, and install and execute software on your computer, all with the utmost stealth, intended to keep you from knowing it was installed and operating on your computer. But it wasn't merely invasive – it was insecure, so anyone (rival spies, criminals, stalkers) could scan the internet for infected computers and take them over:

https://www.ccc.de/en/updates/2011/staatstrojaner

You really couldn't ask for a more perfect example of the maxim that you can't make computer users more secure by making their computers less secure. Despite this, governments continue to demand the introduction of grave, universal secure vulnerabilities into our digital lives. Take "chat control," a proposal to force every device manufacturer to install software that continuously scans your devices for child sex abuse material (AKA "child porn"):

https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/04/eu-parliament-blocks-mass-scanning-our-chats-whats-next

There are plenty of things wrong with this, starting with the fact that it requires your device to secretly grab and transmit sensitive images from your phone, which might include sensitive images of your kids that were requested by their doctors. This isn't a hypothetical: it's something that happened to a dad who subsequently lost his email, his login to every other account, his phone number, all of his files (including his tax records and his business data), and every photo he had ever taken of his family, without appeal, after Google automatically detected a photo he took of his son to send to the family pediatrician. Google also falsely accused this man of sexually abusing his child and sicced the SFPD on him:

https://pluralistic.net/2022/08/22/allopathic-risk/#snitches-get-stitches

But even if you're not worried about this, you should still have the object permanence to recall the lessons of NOBUS and the Bundestrojaner. We can't make computer users more secure by making their computers less secure, and it's hard to imagine something more insecure than a computer that you entrust with every sensitive detail of your life that is designed to run computer code in secret that you can neither inspect nor terminate (let alone delete).

Hardly a day goes by without some country demanding that your computer's security be weakened in the name of protecting you. For years, both Conservative and Labour UK governments have been steaming towards a total ban on working encryption in the name of catching bad guys:

https://pluralistic.net/2023/03/05/theyre-still-trying-to-ban-cryptography/

It's happening in Canada, too, where somehow "digital sovereignty" means "making every device used in Canada vulnerable to foreign spies so that our own spies can access our private communications":

https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/05/canadas-bill-c-22-repackaged-version-last-years-surveillance-nightmare

Then there's the "age verification" laws proliferating all around the world. Remember, there's no such thing as "age verification," there's only "spying on everyone, all the time, to figure out who the kids are":

https://pluralistic.net/2026/06/23/destroy-the-village/#to-save-it

Age verification requires multiple entities to assemble vast corpuses of the kinds of sensitive identification papers that would allow identity thieves to destroy the finances and lives of everyone in the country, and (as foretold) every time it's tried, these files leak. Someday, this will lead to doxing whole countries:

https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/02/discord-faces-backlash-over-age-checks-after-data-breach-exposed-70000-ids/

Age verification is the latest and worst stage in governments' fraught relationship with private sector data-collection. The USA is the worst offender here: it is the largest, richest country in the world that has essentially no privacy protections. The last time the US updated its federal privacy law was in 1988, when the Video Privacy Protection Act banned the disclosure of your VHS rentals. Tech companies spy on you for the same reason your dog licks its balls – because they can, and no one stops them:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/03/10/ice-tech/#foreseeable-outcomes

Which raises an obvious question: why hasn't Congress updated privacy law? And why hasn't the EU figured out how to force the US companies that pretend to be Irish to obey European privacy law? The answer is that governments love private sector data-collection, because it gives them a cheap, off-the-books source of warrantless access to information they aren't legally allowed to acquire:

https://pluralistic.net/2021/04/13/public-interest-pharma/#axciom

From Flock cameras to ICE's use of Google data in its ethnic cleansing campaign, the US government and the commercial surveillance industry have created a public-private partnership from hell, where governments refuse to block commercial spying and companies hand their commercial surveillance data over to governments in return.

From there, it was a short leap to governments insisting that the private sector gather our data. That's the right way to think about age verification, chat control, and other mandates for corporations to spy on us using our devices and our accounts: a government requirement for private-sector mass surveillance.

I have plenty of hostility to corporate overreach, but to have any hope of fighting that overreach, we need governments to be on our side. But for our governments, when it comes to cybersecurity, we are the enemy, and spying corporations are our governments' allies.

Sometimes, that's utterly, literally true. Governments around the world are addicted to buying spyware from cyber-arms dealers, mostly headquartered in Israel and the UAE (with the odd Italian and British company for variety). These arms-dealers practice NOBUS-by-proxy, wherein they discover or pay bounties for bugs in widely used hardware, operating systems and applications and – rather than reporting them to the manufacturer – hoard them and weaponize them, and then sell the weapons to criminals, corporations, and our governments, who give them billions of our money to ensure that our computers have huge, glaring defects in them that leave us terribly vulnerable:

https://citizenlab.ca/focus-area/targeted-surveillance/

In a world where our governments' national security apparatuses were genuinely committed to our safety and security, they would criminally prosecute these companies, not make their owners into billionaires.

This is the context we should all be thinking about when it comes to these "rogue AI hacking agents." The IT systems that these irresponsibly deployed software products have trashed were already terrifyingly vulnerable…by design. Rather than fix these systems, our governmental "security" agencies have spent the past 40 years making them less secure, in order to make it easier for them to catch "bad guys," who now find it trivial to attack us through systems we are all totally reliant upon.

In every way, in every area of law, governments fail to take our security seriously. That even includes copyright law. All over the world, the US Trade Representative has ensured that every country has an "anti-circumvention" law that makes it a crime to reverse-engineer and modify software without permission:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/01/01/39c3/#the-new-coalition

From the very start, these have been a security nightmare. Remember the "Sony Rootkit?" Between 2005-2007, Sony-BMG Music distributed over 6m CDs that were deliberately infected with a piece of malicious software called a "rootkit" that was used to disable your computer's ability to rip (copy) music CDs.

The way this rootkit did so was depraved in its indifference to the security of Sony-BMG's customers: at the same time as Sony-BMG's malware installed its secret anti-ripping program, it also altered their computers' operating systems to make them incapable of detecting any program or file whose name started with $SYS$. Immediately, malicious software authors started adding $SYS$ to the start of all their filenames, and that malicious software was therefore undetectable to millions of users' computers. It was even invisible to anti-virus software. Worse: Sony-BMG's rootkit infected 200,000-300,000 US government and military networks:

https://www.schneier.com/blog/archives/2005/11/sonys_drm_rootk.html

Even worse: after the scandal broke, security researchers all over the world came forward to say that they'd discovered the Sony Rootkit months before it became common knowledge, but their lawyers had told them they weren't allowed to disclose its existence because doing so would fall afoul of anti-circumvention law. For months, this official, deliberate malware epidemic spread in secret because of anti-circumvention law.

It's been more than 20 years since the Sony Rootkit, and anti-circumvention law is now worse than it was in the oughts: now it applies to far more devices from far more vendors. Today, it's common for corporations to threaten their own customers with civil and criminal liability if they have the temerity to hire outside auditors to evaluate the software they entrust their businesses to for security defects. And oh God, it's even worse, because the worst offender is Oracle, who are now the de facto IT czars of America:

https://arstechnica.com/information-technology/2015/08/oracle-security-chief-to-customers-stop-checking-our-code-for-vulnerabilities/

I'm not saying that these are the only problems with our IT security. What I am saying is that the hacking bots everyone's freaking out about didn't appear out of nowhere. If you have just a little object permanence, you can see that they are the terminal stage of a pathology that governments have only ever worsened: by hoarding and weaponizing vulnerabilities; by paying others to hoard and weaponize vulnerabilities; by criminalizing security audits; by insisting that every computer we rely on be equipped with deliberate vulnerabilities in software we can't inspect, terminate or delete.

Yeah, there should be criminal and civil liability for OpenAI, Amazon, Anthropic and every other company whose bots "went rogue" and attacked servers. But that will do nothing to fix our IT.

The table-stakes for a secure digital world are: public agencies that try to make our computers as secure as possible, including by administering the corporate death-penalty to any company that hoards and weaponizes information about bugs in our computer. We should protect and reward individuals and firms who come forward with true disclosures about bugs in systems we rely upon. We should not give companies the right to silence and punish those critics who want to warn us about their defective products. And we should not require that companies make us less secure in the name of making it easier for spies and cops to catch the bad guys.

Alex Cornell is a design director at Meta, where he leads design for Muse. In this episode, he explains how progressive disclosure makes Muse approachable for casual users without limiting technical ones, why its main chat is designed as a single persistent relationship, and how generative UI can feel personal without constantly changing the interface. He also shares how he uses Muse to turn years of Instagram saves into a shopping catalog, analyze his golf and chess games, and transform a 20-year-old blog into a daily podcast.

AI agents should be designed for conversations with people, not interactions with software. Alex’s guiding question for Muse was “How would this work if you were talking to another human?” That principle shaped everything from removing traditional message bubbles to placing the agent at the top of the screen instead of inside a standard chat window.

Progressive disclosure is one of the most useful principles in AI product design. Muse represents the same activity at three levels: a simple emoji for casual users, a task list for anyone who wants more detail, and raw logs for engineers. Each person gets the transparency they need without adding clutter for everyone else.

Visible chain-of-thought is often a workaround for latency, not a valuable feature. Many AI products display reasoning steps to reassure users while a slow model works. Muse is fast enough to operate quietly, showing that agents do not need to perform their thinking when they can simply deliver the result.

Generative UI works best when the structure stays fixed and the content changes. Muse’s avatar, feed items, and idea cards are deliberately designed surfaces. The dynamic elements are the agent’s status emoji, personalized suggestions, and small animations, allowing the experience to feel alive without rebuilding the interface every session.

Instagram saves may be one of the most overlooked data sources for consumer AI. Alex connected years of saved posts to Muse, asked it to organize them, and turned the results into shopping recommendations styled like a SkyMall catalog. It gave forgotten personal data a practical new use without requiring technical setup.

Designing non-deterministic systems requires a different relationship with craft. Alex has spent his career controlling exactly how interfaces appeared, but Muse looks different for every person and cannot guarantee pixel-perfect outputs. Learning to judge the results by usefulness rather than visual precision has been one of the most liberating shifts in his career.

Muse’s single persistent chat is a meaningful product bet. Instead of dividing every interaction into separate threads, it treats the main conversation as an ongoing relationship with the agent. Side chats handle scoped tasks, but the central thread preserves continuous context in a way Alex believes the industry has not fully explored.

Polite prompting may be an effective strategy, not merely good manners. Alex responds to frustrating results with calm, specific feedback rather than anger. He’s also seen agents change their responses when users express frustration more sharply. His conclusion is that clear, respectful direction may produce better results.

#4158. Interactive: Tracking negotiating texts at the COP17 biodiversity summit

📅 12 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Next week, diplomats from around the world will descend on Yerevan, Armenia, to negotiate a series of decisions under the UN Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD).

Between 19 and 30 October, 196 parties will gather to hammer out the precise wording of the dozens of legal texts that make up the decisions under the Conference of the Parties (COP) to the CBD.

This year’s summit is particularly significant, as it marks the halfway point between the agreement of the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework and the deadline for achieving its targets.

The CBD has found that countries are currently not on track to meet 22 out of the framework’s 23 targets.

During the fortnight, delegates will also be negotiating decisions relating to the two supplemental protocols to the CBD: the Cartagena Protocol, which covers biosafety; and the Nagoya Protocol, which covers access and benefit-sharing for the use of genetic resources.

(The Cartagena and Nagoya Protocols, which are each governed by a “meeting of the parties”, or “MOP”, have fewer parties than the CBD as a whole, with 173 and 141, respectively.)

Carbon Brief is tracking the progress of countries towards consensus on dozens of negotiating texts using the interactive table below.

The tracker includes texts from the COP, as well as the MOPs for the Cartagena and Nagoya protocols. It also includes draft decisions from the most recent subsidiary body meetings, as these form the starting point for the negotiations in Yerevan.

The CBD has three subsidiary bodies: the subsidiary body on scientific, technical and technological advice (SBSTTA), the subsidiary body on implementation (SBI) and the subsidiary body on article 8(j) and other provisions (SB8J).

SB8J focuses on issues related to the Indigenous peoples and local communities. It was established at COP16 in Cali, Colombia, in 2024.

The first two columns of the interactive tracker list the topic of each agenda item and the relevant body, with subsequent columns including dates, page counts and links to the original documents.

The table is searchable and can be sorted using the arrows in the header.

Areas of disagreement in UN draft texts are denoted by square brackets. Counting the brackets in a particular iteration of a text therefore gives a sense of how far parties are from reaching consensus.

The bracket column is colour-coded, with dark green indicating a low number of brackets and red indicating a high number of brackets remaining.

Texts can also present parties with choices using “options” or alternatives (“alt”), which are counted together in the sixth column of the table.

The key discussions in Yerevan will centre around two agenda items in particular. The first is the global review, which will assess progress towards the GBF and make recommendations for accelerating action.

The second is finance, which can be separated into two parts: resource mobilisation and the financial mechanism. On resource mobilisation, biodiverse global south countries are likely to push for an increased amount of international finance for conservation.

Disagreement over the creation of the financial mechanism led to the suspension of COP16 in Cali in October 2024, as parties failed to find agreement on the mobilisation of finance before the end of the summit.

Global south countries want the establishment of a separate financial mechanism under the control of the CBD; global north countries largely prefer to maintain the existing mechanism, which sits under the Global Environment Facility.

At the resumed session of COP16 in Rome in early 2025, countries agreed on a roadmap that would lead to the establishment of a permanent mechanism.
