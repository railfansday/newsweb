#2078. Doncaster deserves praise for seeking to maximise SPFL's next TV deal ... but can we trust him to negotiate it?

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

By CALUM CROWE, SCOTTISH DEPUTY CHIEF SPORTS WRITER

Published: 18:08 BST, 20 October 2026 | Updated: 18:12 BST, 20 October 2026

When the Scottish Professional Football League (SPFL) renewed their TV deal with Sky Sports last time around, they came under a lot of flak. Quite rightly, too.

The league chose not to open it out to competitive tender. There was no formal bidding process. Instead, just a cosy continuation of the status quo.

It was only natural to wonder whether it was really a good deal at all. Some insiders at the time reckoned the SPFL could have left £20million on the table by failing to invite bids.

Stewart Robertson, the former Rangers managing director who used to serve on the SPFL board, didn't mince his words when he spoke about this a few years ago.

Speaking even before that last deal had been announced, Robertson took aim at SPFL chief executive Neil Doncaster and claimed that Scottish football was being under-sold.

The SPFL have done the right thing this time. They have opened it up and invited broadcasters to bid for the rights to show Scottish Premiership matches from 2029-30 onwards.

All of this is welcome. But it also relies on Doncaster's skills as a negotiator to maximise value not only for the clubs and the league as a whole, but also to ensure fans don't end up being fleeced.

Neil Doncaster has been criticised for not bringing in more TV revenue for Scottish football

Because that's the danger. If more broadcasters start piling in and want a piece of the pie, it will almost inevitably lead to fans having to fork out for more subscriptions.

It is a difficult balancing act. Doncaster has never come across as a strong and robust negotiator at any point whilst in charge of the SPFL.

Fans are already having to pay for multiple subscriptions with Sky Sports and Premier Sports, along with the cost of season tickets. All of this at a time when there is a cost-of-living crisis.

Opening the process to tender was overdue and correct. Maximising the money is essential if the league is to grow and clubs are to prosper.

But if the only model on offer is one that continually loads extra cost on to fans, then the SPFL will have solved one problem by creating a bigger one.

#2077. Man City punishment warning as legal experts question Premier League sanctions

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

By Naim Beneddra | 23 Oct 2026 15:42

Manchester City's financial-rule scandal represents a major test of the Premier League's credibility, according to two sports law specialists who have warned that an insufficient punishment could undermine confidence in English football's regulatory system.

An independent commission found Manchester City guilty of 114 of the 115 charges brought against them, including serious breaches of financial regulations covering nine seasons between 2009 and 2018.

The findings, published in September, detailed how the club used misleading commercial agreements to inflate revenue and concealed the true state of its finances from auditors and football authorities.

According to the commission, the arrangements enabled City to record approximately £830m in commercial income that did not accurately reflect the underlying financial transactions.

The club have consistently denied wrongdoing and lodged an appeal on October 1, arguing that the commission's conclusions contain significant errors of law, principle and fact.

No punishment has yet been determined, with the disciplinary process and appeal proceedings continuing.

However, the potential consequences have prompted questions about how the Premier League can impose a proportionate sanction in a case of unprecedented scale.

Brazilian publication Trivela interviewed sports law specialists Ana Mizutori and Andrei Kampff, both of whom hold master's degrees in the field, to examine the legal challenges facing the competition.

Their assessments suggest that the eventual punishment must reflect the nature and seriousness of the proven breaches, rather than simply follow the precedents established by other clubs.

Mizutori believes the Manchester City case could have implications extending well beyond the Premier League.

"This is a test of governance for European football, of the ability of sporting bodies to ensure that economic power does not override the integrity of competitions," she told Trivela.

Her warning reflects concerns that an inadequate financial penalty could weaken the deterrent effect of regulations intended to maintain competitive fairness.

A fine that can be comfortably absorbed by a wealthy club, the specialists argue, risks creating the impression that breaching financial regulations is an acceptable commercial gamble.

However, determining an appropriate sanction is considerably more complicated than applying the punishments previously imposed on other Premier League clubs.

Everton received a 10-point deduction in November 2023 for breaching the league's Profitability and Sustainability Rules, subsequently reduced to six on appeal. They were later deducted a further two points for a separate breach.

Nottingham Forest also received a points deduction during the 2023-24 campaign, while Chelsea's more recent financial irregularities resulted in a different disciplinary outcome, including a substantial fine and a suspended transfer ban.

Kampff believes those cases offer useful guidance on disciplinary methods but cannot provide a straightforward formula for City's punishment.

The lawyer highlighted the importance of distinguishing between exceeding permitted losses and deliberately concealing financial information over an extended period.

"The comparison requires distinguishing excessive losses, deliberate concealment and the duration of the conduct," Kampff explained to Trivela.

"City have the aggravating factor of non-cooperation during the investigations. The unprecedented nature of the case leaves room for a decision that could surprise, but it needs to be well justified."

The commission found that City failed to cooperate with aspects of the Premier League's investigation, although the club continue to dispute the overall findings.

Mizutori stressed that the club must retain the full right to challenge the evidence and contest disciplinary decisions.

However, she argued that exercising the right to defend itself should be distinguished from failing to meet legitimate regulatory obligations.

"The right of defence must be fully preserved, but it cannot be confused with the right to prevent or obstruct legitimate sporting oversight," she added.

The difficulty facing the Premier League extends beyond deciding whether City should receive a substantial points deduction, a financial penalty or an even more severe sporting sanction.

There is also the question of whether historical achievements should be reconsidered if the commission's findings survive the appeal process.

City won three Premier League titles between 2009 and 2018, alongside three League Cups and an FA Cup.

Removing historical titles could create further complications involving clubs that missed out on European qualification, prize money or commercial opportunities during the relevant seasons.

Mizutori explained that any rival seeking compensation would need to establish the damage suffered, demonstrate a legally sufficient causal link to the proven misconduct and address possible limitation periods.

Consequently, even if City's appeal fails, the outcome would not automatically entitle rival clubs to financial compensation or retrospective honours.

Kampff also believes that points deductions must carry meaningful sporting consequences if they are to deter serious breaches.

"Financial fair play needs to be an instrument that deters prohibited conduct," he told Trivela.

"Points deductions must have a concrete effect, including the eventual possibility of relegation."

His assessment does not amount to a prediction that City will be relegated, but underlines the importance of ensuring that any sanction has a genuine impact.

Both specialists argue that the disciplinary outcome should be independent, transparent and proportionate to the breaches, while fully respecting the club's legal rights.

The distinction is particularly significant because the appeals process could alter or overturn the commission's original findings.

City's appeal will be considered by an independent Appeal Board, with the Premier League confirming that proceedings will remain confidential until the outcome can be published.

For Mizutori, the wider issue is whether football's financial regulations can protect competitive equality rather than merely oversee clubs' accounts.

"Financial sustainability rules do not just protect clubs' accounting balance sheets," she explained. "They also aim to preserve equal competitive conditions, transparency and the integrity of the competition itself."

With no definitive sanction announced, the Premier League faces the challenge of reaching a decision that can withstand legal scrutiny while maintaining confidence in its ability to enforce its own regulations.

#2068. Piers Morgan disagreed with me but Martin Keown was wrong to question my motives in branding Arsenal 'The Unwatchables'... I have a duty to call it as I see it, writes TOM COLLOMOSSE

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Published: 11:37 BST, 20 October 2026 | Updated: 18:35 BST, 20 October 2026

It was around 2pm on Monday that I learned I was a lifelong Tottenham supporter who was desperate to move to London.

This certainly came as a shock to someone who follows another club entirely and has no plans to leave the Midlands. I also lived and worked in London for nearly 20 years.

Apparently, Martin Keown knows otherwise. Responding to my report of Arsenal’s 1-0 win at Nottingham Forest, Keown was quoted on talkSPORT’s X account as saying ‘I’m told he’s a Spurs fan… a journalist in the West Midlands looking to move to London.’

His anger was sparked by this paragraph in the piece. ‘Fans are disillusioned by the ongoing saga and the wider game needs a spectacular team to celebrate. Sadly, where once we had Arsene Wenger’s Invincibles, we now endure Mikel Arteta’s Unwatchables.’

Piers Morgan had his say later – with one crucial difference. Morgan disagreed with the article in every way but at no point did he speculate on my reasons for writing it.

Responding to my report of Arsenal’s 1-0 win at Nottingham Forest, Keown was quoted saying ‘I’m told he’s a Spurs fan… a journalist in the West Midlands looking to move to London'

Do Arsenal have a duty to entertain? No, and I expect them to win the title this year. Yet with their current approach, they are unlikely to be remembered in the same way the Invincibles were, or the way some of Alex Ferguson’s Manchester United teams or the Liverpool sides of the 1980s were.

Keown was a fine player and is a thoughtful pundit. He won 10 trophies in a hugely successful career with Arsenal and watches their progress with a keen eye. He disagreed with my analysis that the Gunners under Arteta are a tough watch – no problem.

When a journalist writes a forthright article, they know that criticism will follow. ‘You don’t know what you’re talking about’ or ‘What a load of rubbish’ – in the age of social media, these are some of the more polite responses.

Yet pundits should not try to guess why a journalist wrote a piece. A fair number of listeners might have heard that I was ‘a Tottenham fan’ and concluded I had written the piece because I was biased against Arsenal.

For the record, there are many Spurs fans in the media and none show bias, yet football is tribal and fans do not like their teams coming under attack.

That does not justify a pundit making statements – in this case incorrect – that could prompt listeners to doubt a reporter’s professionalism or credibility.

As always when we watch matches, we write about what we see. At Forest, I saw an Arsenal side put together at huge expense who registered a single shot on target, against a team who had yet to score at home this season and were without their best player.

In those circumstances, I thought they were disappointing and said so. But it will never be anything to do with the club I support - who, for the record, are not a member of the so-called big six, and nor do they play in London or the Midlands.

Saka has clocked up 13,439 Arsenal minutes since the start of the 2022-23 season, and his exceptional workload, physical playstyle and susceptibility to fouls increase the risk of the winger picking up at least a moderately severe injury.

An extended break ought to do Saka the world of good, but while he continues to pick up a growing number of muscular injuries, long-term concerns over his robustness will only become louder.
