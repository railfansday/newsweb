#79. Denmark to ban mink farming in government U-turn

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Mink farming will end in Denmark by August 2029 after the government decided to drop plans to pursue an exemption to an EU-wide ban on the practice.

Denmark was once the largest producer of mink fur in the world, but the industry has declined due to global bans on fur farming, a decline in the fur trade and an earlier outbreak of a Covid strain in the nation's mink population.

The ban is due to come into effect on 7 August next year, and is followed by a two-year transition period to phase out the practice.

Danish Nature Minister Christian Rabjerg Madsen said the government had chosen animal welfare.

"A business should not continue just because it has existed for a long time. It should also make sense for society, for the economy and for the animals," he said.

The Danish government had initially sought an exemption for its mink industry when the EU added the mammal to its list of invasive species in 2025, prohibiting people from keeping them.

However, the current coalition government, elected in June, reversed this decision and decided not to proceed with the application on Friday.

Social Democrat MP Brigitte Vind said it was "a good day for animal welfare".

"It is absolutely the right decision not to apply for a special scheme for mink breeding in Denmark," she said.

The Danish Mink Association, which represents breeders, has been approached for comment.

Denmark is one of the few European countries to still produce mink fur, one of the more common pelts used in making fur garments.

However, its production has slipped behind that of other countries, such as Poland and China, in recent years.

The industry faced a crisis during the coronavirus pandemic, when the government ordered a mass cull of mink after a mutated strain of Covid-19 was found in the animals. Approximately 15 million were killed.

At the time, the country had huge export markets to China and Hong Kong. The government later admitted it had no legal basis for the culling.

There are now approximately 15,000 mink left in Denmark.

No costs have yet been determined for closing Denmark's seven remaining mink farms, but an animal welfare spokesperson for one of the governing coalition parties previously estimated it would be around 14m Danish krone (Â£1.56m), local media report, but the final amount could be higher.

The wearing of fur is a controversial subject within fashion circles, with publications and fashion brands choosing not to use or promote it several years ago.

But it re-entered the spotlight last year, with celebrities and fashionistas wearing both vintage and so-called faux fur.

#77. Romania blames Russia for ship attack in the Black Sea

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Russian strikes against ships attempting to export Ukrainian grain and cargo through the western Black Sea have sharply increased this month.

Romanian President Nicusor Dan blamed Russia for an attack on a merchant vessel in the Black Sea on Friday morning.

A drone strike sank the merchant ship, with 13 Ukrainian and Azeri crew members on board, near Romanian territorial waters.

The attack killed at least 1 sailor, and three others were still missing, according to Dan.

Nine of the 13 crew members have been rescued.

The drone strike on the ship, called Khoryv, is believed to be the fourth merchant vessel to have been attacked off the coast of Romania this month.

Two weeks ago, two merchant vessels were also set ablaze in the western Black Sea, which is a vital shipping corridor heavily used by Ukraine.

One of those ships was inside Romanian territorial waters. Romania raised concerns about Russian provocation in waters at the edge of or controlled by NATO members.

Bulgaria, also a NATO member like Romania, has raised similar concerns.

Bulgaria has blamed Russia for having edged too close to its exclusive economic zone in the Black Sea after merchant ships were hit by drones near the  country's coastline earlier this month too.

Both Romania and Bulgaria have called on NATO to do more to protect commercial shipping in the Black Sea.

NATO said earlier this week that it was sending more maritime patrol aircraft and radar systems to the Black Sea without having blamed any country for the attacks there.

About 23 sailors had been reported dead or missing in incidents in the Black Sea in the 10 days leading up to October 15, according to Reuters.

Ursula von der Leyen, the president of the European Commission, condemned the increasing attacks on vessels in the Black Sea too, earlier this month calling it "unacceptable" that "the Black Sea has become another front of escalation of the Russian war in Ukraine."

#76. ‘Bad decisions’: Man convicted for taking Peachy the flamingo from Las Vegas hotel

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Mitchell Fairbarn found guilty of animal cruelty after prosecutors say he hurt and frightened the 27-year-old bird

A Canadian man was convicted of animal cruelty charges on Monday for snatching a flamingo named Peachy from its enclosure at an iconic Las Vegas hotel.

Prosecutors said it was a malicious prank while a lawyer for the tourist insisted it was drunken foolishness not meant to harm the bird.

Prosecutors said tourist Mitchell Fairbarn hurt and frightened the 27-year-old Chilean flamingo when he took it from its habitat at the Flamingo Las Vegas Hotel and Casino and carried the squawking bird to his room on 3 March. Defense attorney Josh Tomsheck told jurors at trial in a Nevada court that the man deeply regretted the “bad decisions” he made while intoxicated.

After about five hours of deliberations, jurors returned convictions on felony and misdemeanor animal cruelty counts against the Ontario, Canada, man.

Fairbarn remains under house arrest pending a sentencing hearing set for 1 December. He faces up to four years in prison for the felony and possibly six more months for the misdemeanor conviction. Following the verdict, he declined to comment as he left the courtroom with his wife. He showed no visible emotion.

After the incident, the flamingo was mostly unharmed but is more anxious and nervous when being evaluated, said Peachy’s veterinarian, Tiffany Moore, medical director of Lone Mountain Animal Hospital.

Prosecutors told jurors earlier that Fairbarn willfully caused the bird physical pain, noting feathers were found on the hotel floor. Video showed the bird squawking while Fairbarn handled it and prosecutors said Peachy felt pain when it was pulled by its feet.

Video played at the trial showed Fairbarn holding the flamingo in an elevator and carrying it down a hotel hallway at the famous hotel. The Flamingo is one of the oldest hotels on the Las Vegas Strip, and its well-known flamingo habitat also features hummingbirds, turtles, koi and other species.

Peachy was missing some feathers after being taken.

Las Vegas police responded to the scene after security staff reported that someone had taken the flamingo. When Peachy was located, it was missing seven to eight feathers, had blood on its feathers and injuries to its wings, authorities said.

Taking the bird was “dumb” and “fueled by drunkenness”, the defense attorney Tomsheck said. But he added that his client never intended any harm to the bird, a key element in the felony charge.

“He should not have done that, should not have gone into the habitat, shouldn’t have touched a bird, shouldn’t have taken Peachy inside,” Tomsheck said. “The truth is, Mitch was drunk. Mitch was stupid. He made bad decisions.”

Fairbarn could be seen in court at one point with head lowered, hands covering his eyes.

The man had received death threats after taking the flamingo, and he was unable to leave the US when his father died while Fairbarn was awaiting trial, said Haley Willming, the Clark county prosecutor. While those events were unfortunate, the prosecutor said, “none of that would have happened had he not done what he did, had he not made the choices he made”.

Jurors began deliberating shortly before midday after closing arguments on Monday morning.

Nevada animal rights advocate John Waudby, who recently helped strengthen the state’s animal cruelty laws, said he believes justice was delivered on Peachy’s behalf.

“I hope this sends a message to anybody else that wants to mess with our animals, that you’re not going to do it in Nevada,” he said outside the courtroom after Monday’s verdict.

The war in Ukraine has cost an estimated $2.6 billion of damage to heritage and cultural sites in the country, the UN cultural body UNESCO said in a recently published report.

The report, which covers one year of war in Ukraine between February 24, 2022, and February 24, 2023, was a joint assessment conducted by the Ukrainian government, the World Bank, the European Commission, and the United Nations.

UNESCO was in charge of the part of the report dedicated to culture and tourism.

“As of February 24, 2023, the total damage cost from identified assets is estimated at $2.6 billion,” the report said in its assessment of damage related to culture and tourism.

Some $1.7 billion of that cost was distributed to “historic cities, buildings, and sites imbued with recognized cultural/social values,” $650 million to tourism facilities, $143 million to “movable cultural properties and collections, repositories of culture,” and $150 million to “buildings/workshops/ateliers dedicated to cultural and creative industries,” according to UNESCO.

The most impacted region in Ukraine was Kharkivska, which has suffered 30% of the total damage to its cultural assets, the report added.

UNESCO said that losses are estimated at around $15.2 billion, which includes revenue losses from tourism, art, sports, entertainment, recreation, cultural education, and creative and cultural industries.

“The war has significantly impacted the diversity and richness of culture and cultural heritage in Ukraine, causing damage to cultural infrastructure and assets, reducing livelihoods for cultural creators, bearers and practitioners, limiting access to culture, and impeding the exercise of cultural rights,” the report said.

UNESCO’s head Audrey Azoulay is currently in Ukraine, the organization told CNN on Monday.

#67. Alberta voters overwhelmingly reject plan to leave Canada in referendum

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Result is a victory for prime minister Mark Carney and supporters of Canadian unity

Voters in Canada’s Alberta have overwhelmingly rejected a proposal to begin the process of independence, delivering a decisive victory for supporters of Canadian unity and deepening divisions within the separatist movement.

With nearly 1.9m votes counted, roughly 76% people supported remaining in Canada, while 24% backed beginning a process toward separation, according to unofficial results from Elections Alberta.

The Canadian prime minister, Mark Carney, who grew up in Edmonton, Alberta’s capital, celebrated the result.

“Albertans made a clear choice. They chose unity, to build together, and to take care of each other. Above all, Albertans chose Canada.”

He said the result would allow Canadians to move forward together and accelerate efforts to diversify trade, develop major infrastructure projects and make Canada a global energy powerhouse, with Alberta at its centre.

The results showed an overwhelming majority of Albertans had voted to remain and one radio show host posted: “Someone owes Albertans $130m for this theatrical waste of taxpayers’ money.”

Pollster David Coletto told the Guardian that residents felt the referendum was “a distraction away” from what they wanted their government to focus on.

“There was almost a general sadness about the wasted energy spent on this rather than the more real threats they see, including the Trump administration,” he said. “Most Albertans feel deep grievances with the federal government while also being proudly Canadian. The marriage may be rocky at times, and most Albertans want to see some thing changed, but very few are willing to see the marriage end.”

The province’s separatists, however, saw a silver lining. “We don’t have the final results. I think the numbers are going to fluctuate, but it is fair to say that at least a quarter of Albertans feel that Alberta needs to be treated better,” said Keith Wilson, a leader of the separatist movement.

Others took a different view of the results. “The result is bleak. Independence feels far away and we should not pretend this was not a defeat, however, it changes nothing about the reality of the circumstances Albertans find themselves in,” David Parker, a longtime far-right organiser linked to a massive breach of voter information, wrote on social media.

The Alberta premier, Danielle Smith, welcomed the early results, saying they showed widespread support for remaining in Canada. “Albertans have voted for a stronger Alberta in a united Canada,” Smith posted on social media.

Even though the vote was not explicitly about independence – a point that frustrated separatists – the ballot question nonetheless exposed divisions in Alberta’s politics and raised questions about the durability of national unity.

Delays at roughly 100 of the 1,400 at polling stations across the province pushed results back by more than two hours, but shortly after polls closed on Monday evening the results suggested a decisive win for those looking to remain in Canada.

The separation question – whether to remain in Canada or begin a legal process that could lead to a second vote – was one of 10 put to Albertans on Monday. The others covered issues including immigration, healthcare, education and the province’s constitutional powers. Elections Alberta said the remaining results could take days to count.

In the days leading up to the vote, provincial leaders and federal politicians crisscrossed the province to make the case for a unified country.

Carney warned against a vote from residents of the oil-rich province simply looking to “send a message” to the federal government. “A vote against Canada is a dangerous bluff that will lead to years of deep uncertainty and lost opportunities for Albertans and Canadians,” he told an energetic crowd last week.

The Conservative leader, Pierre Poilievre, whose parliamentary seat is in the province, told a crowd of supporters that Albertans were not “quitters”.

“To those people who are thinking about taking out their frustration on the current government by voting for separation in the referendum, I say this: Canada is more than a government,” he said. “It is a people, a flag, a land, a history and a promise.”

Data from Elections Alberta indicated roughly 43% of eligible voters – or 1.3 million Albertans – had already cast ballots before Monday’s vote through advance voting and mail-in ballots. That figure pointed to a potentially higher turnout than the province’s last referendum in 2021.

Smith’s tenure as leader of the United Conservative party is due in large part to separatists within the party who ousted her predecessor, Jason Kenney. In recent months she has tried to placate both the separatists and federalists in the party, in a province where a majority is not interested in secession.

The referendum became a political liability for Smith’s party, whose popular support has tumbled in recent months, including with the loss of a one-time conservative stronghold in a by-election.

Still, members of the separatist movement told the Guardian they would continue their push for independence. And in the aftermath of the results, one prominent separatist leader said he would push for a special general meeting of the party to remove Smith.

Jeffrey Rath told Global News that the Alberta independence will remain nothing but a dream long as Smith, whom he called an “avowed federalist” remains in power. He said it was time for separatists to “take back ownership” of the UCP.

“I am Canadian because I’m Canadian, not in a transactional way and regardless of the flavour of prime minister,” Farkas said. “And I’m going to continue to make the case for how our future is strongest together, because no result will permanently put this to bed. “The separatist movement sees this as a long-term project.”

#66. Afghan women could lose one of their last paths to a higher education

The US has defunded one of the last universities giving Afghan women the chance to receive an education after the Taliban takeover in 2021. DW spoke with a student who says she's lost hope of ever earning a degree.

"Laila" awoke in the middle of the night for a glass of water, checked her phone almost by accident, and saw an email that made her stomach drop. The US would stop funding the American University of Afghanistan (AUAF), one of the last places women can still study in the country.

Laila said she sat on her bed, staring, her thoughts racing through the past five years and everything she had endured just to get an education.

It had not been easy to find a way to study in Afghanistan after the Taliban outlawed women's education.

The American University campus was already taken over by the Taliban, but she could study secretly through the university's online program. But now that the university is losing its financing, she told DW she and her classmates fear it will shut down entirely. Her name has been changed for safety reasons.

Laila is in her mid-twenties. She studies political science and international studies and is one of around 1,000 enrolled students at the university, three-quarters of them women.

When the Taliban returned to power in August 2021, Laila was studying at another university in Kabul, with just two final exams remaining to finish her semester. Then, in December 2022, the Taliban announced the ban. Laila's degree was left unfinished.

Laila secured a scholarship to AUAF and started over. "Not every girl got that opportunity," she said.

Laila has told few people outside of her family about her online studies. On most days, she stays home for remote lectures, alongside classmates who use aliases and keep their cameras off for security. "I just know their voices," she said.

Laila speaks English with an American accent. She learned it as a child, watching cartoons. In her early twenties, she was learning even more about the world outside Afghanistan and studying foreign governments.

Suddenly, a man came in, stopped at the entrance, and shouted "Allahu Akbar." He yelled that the mujahideen would always be victorious.

That was the day the Taliban retook power in Afghanistan, they soon forced women out of public life, banning them from many jobs and from attending school and university.

Laila said she recalls losing feeling in her hands or feet. She pulled money from her purse and slammed it on the table without checking the amount. "We all thought maybe this would be the last day that we are alive," she said.

"What if they see my emails from my work, from my university?" she said.  "It's always in the back of my head that maybe tomorrow it's my turn. Maybe they will detain me."

Even her home isn't safe. Laila said Taliban authorities have entered on several occasions to search their belongings.

Yet, she has continued studying. For all its limitations and risks, the online program offered a chance to graduate.

With the US pulling out as the university's primary funder, Laila said she doubts whether she has the strength to start over.

The American University of Afghanistan was always intended, largely, for women. Before the campus opened in 2006, former US first lady Laura Bush announced that the US had committed millions to support it.

Appearing in Kabul, Bush said the new university would teach "future leaders," particularly women. Women like Laila, who was four years old at the time.

"These are more than just development projects," Bush said at the time. "They also signify the bond between the American and Afghan people. They are symbols of our shared hopes and dreams."

Patrick Fine was Afghanistan mission director at USAID, the now-defunct American development agency. Fine told DW that many at USAID considered the university idea risky. Critics thought training elites was a waste of money.

But the Bush administration saw an opportunity to stabilize a budding democracy, Fine said.

By 2003, the Taliban had been driven from power. US President George W. Bush wanted to foster goodwill for the US-backed interim government that had replaced the Taliban. The university served that goal, and also offered other "facets of advantage" in educating women, training future politicians, and supporting the Afghan economy.

"It really impacted the lives of thousands of Afghan youth," Fine said, referencing alumni. "The organization has managed to achieve that against incredible odds."

The university continued with US support. But in Washington, that support started to come under question.

The US government suspected the university of mishandling money. In 2016, US inspectors opened an investigation into its finances. By 2018, investigators said they could not tell exactly how it was spending US aid money, because its accounting was so poor.

John Sopko, a former US federal inspector, oversaw that investigation. When DW contacted him for this story, he replied, "I was surprised they were still funding AUAF."

This year, after the funding was pulled, the US State Department Press Office told DW in an email that the move was made given the university's "history of repeated lack of accountability for millions of American taxpayer dollars."

"Those are just smear tactics," he said. He conceded that past audits found the university struggling to keep up with USAID's extensive rules, "but they didn't find major fraud," he said.

The US isn't the university's only funder, but it has long covered most of its costs: nearly 90% as of 2018, according to a US report.

The US State Department told DW that the university is "free to identify other funders and partners, so the end of their receipt of American taxpayer money does not necessarily equate to an end to their operations."

But without the $25 million grant the State Department recently refused, the university's future is in doubt.

"I have lost all my motivation and hope," Laila said, adding that each time she prepares for a lecture, she wonders whether there will be another.

Since she learned about the funding cut, she said she's barely left her room. Her curtains are usually closed, and she's lost her appetite. "Maybe I'm so disappointed that I can't even cry about it," she said.

She said she was supposed to have graduated years ago. She was supposed to be making the world a better place. She was supposed to be studying for a master's degree abroad. Now, she's thinking, maybe she'll find a way in five or ten years.

Health tech companies have become a major target for financially motivated cybercriminals, who aim to steal as much sensitive data as possible and threaten to publish it if they aren’t paid a ransom.

In recent months, a cyberattack resulted in the mass-wiping of employee devices at medical tech maker Stryker in March, and a hack on medical implant manufacturer Boston Scientific in August forced the company’s network offline for weeks, sparking “global disruption” to its operations. Meanwhile, separate hacks throughout the year caused massive breaches of patient data at medical recordkeeping company CareCloud, Scotland-based health tech provider Craneware, and U.S. pharmaceutical distribution giant McKesson.

The lab-testing giant has not yet said how many healthcare customers, or patients, had information stolen during the attack on its Quanum systems.

Quest’s website and materials say Quanum is used by about half a million clinicians in thousands of locations across North America. It’s unclear how many health records Quanum holds, but one 2026 case study on Quest’s website shows how a single health system with more than 6 million patients can hold hundreds of millions of related medical documents and charts.

Quest did not say when the breach began or whether any hacking group has contacted the company with demands, such as payment in exchange to not release the stolen data. Amanda Rivera, a spokesperson for Quest, did not answer TechCrunch’s questions about the incident and referred to its security notice.

In the notice, Quest said that some patients’ protected health information was taken, including “personal information and patient records,” but the company is still investigating what specific kinds of data were taken. (The website post currently contains “noindex” code, which tells search engines not to list the page in search results, making it far more difficult for people to find the page.)

This is Quest’s second known major data breach in a decade, after losing 11.9 million patient records in a cyberattack on a third-party billing company in 2019.

#61. Oil spill washes up on UAE beaches after tanker strike

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

An oil spill from a tanker attacked near the Strait of Hormuz is spreading along the United Arab Emirates (UAE) coastline, forcing the closure of some beaches and coastal areas.

Satellite images from Monday show the beginnings of the oil spill from a ship matching the location and dimensions of Gem No.2, which was damaged between 9 and 10 October during a record surge of vessel strikes by Iran.

The slick of oil had spread more than 35km (22 miles) from the tanker by Wednesday, the satellite imagery showed.

UAE authorities said on Thursday that the incident is affecting beaches near the northern city of Umm Al Quwain and verified video from Friday shows the oil washing ashore, colouring the water black.

The UAE's Ministry of Climate Change and Environment said on Thursday that it "immediately began containment" in order to "limit environmental impacts".

Prof Steve Widdicombe, director of Sharjah Marine Science Research Centre in the UAE, told BBC Verify it was "inevitable that there will be an impact", but researchers caution it is too soon to assess extent of the damage.

Initially a much smaller oil leak could be seen emerging from the ship on 19 October.

But the slick appears to have rapidly spread over two days, according to radar satellite imagery reviewed by BBC Verify and intelligence firm MAIAR.

Oil spills can cause immediate deaths of marine animals and other organisms, for example by physically trapping turtles, smothering the feathers of seabirds or damaging the gills of fish.

They can also cause longer-lasting damage, Prof Widdicombe said, if species remain exposed to toxic compounds from the oil which could impact their ability to reproduce.

Researchers have cautioned the environmental impact at this stage is very uncertain, as it depends on factors including the volume and type of oil spilled, how quickly it evaporates or breaks down and the effectiveness of the clean-up operation.

But the spill does extend towards Siniyah and Khor Al Beidah islands, a globally recognised Key Biodiversity Area containing habitats like corals, seagrasses and saltmarshes, as well as species including green turtles and the critically endangered Halavi guitarfish.

The proximity of the spill to this area is the "most concerning environmental angle", according to Eoghan Darbyshire, a scientist at the Conflict and Environment Observatory.

BBC Verify analysis of the dimensions and location of the ship indicate they match a crude oil tanker called Gem No.2.

Maritime intelligence firm Vanguard reported the Panama-flagged tanker was "struck by an unknown projectile" on 9 October, just north of the town of Al Jazeera Al Hamra, causing a fire on board that was later extinguished.

Verified footage first posted on social media on 10 October showed the crew running across the deck and putting on lifejackets as a large cloud of black smoke rose over the ship.

Iranian state media shared footage of a ship matching Gem No.2 which appeared to show the vessel on fire with burning oil spilling into the sea.

The ship had been attempting to exit the Strait of Hormuz via an "unauthorized and dangerous route", according to state media.

All 22 Indian crew members were rescued, the Indian consulate in Dubai said on 11 October.

Gem No.2 is one of a record number of ships that reported being attacked in and around the Strait of Hormuz at the start of October.

Security experts told BBC Verify this was part of an Iranian effort to assert greater control over the strait, in response to the US military successfully escorting more vessels through the vital shipping lane.

Tankers and other commercial vessels have come under attack from both Iranian and US forces in and around the Strait of Hormuz amid the conflict between the two countries.

Another major oil slick washed up on the shores of Iran's Qeshm Island in August, which experts said threatened wildlife, protected ecosystems and fishing communities.
