#1520. Revealed: Cesc Fabregas told Como players to TAKE MERCY on battered Man United, leaving the score at just 4-1 instead of going for even more

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Published: 08:03 BST, 22 October 2026 | Updated: 15:24 BST, 22 October 2026

Cesc Fabregas revealed that he told his players to ease off Manchester United during their Champions League humiliation at the hands of Como in Italy on Wednesday night.

Como were four goals up against Michael Carrick's side when Fabregas ordered his players to conserve energy ahead of Saturday's Serie A clash with Sassuolo.

United scored a late consolation goal through substitute Joshua Zirkzee, and Fabregas admitted that he was angry at himself for not heaping further embarrassment on his opponents.

'I regret the last 10 minutes because I was the one who told the team to slow down the tempo because there's another match and at 4-0 we could manage things a little,' said the former Arsenal and Chelsea midfielder.

'I told the players to stop running and stay in the middle and that is something I regret because I'm the first to always push them to fight until the end. Their goal hurt me because we dropped our intensity and dropped our defensive block, but it was my fault.

'I'm very happy with how the players applied everything we tried to do, not only in terms of quality, which was high, but also the way we were pressing relentlessly until the 85th minute.

Cesc Fabregas and his Como players celebrate beating Man United - but he wasn't happy

Fabregas shakes hands with embattled Man United boss Michael Carrick after the game

'We are trying to create a culture at the club, a style of play and an identity. Tonight was another convincing moment for us.'

Como celebrated the greatest result in the club's history, seven years after they were playing in the fourth tier of Italian football.

'I can't lie to you that it (beating United) wasn't pride,' added Fabregas. 'We know the history of our club, how much they have suffered over the last many years. Obviously, I hope that tomorrow all the boys and girls go to school wearing the Como shirt and feel proud.

'I always tell the lads, enjoy yourselves, fight, do everything to make the best career possible for each one of you, because it goes by quickly.

'They made me enjoy myself tonight, but not so much for the win, for the result, because they played like I like my team to play, with conviction, with quality, with a clear head, with aggressiveness, with a fierce pressing.'

Como's star man was Nico Paz who tormented United with two goals and an assist to hand the Premier League club their heaviest defeat in Europe since losing 4-0 to Barcelona in 1994.

'It has been one of the most beautiful nights of my career,' said Paz. 'I'm still young, but I will never forget it. I used to dream of playing in a match like this, and tonight we've gone even further. It's a historic night for Como fans.'

#1514. Will this sleep-disorder drug be the next big blockbuster?

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Amanda Heidt is a freelance journalist in southeast Utah.

When US regulators approved a treatment for narcolepsy in August, some researchers immediately predicted a bright future for the class of compounds it belongs to, known as orexin agonists. Much like blockbuster GLP-1 drugs, such as Ozempic, which were initially approved to treat diabetes but have since taken off as treatments for other conditions, these new drugs seem capable of much more than treating sleep disorders.

First-of-its-kind narcolepsy drug opens door to new therapies for the brain

First-of-its-kind narcolepsy drug opens door to new therapies for the brain

“Diabetes was the tip of the iceberg for GLP-1s, and I’m convinced the same story will play out for orexin,” says Andrew Plump, president of research and development at Takeda, the Tokyo-based pharmaceutical company that developed the newly approved drug, called Orzeyful (oveporexton). “Our goal now is to unravel the science and understand what’s below the surface.”

This excitement comes on the heels of decades of research about orexins — peptides that are produced by the brain and that have a role in modulating metabolism, mood, appetite, memory and, most notably, sleep and wakefulness cycles. Now, scientists are racing to work out how best to apply what they have found in the clinic, exploring the use of orexin agonists in the treatment of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and other conditions in which attention and alertness are dysregulated.

“Anyone who has gotten a poor night of sleep can tell you that it impacts nearly every aspect of their life,” says Luis de Lecea, a psychiatrist at Stanford University in California, who was among the first to identify orexins. “It is very rewarding to have a hand in something that has led to a drug that helps people.”

De Lecea was a budding scientist with a new laboratory at the Scripps Research Institute in San Diego, California, when, in the mid-1990s, he took on a daunting project: identifying proteins produced only in certain parts of the human brain. Groups at the institute were divvying up brain regions to study, and de Lecea chose the hypothalamus, an almond-sized region deep in the brain’s interior.

The team generated a list of hypothalamus-specific messenger RNAs — small chunks of genetic material that carry the instructions for making proteins. Sifting through the list, the scientists identified a pair of neuropeptides that they named hypocretin 1 and 21. Unbeknown to de Lecea, researchers in Texas had, around the same time, isolated the equivalent peptides in rats, dubbing them orexin A and B2.

Neuroscientist Takeshi Sakurai was working in the lab of Masashi Yanagisawa, a molecular biologist then at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center in Dallas, when the group first identified the orexins. The researchers suspected that the peptides regulated feeding in rodents — the name comes from the Greek word orexis, meaning appetite — but when they artificially blocked orexin signalling, they found something unexpected.

Narcolepsy confirmed as autoimmune disease

Narcolepsy confirmed as autoimmune disease

“The animals sometimes abruptly collapsed,” says Sakurai, now at the University of Tsukuba in Japan. The behaviour bore a striking resemblance to a hallmark of narcolepsy type 1 in humans: a sudden, temporary loss of muscle control known as cataplexy. After further work, the team landed on a more complete understanding of the role of orexins. “Orexin does not simply wake us up, it keeps wakefulness stable,” he says.

Back in California, researchers were making more discoveries. Emmanuel Mignot, a sleep-medicine specialist at Stanford, and his colleagues were the first to identify a mutation in the gene that encodes orexin receptor 2 as the cause of canine narcolepsy3. In a study of people with narcolepsy type 1 who had died, Jerome Siegel, a neuroscientist at the University of California, Los Angeles, and his colleagues found that the brains of these people nearly always had a massive loss of the neurons that produce orexins4.

It’s a small population — just 70,000 cells among the hundreds of thousands in the hypothalamus — yet they seem to be crucial.

“Once those neurons are gone, the brain isn’t able to find a workaround,” Siegel says.

From the moment they first noted the link between orexins and sleep, Sakurai says he and his team recognized the therapeutic potential. In a few years, so did pharmaceutical companies.

The first investigations at Takeda began in the late 2000s as a pet project. One of the technicians there had a friend with narcolepsy type 1, and he began looking for a molecule that could bind to orexin receptors and restore a normal sleep–wake cycle.

When the technician scanned Takeda’s libraries for a molecule that met the criteria, only a single candidate, called TAK-925, came back. Going forward with it would be a risk, says Plump. “No chemist in their right mind would start a drug-discovery programme with a single hit.”

First orexin agonists address root cause of narcolepsy

First orexin agonists address root cause of narcolepsy

Nevertheless, Takeda researchers gave it a try and the molecule performed well in animals. When the company launched the first human trial in 2017, the technician drove his friend to the study centre so that she could participate. He contacted Plump right away, saying that the drug seemed to work for her. “He could tell just from speaking with her,” Plump says.

Orzeyful, which has now received approval in three countries, builds on the foundation that TAK-925 and other early candidates set. The drug is a marked improvement on existing therapies that only mask symptoms of the disease and often come with severe side effects.

Mignot, who led Takeda’s late-stage trials says that people report that “after they take this drug, suddenly they really know what it is to be awake”.

With the approval of Orzeyful, competitors are now racing to tap into a global market for narcolepsy drugs that analysts predict could top US$6.4 billion by 2031. Narcolepsy — both type 1 and type 2, which is not associated with a die-off of orexin-producing neurons — affects roughly three million people worldwide, and estimates suggest that many more cases go unreported or misdiagnosed.

#1501. FIFA president Gianni Infantino apologizes for World Cup privatization plans but still faces scrutiny

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

FIFA president Gianni Infantino apologized to members of the FIFA Council in a meeting on Thursday in Zurich, the first gathering of the organization's main decision-making body since Infantino announced and then scrapped a controversial plan to privatize the World Cup.

The specifics of Infantino's apology were not made public but FIFA said that the Council was updated on the legal background behind FIFA Forward Enterprise (FFE), which was a proposed subsidiary that controlled the commercial rights to the men's and women's World Cups and Club World Cups. FFE was valued at around $20 billion and minority stakes were to be sold to private investors headlined by Thrive Eternal, the investment firm of venture capitalist Joshua Kushner, the brother of U.S. president Donald Trump's son-in-law, Jared. The plan received criticism from the moment it became public knowledge in a report from the Times of London in July and was abandoned within a matter of days, though a legal battle over the proposal is now underway in the U.S. and the incident continues to loom large as Infantino seeks re-election.

Infantino fielded questions from Council members about FFE on Thursday and asked them to submit more queries for a subsequent meeting, the session ultimately drawing mixed reviews from those in the room. Ahmed Yahya, the president of Mauritania's federation, said he believes Infantino is the "right president" and that the majority of FIFA's member associations (MAs) feel the same way but others held back on endorsements. German Football Association president Bernd Neuendorf insisted he had no confidence in Infantino, while England's chairwoman Debbie Hewitt said Infantino was not forthcoming enough in his answers on Thursday.

"One of the disappointing things is that we didn't get answers to anything that we had specifically requested, for example, getting the papers from FFE," Hewitt said. "I asked the question, 'Why not?' and we were asked to submit those questions in writing. I think the man has to answer those questions and if he doesn't answer those questions now, he's not fit to be FIFA president and that was what was shared in the meeting. I've repeatedly asked for the papers to be shared. I haven't received them and still in spite of the request today, I haven't received them."

Infantino attempted to address some of the concerns around FFE, including agreeing to set up a working group to review governance procedures. FIFA also proposed an increase to the funds it usually distributes to the 211 MAs, who could now pocket $25 million over the next four-year cycle should the plan get the green light. This was in response to criticism from UEFA, Concacaf and the AFC that FIFA should use its existing reserves to further fund soccer projects around the world rather than FFE, which was unpopularly billed as a means to increase available resources for MAs.

The scrutiny on the FIFA president does not end there. He continues to face criticism for FIFA's handling of Folarin Balogun's red card suspension during the World Cup, in which the governing body cited a rarely used rule to lift his one-match ban for the U.S. men's national team's round of 16 defeat to Belgium. Infantino has been unable to shake the allegation that political pressure led to the decision, particularly after Trump said he called Infantino asking him to review FIFA's longstanding rules.

FIFA's legal team will now review the disciplinary procedures following the Balogun affair and are set to present their findings at the Council's next meeting in December. Infantino's detractors continue to cite the incident as an example of the president's shortcomings in recent months, including the awarding of the inaugural FIFA Peace Prize to Trump at last year's World Cup draw in Washington, D.C. It is unclear if the Peace Prize will be awarded again this year, since FIFA's Social Responsibility Committee will assess the award before the Council makes a decision on it in December.

The choices regarding the Peace Prize and Balogun's suspension were taken outside of FIFA's usual governance procedures, which is why Council members continue to ask for more information on how those decisions were reached.

"It is the rationale for why FFE happened in the way that it did, the Balogun case, the Peace Prize," Hewitt said. "The responses to those questions, which I still don't have answers to, will determine from my personal point of view whether or not Gianni is the right candidate. That will be the point at which or not we can say that any individual that was involved in FFE can or cannot be trusted because until we see what actually happened it's very difficult to say behavior needs or not to change … Those papers coming to Council are a crucial next part of us feeling comfortable as to what, if any, criminal proceedings need to take place."

Hewitt believed there was "some contrition" in Infantino's apology but said the president did not offer enough information to change her stance on his re-election.

"From my position, nothing has shifted," she said. "I've no new information that makes me feel any differently, other than we do have a president who is apologetic."

Infantino is seeking his fourth and final term in March's election, which will take place during the FIFA Congress in Rabat, Morocco. Ex-Switzerland international Ramon Vega is his only opponent thus far though prospective candidates have until Nov. 18 to submit their interest.
