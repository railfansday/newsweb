#1960. Hull City vs Brentford - prediction, team news, lineups

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

By Ben Sully | 23 Oct 2026 18:06, Last updated: 23 Oct 2026 22:44

Sunday's Premier League meeting at the MKM Stadium will pit Hull City's unbeaten home record against a Brentford side yet to lose on their travels this term.

The Bees head into the weekend in sixth spot in the Premier League table, while the Tigers are one point worse off in ninth position.

Hull made an impressive start to their first Premier League campaign in 10 years, taking eight points from a possible 12 to occupy a top-four spot after the opening four matchdays.

However, Sergej Jakirovic's side have experienced something of a reality check in recent times, having mustered just one point from their last three outings.

After falling to their first league defeat of the season away to Newcastle United, the Tigers played out a 2-2 draw at home to Everton, before they fell to a 1-0 defeat against Fulham at Craven Cottage last Saturday.

Jakirovic admitted after the game that "it was men against boys", a comment that was backed up by the fact Hull failed to register a single shot on target and registered just 139 passes, their lowest Premier League pass tally since mustering 134 in a 1-0 defeat to Blackburn Rovers in February 2010.

While they are now winless in five Premier League games, Hull are still sitting in the top half of the table and will be looking forward to returning to the MKM Stadium, where they are yet to lose this season after recording one win and two draws in their first three home games.

The Tigers claimed clean sheets in two of those three home outings, and they will need all that defensive resilience when they take on a Brentford side that are the league's fourth-highest scorers with 13 goals.

While Sunday's fixture may be the first top-flight meeting between the two sides, Hull have won four of their last five competitive home games against Brentford, although they slumped to a heavy 5-1 defeat in their most recent encounter at the MKM Stadium in February 2020.

Brentford, meanwhile, are sitting in sixth spot and three points adrift of the top four after winning two, drawing four and losing one of their first seven matches.

Keith Andrews's side suffered their first loss of the season in last Saturday's home clash with Liverpool, despite taking a 44th-minute lead through Keane Lewis-Potter.

However, the complexion of the contest changed dramatically when Yehor Yarmolyuk was sent off for violent conduct in first-half stoppage time, handing Liverpool a numerical advantage that helped them turn the game in their favour with goals from Victor Munoz and Alexander Isak.

The loss was compounded by Vitaly Janelt's late red card, leaving Andrews with some crucial selection decisions to make ahead of the club's first meeting with Hull in six years, with the visitors looking to extend their unbeaten head-to-head run to four matches after previously recording a pair of 5-1 wins on either side of a 1-1 draw.

The Bees head into the weekend as one of four Premier League teams yet to lose on the road this season, although they are also still searching for their first away league win after playing out draws against Leeds United, Bournemouth and Aston Villa.

In fact, Brentford have gone eight consecutive top-flight away games since enjoying a run of three consecutive road wins in February.

The visitors will at least fancy their chances of striking first in Sunday's fixture, having netted the opening goal in all seven of their seven Premier League outings this season.

Hull's considerable injury list features Jack Butland, Charlie Hughes, Matt Targett, Eliot Matazo, Darko Gyabi and Ilyas Ansah.

Defender Nobel Mendy is set to return to team training on Monday, with the aim of him returning to the squad for the home clash against Ipswich Town on October 31.

Mohamed-Ali Cho and Lucas Gourna-Douath, who have both missed the last two games, are in contention to make Sunday's squad after recently making their welcome return to training.

In more positive news, Robinio Vaz has seemingly avoided any lasting damage from the tackle that saw Fulham's Ryan Sessegnon sent off in the closing stages of last Saturday's contest.

As for the Bees, they remain without the services of Joshua Dasilva, Kaye Furo and Antoni Milambo due to injury.

Defender Jannik Schuster is back in training after missing the defeat to Liverpool due to a combination of illness and a knock.

Andrews will be forced to completely change his central midfield pairing, with Yarmolyuk and Janelt set to serve three-match and one-game bans, respectively.

Mathias Jensen will drop back from the number 10 spot to operate alongside Mamadou Sangare in the engine room, while Mikkel Damsgaard and Kevin Schade are set to come into the side to feature in the attacking trio behind Igor Thiago.

We had a training session planned after the game for the players that didn’t have enough minutes to give everybody the recovery day on Sunday, and to get everybody in the right rhythm. In two very unfortunate actions, we lost two players, and yeah, not great.

They’re both going to miss tomorrow’s match, that’s for sure and today they’re going to have a further scan. Tomorrow we’ll know more. Then we’ll see how they’re going to go. Piero’s one looks a little bit more serious I think, but we’ll have to wait and see.

Havertz wasn’t great on Saturday, it’s not as if he put in the kind of performance that absolutely demands to start the next game, and as I said before Leeds, it’s Arteta’s job to get Gyokeres going. Once he’s on the pitch the responsibility lies with the player himself, but unless you give him that opportunity to play, there’s little he can do to change the trajectory of his season thus far. I can’t say I’m his biggest fan, but I want to see him start this evening. #1954. The night Rio Ngumoha cemented his status as a future world-beater: Liverpool had a £400m attack at their disposal but it was the 18-year-old who shone brightest in chaotic comeback win, writes LEWIS STEELE

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Published: 22:24 BST, 20 October 2026 | Updated: 23:57 BST, 20 October 2026

They used to call it 'Pep roulette' at Manchester City. A phrase coined by fantasy football nerds about Pep Guardiola's tendency to chop and change his front line every week to devastating effect.

We haven't refined our snazzy phrase for Andoni Iraola yet – the working drafts are 'AI Bingo' or 'Iraola tombola' – but the Liverpool boss has a devastating depth of attacking talent that, if they all fulfil their potential, can lead this team to dizzying heights.

That is caveated with a reminder that Iraola's Reds are simultaneously plagued by many teething problems in other areas and are far from complete. We will discuss that in due course but first, a word on Iraola's deck of cards that contains multiple aces and jokers.

With the best part of half a billion pounds worth of attackers not starting this comeback victory against VIllarreal, it was left to others to step up and no one caught the eye more than the potential world-beater but lesser-heralded Rio Ngumoha.

At just 18, Ngumoha already has three England caps and is well on the radar of Thomas Tuchel, but he is not yet guaranteed a starting berth at Liverpool and has had to bide his time due to competition and having been asked to swap wings by Iraola after the arrival of left-winger Bradley Barcola.

The teenager, who was poached from Chelsea in 2024 to much fury at Stamford Bridge, will cost a maximum of £6.8million but his ceiling is just as high as £125m Alexander Isak, £123m Barcola or £116m Florian Wirtz.

Liverpool beat Villarreal 4-3 in a chaotic Champions League encounter at Anfield on Tuesday

Alexis Mac Allister was Liverpool's hero, scoring twice as the Reds came from behind to win

But it was Rio Ngumoha's display that was most impressive as he showed all of his quality

Ngumoha is yet to score this season but was the shining light here with two assists in a spell-binding performance that helped tilt the game in Liverpool's favour. That was far from guaranteed after a shoddy, uncharacteristic and worrying first half an hour.

Alexis Mac Allister is also worthy of vast praise as the Argentine struck a brace to continue his resurgence and stellar season after a year-long dip, while Ronald Araujo and substitute Wirtz also scored.

As the scoreline suggests, Liverpool's defence was all at sea and that will provide a headache for Iraola in the coming days, especially given they have conceded 10 in their last four games, also shipping three to Manchester City and Austrian side LASK.

But while the boss agonises over fixing the defence, he knows he has cracked it at the other end. Ngumoha can trouble any defence, Isak can win any game with his instinctive poacher-like finishing, Wirtz and Barcola are starting to show signs they can unlock stubborn sides.

Then there is Cody Gakpo, who returned from injury and showed signs he is picking up where he left off, and the energetic Victor Munoz, who missed this game but was described as a 'Formula One car' in these pages yesterday. And don't forget £79m injured striker Hugo Ekitike.

Liverpool's array of weapons in attack were needed to help them get over the mess of the first half an hour, as Iraola was being taught a lesson by his former right-hand man, Inigo Perez, who was the No 2 during the Basque's formative years at Rayo Vallecano.

Perez could not move to England when Iraola did because some wise cracks at the Home Office said he was not skilled enough for a work permit.

The 38-year-old has made a mockery of that since, leading Rayo to the Conference League final after taking over from Iraola as head coach and now managing in the Champions League, where, for 35 minutes at least, his team played six-time European kings Liverpool off the park.

Liverpool fought back after a dreadful first 30 minutes that saw Villarreal go 2-0 ahead

Ronald Araujo scored his second goal in a week and celebrated by jumping into the stands

Florian Wirtz (right) was also on the scoresheet to cap off a good few days for the German

Rio Ngumoha said his virtuoso performance against Villarreal was up there with the best in his budding career.

The 18-year-old registered two assists and was the star man as Liverpool put a worrying start behind them to come from 2-0 down to beat their Spanish opposition.

And the three-time England cap said: 'It's up there with one of the best I've played, I think. I enjoyed myself out there. The team helped me as well a lot. I enjoyed today.

'Like I always say, we have the best fans in the world. To hear my name being chanted is a dream.'

Boss Andoni Iraola added: 'The good thing with Rio is he is still 18, he is not the full package, he can add things fortunately for us.

'Hopefully he can continue in the same way. He's not always going to be like this, he will have ups and downs in the process.

'But I'm happy because attitude-wise I think he's in a great place.'

Alexis Mac Allister was also a shining light and scored twice. The Argentine said: 'Look at Rio, he was amazing tonight. He made the difference. He was really important and I'm glad for him.

'It's clear the quality Rio has. Everyone knows. It's easy to say he's a really good player but he needs to focus on other things. He worked really hard off the ball.'

On his fine headed goal, Mac Allister added: 'It's not something I really practice. My dad is a bit smaller than me but he was quite good in the air so maybe it's something I got from him.

'Last year was a really difficult year for all of us. It was clear I was not at the level and I am the first one to say. Something wasn't right in the group and this manager changed it. The way we play is different, I don't like to compare. I'm feeling good.'

Villarreal were magic, Liverpool tragic. This was by far the worst half hour of the Iraola era, more worrying than the capitulation against Manchester City. At least that time they had the defence of going down to 10 men.

No excuse on this occasion, apprentice Perez was simply schooling master Iraola.

Villarreal, nicknamed El Submarino Amarillo or The Yellow Submarine, were enjoying themselves in the land of the Beatles – whose song inspired that tag and is played before games at Estadio de la Ceramica. They were ahead after just four minutes.

Virgil van Dijk made a honking error to head a long ball straight to the path of Georges Mikautadze, who could not believe his luck and scored. Van Dijk barely makes one wrong step, never mind a catastrophic mistake like this, and everyone at Anfield looked shellshocked.

The 1,500 or so away fans and sea of vibrant yellow shirts were soon jumping for joy again on 29 minutes when Sergi Cardona sprung through a shoddy offside trap and waltzed through on goal to slot past a helpless Alisson for 2-0.

Iraola was left scratching his head and probably regretting the six changes he made from the team that fought from behind to beat Brentford on Saturday.

But out of nowhere, his team found something and started the comeback, through a Mac Allister header on 36 minutes. The Argentine may be small but is as good as most in the air and connected well with Kostas Tsimikas's corner to score.

Anfield found its voice and the Reds responded. Ngumoha made the equaliser with a fine ball that was headed home by Ronald Araujo for his second goal in a week after scoring at LASK.

The Uruguayan loanee, here for the season from Barcelona, is quickly becoming a cult hero and is being affectionately referred to as 'Big Ron'. He jumped into the Anfield Road End and made the night of one lucky lady, who received a bear hug.

There was never any looking back from there, and Mac Allister put Liverpool ahead on 65 minutes after a dancing run from Ngumoha, before Wirtz came on and put gloss on the victory with a fine finish to continue an eye-catching week for the much-criticised German.

Tani Oluwaseyi made it a nervy finish with an 87th-minute goal which highlighted Liverpool's defensive issues once more, but at least they can follow the old mantra of 'you can score, we will just score more'.

How long that will last, who knows, but it is certainly not going to be boring this year.

#1951. Andoni Iraola, you genius: He's found Liverpool a superstar

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Liverpool handed Andoni Iraola several problems this season. The Reds' head coach has found a genius solution, however.

Andoni Iraola took over Liverpool in the summer but things weren't made easy for him. The squad just isn't balanced, for one, with glaring problems in midfield, defence and attack.

The main midfield issue? A lack of a defensive midfielder. And arguably, a lack of real options in the side - the club sold Curtis Jones without replacing him and asked Iraola to rely on Trey Nyoni.

The defensive issues are different. They're more long-term - only Jeremy Jacquet and Giovanni Leoni are contracted to Liverpool at centre-back beyond this season and one of those hasn't played in over a year.

Then there's the attack, which is different once again. The options are there but they're unbelievably lopsided. Four left-wingers, zero right-wingers, and that's asked Iraola to be creative.

The solution to finding a right-winger is to convert a left-winger. That much is obvious. It's easier said than done, however, as all of Liverpool's options are right-footed and all prefer to cut inside.

They can't naturally do that from the other flank and it's why I didn't believe Rio Ngumoha could play that role. He's raw, still learning the game, and so asking him to do something completely different to what got him into the first-team seemed mad, quite honestly.

Ngumoha likes to cut inside and work a shot at goal. Maybe a pass into the box. Cutting inside from the right means being on his left and suddenly a shot at goal isn't all that viable.

Iraola, though, stuck to his guns and persisted with Ngumoha on the right. It has paid dividends. The teenager is more dangerous than ever, learning to consistently get to the byline and split defences, rather than cutting inside in front of them.

It's a far, far more dangerous skill than just working the ball onto your shooting foot. Villarreal saw that first hand as they had no real idea of how to stop Ngumoha - he could drive on the outside of them, quickly getting them all running back towards their own goal.

A cut-back led to Alexis Mac Allister scoring, while he also demonstrated his ability to switch flanks with a first-half assist from the left for Ronald Araujo. (Even if that one did rely on some shocking defending).

But Iraola's insistence that Ngumoha can learn to play on the right has proven to be genius. Liverpool have a better player as a result - someone who asks serious questions of defences and bring an entirely new element to the team.

Defences can't simply stay compact and deny an opportunity to cut inside. That allows Ngumoha to get into the box and hit the byline close to goal. Instead, they'll need a different defence to stop Liverpool - which only opens the door further for others.

#1950. Ken Friar talks to Nick Ames | So Paddy Got Up (2012)

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

In 2012, journalist Nick Ames, then working for Arsenal, interviewed Ken Friar for an Arseblog Anthology called ‘So Paddy Got Up’.

In light of today’s very sad news of Mr Friar’s passing, we felt it apt to revisit that conversation as it appeared then.

The door is slightly ajar when I knock, as it so often is. Ken Friar OBE must have looked out of his office window, or perhaps even walked out onto his balcony overlooking Drayton Park, hundreds of times – surveying the sweeping, poignant panorama of the new Highbury Square development that these days greets his every sideways glance. Now, though, he’s facing squarely the other way and watching the screen of his Mac. It’s August, a particularly busy month in modern-day football, and the man still regarded by all and sundry as ‘Mr Friar’ shows absolutely no sign of letting up.

Almost to the day, this is the start of his 62nd year on Arsenal’s permanent staff. when you add another five or so as a temporary employee, he’s been a cog – of every size imaginable – in the Arsenal wheel for comfortably more than half of the club’s 125-year existence. Nobody has embodied the Gunners’ tradition, and its concurrent moving with the times, as adeptly or as selflessly even if, as he puts it with characteristic self-deprecating humour, “I’m still looking for a regular job!”

If the club’s rate of change during the first half-century of his employment could be described as stately, the last decade’s developments have been positively supersonic; it’s rarely been easy and has, as he tells me, often been plain exhausting. The warmth and wit remain, though, as does the twinkle in his eye as he recounts just some of a wealth of Arsenal experiences that will, surely, never be matched.

The beginning seems a very long time ago now, but can we start there? The story of how your involvement with Arsenal began is well-told, but remarkable, and bears repeating.

It’s honestly not a story I’m proud of, just one of those things than happened and I suppose you can’t alter history. I was a young boy and, as boys did then, we were playing football with a tennis ball outside the stadium on Avenell Road. The ball rolled away, and I ran after it as it disappeared underneath a big car. I scrambled under it, trying to rescue the ball, when a voice boomed out: “Boy, what are you doing?” I was scared to death! It turned out to be George Allison, the manager at the time. For some reason, he then told me to come back the following day. Why I obeyed him, I have no idea even now – it would have been easier to run away, and that would have been the end of it. But I returned as directed, and that’s how it all started.

I went back and saw him – he introduced me to the box-office manager of the time and I then started as managing director! No, I began on half a crown a week running messages from the front door to the box office on a match-day. I was at Highbury County, a local grammar school, so this was something extra. But believe me, I still have no idea what made me come back.

But message boy to managing director is still an incredible leap. How did the progression work from there?

When the time to leave school arrived I had various things on the table. I wanted to be a stockbroker, and there was a position with a firm that I was really keen on taking. Five years had passed since I first started working at Arsenal, and the Club had been onto my family saying they’d like me to come and work full-time. My parents agreed, but I wasn’t keen because the salary I’d been offered at the stockbrokers’ office was twice as high – another £1.50 a week – as I’d get at Arsenal. Eventually they won, and I started here as a full-time employee in 1950, on £78 a year. From there, I worked through all kinds of departments. The business was very different then, remember – even when we were still over at Highbury in the early 2000’s, we only had one hundred and nine staff. That’s nearly quadrupled now.

From then, your rise was – in its understated way – meteoric…

Well, it was steady. I first became a bit more involved at boardroom level in 1965 – the club secretary was the big job back then and I was made his assistant. I moved up to become secretary in 1973; then became managing director ten years later. I stayed in that position until a time when I very much thought I’d be playing golf, and ended up deeply involved with Danny Fiszman and the move to Emirates, as you know.

Does the growth you spoke of just now astonish you?

I sometimes sit back, yes, and think of the way the club has changed and evolved. But remember, Nick, you’ve grown up in a period when everything is there. Back then we had no photocopiers, for example. Any reports had to be, typed and if there were twelve copies the girl had to do each one three times with carbon papers. I would produce reports and it would take me perhaps a day to do a report I could now write in five minutes.

Another example for you – tickets. Every ticket was produced by a printing company. They’d arrive in books and then had to be checked physically against the seating plan. Then you’d sell them over the counter and people would queue to buy them from the front entrance in Avenell Road up the hill, down the hill, right along to the tube station – thousands upon thousands of people, when it was a big game. We did all that on very few staff, but now it’s unfathomable to people.

And that’s just part of it – then there were the other areas of the business. Players used to get one-year contracts, there was a fixed salary and every year you’d renew all of the contacts in one day! We didn’t have massive numbers of shareholders or much of a commercial operation – instead we had lotteries that made us a £1000 a week and that felt like a fortune. We eventually had the highest shirt sponsorship deal around, with JVC, for over £100,000 a year and that was a big thing in its day. The scale of things has changed immeasurably.

With that, business practices themselves have moved on. Is it less about the bonhomie now?

It always has been, and still is, very cordial, but you have to remember that, although the bonhomie you mention was always there, the Club was still run like a business. The likes of Sir Samuel Hill-Wood and Sir Bracewell-Smith were both big businesspeople; Denis Hill-Wood was a senior partner in a stockbroking firm; Peter Hill-Wood was vice-chairman of a bank; Sir Chips Keswick was chairman of one. So it’s not been all “What’s next old chap?” – far from it. Once that door shuts it’s very much a business affair. In the boardroom it’s always been “Chairman” and “Sir” even if we’re on first-name terms, and are very good friends, outside it.

I count twenty major trophies in the cabinet since you started here. It’s such a broad question, but have any been more satisfying than others?

It’s hard to talk about any in particular; there have been so many events, memories and successes. I suppose one had to be the 1970/71 ‘double’ because we’d not won anything since 1953 – we’d endured seventeen years with nothing and suddenly started to win things. That was enormous and such a great satisfaction. There was also 1989 at Anfield of course. But tomorrow’s match is always the most important – that’s a maxim I always stand by.

Seventeen years with nothing perhaps puts the current run into perspective. But it leads me to ask you whether there have ever been any particularly dark periods in the years here – times when you thought the outlook was bleak.

There have certainly been times where you think the light’s been switched off, yes. But, honestly, it’s always been a great place to work and it’s the sort of job you wouldn’t be able to do unless you absolutely loved it. It’s more than a nine-to-five – the demands can be huge. I remember we’d be working over eighty hours a week regularly, for nearly six years, when we were working on the new stadium. I remember Danny once asking: “Do you feel tired?”

I said, “I do, a little bit,” and he then told me we’d worked eighty-seven hours that week. So that, in particular, was a very challenging time.

We were starting meetings at lawyers’ offices at 8am and I’d sometimes put my key in my lock back home at six o’clock the following morning. My son was living with us at the time as his house was being decorated – one morning I gingerly put the key in the door so as not to wake anybody. He came down the stairs and asked: “What time do you call this?!”

It was exactly what I’d been saying to him all his life.

Well, for me it’s been a privilege. I’ve been so lucky to be involved with a club like this. We’ve worked as a team, we’ve had a great unit off the pitch and it’s always been ‘we’ rather than ‘me’.

The ‘we’ includes countless memorable characters from down the years, of course – does anyone in particular spring to mind?

I remember an old guy that worked in the ticket office when I first started – somebody came in and said they’d like to buy two seats for the weekend and asked if there would be a post in front of the seats. The chap said sternly: “Sir, I would remind you that our stand is supported by voluntary contribution only!” There were so many characters like that that maybe you wouldn’t get today. Then there were people like Denis Hill-Wood, Peter’s father – he was a great personality and loved by everyone, a real part of the Club’s fabric. And there are the ex-players, too, those who keep coming back and being a part of that we do. We still see the 1970/71 guys on a regular basis for example, and others from down the years too. Arthur Shaw is now 84 and he still comes in regularly. That affinity, that love, really counts for something.

A phenomenon. What he did was phenomenal. He gave up so much, and probably lost a lot of money personally, in devoting the same eighty-odd hours a week – bearing in mind he had quite a big business to run too. He was utterly devoted to the Club, loved it – he’d wear the same red socks to every match through superstition, and we went to every game together home and away. He was a great, great character and a lovely, lovely man.

What makes Arsenal, the club of which you are such an enduring symbol, differ from others?

It’s the people that make the Club. The fans make it, the manager makes it and all the people that work for it make it. We have a set of values that I think are treasured, and we should never lose sight of those values – they were laid in place some eighty or ninety years ago by the Hill-Wood family and have been perpetuated ever since. I suppose if I’m to take credit for anything it’s for playing a part in continuing those traditions. Tradition doesn’t pay the wages but it’s a very important part of the structure, the fabric, of the Club.

Here’s an example. In 1939, players going off to the war were granted bonuses of £500 – a big thing back then. These bonuses were to be loaned back to the club at two-percent, so actually they never got the money. That came to light in the early 1980’s – the chairman found out and I was told to go back and find as many of these ex-players as we could. We then compounded that money and paid them some forty years on. That’s one real-life instance of the values we seek to live by.

The number of managers you’ve worked with here is in double figures, but you and Arsène have been particularly close…

It’s been a great time. He’s good at everything, one of those people you wish you hated! We were once in a meeting with people of four or five nationalities and he just kept changing languages effortlessly. Once or twice he’s shaken me – we might have a relatively obscure player recommended and he’d reel off the chap’s full professional and personal details off the top of his head. We were in Spain recently and he knew twenty of the players on the teamsheet, everything about them. He has a phenomenal football brain and can discuss any other subject you like with remarkable aplomb. I’ve travelled all over the place with him and he’s never lost his temper with anyone! It goes without saying that we’ve been so lucky to have him, and the day he decides to leave us will certainly be one of the saddest days.

On the other side of this corridor, it’s possible to look out onto the bridge that was named after you last year. Could you ever have imagined it?

It’s a bit embarrassing; I’ll have to catch the people responsible! We’d planned what we’d do to commemorate Danny, then the meeting was stopped and an addendum announced that a decision had been made to name the second bridge after me. I don’t even remember what I said at the opening ceremony – but I think it was along the lines of “when I started I thought the only thing they’d ever name after me was a coat-hanger.” Needless to say, someone gave me one of my very own with my name on it the other day! But it’s incredibly flattering to have a bridge named after me – now I just need planning permission for a toll at either end of it!

And as you cross that bridge daily, walking between Highbury House and the stadium, do you feel that you have as much energy as ever?

Well, I’ve cut back now; it’s just sixty hours a week! No, I’m still here every morning before 8 – I try not to stay into the evening as the pressure isn’t there to do so. But look, I work because I love the place and the people. I still get a great kick out of it, and am probably the luckiest guy alive to still be doing what I’ve always loved to do.

The interview reaches its natural conclusion, we say our goodbyes and instantly, after shutting the office door on my way out, I hear Mr Friar returning one of the calls he’s evidently missed during our conversation. Six hours of the working day still remain; not a second will be wasted, because not one ever has been.

The landscape upon which his labour of love sits may have changed forever, but a man whose term of service extends well beyond the combined ages of Theo Walcott, Jack Wilshere and Alex Oxlade-Chamberlain is not planning to wind down any time soon.
