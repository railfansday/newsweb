A Chelsea youngster has been advised to consider his future at Stamford Bridge after struggling to secure regular minutes under Xabi Alonso, with a former Blues winger explaining why a temporary departure could be the best move for his development.

The teenager arrived in west London with enormous expectations, but his progress has stalled amid fierce competition for places in Chelsea’s attack.

With opportunities limited this season, questions are beginning to emerge over whether he can develop as quickly as the club would hope without more regular first-team football.

Former Chelsea winger Scott Sinclair believes the situation highlights the importance of playing consistently at a young age.

Sinclair knows what it takes to develop at Chelsea, having experienced several loan spells during his own time at the club.

Speaking to Andy’s Bet Club, per the Metro, he explained: “As a young player, all you want to do is play football.

“In my experience, having been on loan six times during my spell at Chelsea, you just want to be playing for your parent club. But when you aren’t, you have to go out on loan to gain experience and game time.

“The longer you go without playing, the less match-fit you become, which makes it even harder to get into the side when an opportunity arises and that leads to doubt.

“Only Estevao can decide what is best for him, but because he is so young, getting consistent game time is essential and having been in the same position, I’d say he should go and play.”

Sinclair’s comments underline the risk of allowing a talented young player to spend too much time on the sidelines, particularly when regular football is so important for development and confidence.

Chelsea invested heavily in their young attacker and will be keen to see that investment pay off. However, the competition for attacking places means a consistent role in Alonso’s starting XI is far from guaranteed.

The Brazilian — once described as “special” by former Blues boss Enzo Maresca — enjoyed an encouraging first season at Stamford Bridge, scoring eight goals in 36 appearances across all competitions. Yet he has struggled to build on that start, having not made a Premier League start this season.

A January loan move could provide a potential solution, giving him the opportunity to gain valuable experience and return to Chelsea better prepared to compete for a regular role.

A permanent departure would be a much bigger decision, particularly given his potential and long-term importance to the club.

For now, his immediate priority will be earning more minutes under Alonso. If those opportunities continue to prove elusive, Sinclair believes playing elsewhere could be the right step for his development.

#1919. McKennie’s versatility gives Juventus more tactical options

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

As Serie A resumes after the international break, Weston McKennie’s importance to Juventus is less about a single position than the range of problems he can help solve. Football Italia has examined the idea of a “McKennie zone”, but its published account does not map out fixed boundaries; the clearest takeaway is that his adaptability could make him increasingly influential.

The Football Italia Show, presented by Lorenzo Bettoni and Matt Santangelo, focused on McKennie’s tactical function alongside Christian Pulisic’s role at Milan. It described both Americans as having endured injury-disrupted starts, with fitness concerns now largely behind them and their influence expected to grow as the competition for Serie A and European places intensifies.

That framing matters for a Juventus side whose choices can change with the availability of its squad and the demands of a particular match. Football Italia highlighted McKennie’s versatility and tactical intelligence, presenting his capacity to adapt as central to the discussion rather than identifying one settled role as the source of his value.

The phrase “McKennie zone” should therefore be treated as a shorthand for that flexibility, not as a precisely defined patch of the pitch. The supplied Football Italia account does not specify the zone’s exact limits or provide a complete list of positions, so it would be premature to present a detailed positional map as the show’s conclusion.

Separate reporting by Gazzetta dello Sport offers a more concrete picture of how Juventus have used that adaptability. It says McKennie can be deployed on either flank, as an attacking midfielder or in his more natural midfield role, and adds that he has also been used as a centre-forward in an emergency. That is useful context, but it is Gazzetta’s account rather than a precise definition supplied by Football Italia.

The distinction is important: a player moving between jobs does not necessarily have one new, permanent position. Juventus can change where McKennie starts or what he is asked to do according to the game, without making the “zone” a fixed tactical system.

Gazzetta reports that Juventus face seven matches in roughly 20 days, beginning away to Cagliari on Sunday 11 October and running through the home match against Napoli on Sunday 1 November. The sequence also includes Celta Vigo, Lazio, Rennes, Lecce and Genoa, creating a demanding spell in which rotation and cover across the pitch could matter.

The same report describes a squad affected by injuries and says McKennie had initially been limited by muscular fatigue before being brought back into the picture. Football Italia’s account independently notes that injuries disrupted his and Pulisic’s early-season rhythm. It does not give a match-by-match return plan, but it does expect both players to have a greater influence as the campaign gathers pace.

For Juventus, the value of a player who can cover different duties is especially apparent when absences restrict the coach’s choices. McKennie’s role may give the staff room to adjust the balance of a side or cover a gap without making a substitution solely to replace one position. His role in Juventus’ setup is consequently best understood through the tasks he can perform, rather than a label alone.

That flexibility is not, by itself, proof that every deployment will work equally well or that he will be asked to perform the same job in every fixture. A versatile player can be a solution to squad pressure, a deliberate tactical choice, or both; the available Football Italia material does not establish a permanent plan for McKennie after the break.

Football Italia’s strongest point is that McKennie could become more significant for Juventus in the months ahead because he can adapt to different roles. Its analysis links that value to versatility and tactical intelligence, against the backdrop of an injury-affected start and a more intense phase of the season.

So the “McKennie zone” should not be read as evidence that Juventus have assigned him one clearly bounded area or settled position. Its significance lies in the choices his range gives the team: McKennie can be considered for different jobs as the squad’s needs and the match change. That, rather than a single coordinate on the pitch, is what makes his role worth watching when Serie A returns.

#1914. Broken promises and nobody taking responsibility: Erik ten Hag is right - Man United are addicted to going around in circles, writes NATHAN SALT, and Michael Carrick is learning his bosses don't have a clear plan

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Published: 16:00 BST, 20 October 2026 | Updated: 11:09 BST, 21 October 2026

Michael Carrick wore a smile on his face as the rain came down at Carrington with Manchester United putting the final touches to preparations ahead of flying to Italy to face Como.

A brave face? Perhaps. Only hours earlier a bombshell interview with former manager Erik ten Hag dropped in which he took multiple swipes at Ineos and the club’s ownership, as well as accusing the club of enabling a toxic culture in the dressing room to exist to this day.

‘They are continuing the toxic culture; they fuel it, they give the power to the players,’ Ten Hag claimed in a sit-down with the Athletic.

He, too, took a swipe at those in the boardroom for both a lack of ideas and a lack of truth behind their promises.

‘They promised me loyalty, they promised me backing, they promised communication, that we get aligned,’ Ten Hag said of Ineos. ‘We spoke about the issues. But the promises, they didn’t keep.’

So, when Carrick touches down in Milan, and makes the trip over to Como for tonight’s press conference, all involved can be forgiven for feeling an overwhelming sense of deja vu. That sense that, no matter how many players they change, how many managers they change, how many executives come and go, this is a club that’s seemingly addicted with going round in circles.

Michael Carrick pictured grinning in Man United training - but it's not all smiles at the club

Former Red Devils boss Erik ten Hag (right) was the latest person to hit out at the club on Tuesday, one week on from Lisandro Martinez's (left) tirade in Madrid

That sense, too, that a lack of accountability is infectious at the club from top to bottom. Players have not played to their potential for years now, and yet like to point the finger elsewhere. Tactically, managers have left a lot to be desired. Those in positions of power continue to make one mistake after another.

Where toxicity has faded away from the dressing room – there is a genuine sense of camaraderie in this current first-team set-up and those close to players spoken to by Daily Mail Sport attest to that, putting aside just how poorly they so often perform – the finger-pointing and lack of accountability remains.

Blame went to Ten Hag, then it was Dan Ashworth, then it was Marcus Rashford’s fault, or Alejandro Garnacho, or Ruben Amorim, and now it’s Carrick and Lisandro Martinez front and centre in the firing line.

Dig deeper though to what many of these players, far from blameless in the club’s demise, have said and much of it still chimes as true. That’s the biggest indictment of all against Manchester United.

For every Martinez interview, in which he explicitly called out director of football Jason Wilcox and the club’s ownership in light of a 2-0 defeat to Atletico Madrid last week, there’s Cristiano Ronaldo, or Raphael Varane, or before those two, Dean Henderson.

‘Not only the manager, but the other two or three guys who are around the club [at the senior executive level]. I felt betrayed,’ Ronaldo said in a now-infamous sit-down with Piers Morgan which led to his contract at United being ripped up.

‘I don't care, people should listen to the truth. Yes, I felt betrayed.

‘The progress was zero. Since Sir Alex [Ferguson] left, I saw no evolution in the club. Nothing had changed.’

Then there was Varane, a player indirectly called out by Ten Hag in his latest interview in what he referred to as the ‘Madrid connection’ which upset the balance of power in the dressing room.

‘I think that’s what the Madrid connection brought in, that players are powerful and they want to decide,’ Ten Hag alluded to.

Ten Hag also blasted Cristiano Ronaldo, who himself attacked the club's hierarchy when he left

Jason Wilcox was singled out by Martinez, accused of lacking ambition in the transfer market

By the time Varane departed, he spoke clearly and concisely on the issues that stop United moving back to the very top. To this day it remains a damning, and yet accurate, assessment of the state of the club.

‘Even from the inside as a player, there wasn't a clear structure on how things were going to work,’ Varane said last year.

‘There hasn't been a fixed methodology for years. At Madrid they have a defined way of doing things at every level of the club.

‘At Manchester it was not clear or defined enough to know how to recruit, how to play, how to communicate.’

And Henderson, back in 2022, let rip when he said: ‘To sit there and waste 12 months, it is criminal really.

‘At my age, I was fuming. I told the hierarchy that I need to be playing football, I don't want to be here and play second fiddle, it was planned out. I was almost gone before the manager (Ten Hag) came through the door. I have not spoken to him since.’

Take all of those comments in isolation and most would still apply today. Where is the structure? The methodology? The game model? The communication? The plan?

Now it’s Carrick who is the manager trying to pull a rabbit out of the hat where Ten Hag and his successor Ruben Amorim ultimately failed.

This is not to say that revisionism around Ten Hag should go into overdrive. An abnormally close relationship between the SEG player agency and United’s recruitment set the club back years with signings made, such as Antony and Rasmus Hojlund.

Results and performances, too, by the end were not good enough, no matter how much he pushes back on the decision and timing to move him on.

But when he outlines broken promises, poor communication lines, and being let down, few will say he’s not speaking the gospel.

‘The leadership, you need their competence, you need the right strategy,’ Ten Hag added.

‘What is your identity? What are the work methods? You need the right structures and quality people. Then you need time.’

Manchester United are six matches without a win ahead of Wednesday's trip to Como in Italy

Carrick needs time; Ten Hag himself said as such. But United as a club, with football decisions dictated by Ineos, have had plenty of time and yet find themselves in the same place as they were when they flew to Ibiza cap in hand to convince Ten Hag that, despite flirting with managers over the summer, they did in fact want him to stay.

Holes in United’s squad were obvious then and are obvious now with a defensive crisis underscoring a trip to Como that needs to yield a victory after six games without a win and just two wins from the last ten.

The methodology flipped from Ten Hag’s bid to become a dominant, expansive side, to Amorim’s 3-4-2-1 press-heavy group. Now it’s back to a possession-based approach with Carrick at the helm, and it was a back five in the style of Amorim that squeaked out a draw at Leeds.

‘You can't keep changing, changing, changing the manager,’ Martínez said post-Atletico. ‘What about other areas? [looks up] I can't say more.

‘Tonight it is easy to say the coach, easy to say the players but also we have to see other areas [points up].’

Carrick will face all of it when he sits in front of the microphones in Como but the short and simple answer is that United haven’t had a clear plan for years. They may not be as toxic as they were under Ten Hag’s watch but there’s no denying that United are a club that continue to go around in circles.

As Harry Kane closes in on some of football’s most prestigious accolades, the man who gave him his big Premier League break fears Tottenham have failed their next generation.

Kane’s success, competing for the Ballon d’Or and becoming England’s most-capped player, reflects well upon Spurs, the club where he came through the youth ranks and scored a club-record 280 goals before leaving for Bayern Munich three years ago.

At least, it does to a point. For Tim Sherwood, the brilliant rise of Kane serves to highlight how English clubs can produce more world-class talent if they are prepared to show more faith.

‘The academy side of that club is unrecognisable now,’ Sherwood, 57, tells Daily Mail Sport. ‘They might as well close it up and batten down the doors.

‘You can’t tell me there’s not been one player through that academy who has not been good enough to play for Tottenham on a regular basis since Harry Kane.

‘Either they have let whoever has been the manager close them off or they have not been banging down the door like we used to do. They have failed them.’

Kane scored his first Premier League start for Tottenham in 2014 against Sunderland

Tim Sherwood, who gave Kane his break, fears Tottenham are failing to give chances to the next generation

It was Sherwood who thrust him into the team for his first Premier League start against Sunderland aged 20, the first of six successive starts at the end of the season.

‘I didn’t do it because I was on an ego trip trying to put in as many academy boys as possible,’ says Sherwood.

I know how vital it is for someone to trust you and give you an opportunity.’

When Mauricio Pochettino replaced Sherwood, Kane knew what was required to score in the Premier League. But amid seven different managers in seven years since Pochettino, such opportunities have vanished. The only academy graduate to play in the Premier League for Spurs this season is Mikey Moore, now on loan at Cologne in Germany.

‘I know how important it is for the Tottenham manager to get results,’ says Sherwood. ‘They tend not to look at the academy. Or they’re waiting for a Lionel Messi to fall from the tree, but it doesn’t happen like that.

He was never afraid to ask questions if he didn’t understand something and once he understood, he did whatever he could to improve.

‘Just because he didn’t score bundles of goals everywhere he went doesn’t mean he failed. It was just about progression, and it was far better for his development than Mickey Mouse U21s football where no one cares who wins or loses.

‘We weren’t normal in the way we fought for those kids. I have enormous pride in the work put in by me, Chris and Les and everyone else who helped develop Harry Kane.’

Spurs have struggled for goals since Kane and in the summer they sold 21-year-old Will Lankshear to Middlesbrough, where he has scored 11 in 14 games.

‘There are clear parallels,’ says Sherwood. ‘I knew Harry and the other boys like they were my own. I knew how their hearts would beat and how to work them. I don’t know Will Lankshear but if I’d worked with him, I would know him and know if I felt he had the drive and determination and ability to play for Tottenham. I would not have let them sell him for £10m.

‘Put an arm around him and give him a chance. Tell him, “Don’t worry what the crowd are thinking, you’ve got the opportunity and if you do well they’re going to love you”.

‘Big him up. Tell him he can be the next Harry Kane. There’s no reason he can’t be. What are you selling Will Lankshear for when you’ve got strikers who can’t score a goal? Troy Parrott is another I would not have let go.’

Kane, meanwhile, forges on towards his dreams. ‘If he can win the Champions League or the Ballon d’Or I know what he will say,’ says Sherwood. ‘And it won’t be a stock answer, it will be a genuine answer. The genuine answer is he would rather win the Champions League with Bayern Munich than win the Ballon d’Or for himself.

‘That’s the mentality we’re talking about. That’s why he’s the best. That’s why I’ll have a lot of respect for the Ballon d’Or if he wins and I won’t if he doesn’t.

‘Kane is a total team man. That’s why he’s a great example to any kid coming through but those kids will not get the same opportunities unless there are people inside these academies fighting for them.’
