#938. Dwight Yorke: ‘I’m not a kid trying to get into management – I’m a manager’

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Ghana may soon come calling for Trinidad and Tobago’s former captain and manager, who is proud of his CV since his days as a Premier League striker

Dwight Yorke wants you to stop calling him a former player. “I’m not a kid trying to get into management for the first time,” he says from his home in Dubai. “I’ve done that and I want that message to come across clearly.”

The message has a deadline. Carlos Queiroz left his job as Ghana head coach on 1 October and the Ghana Football Association is searching for his successor. Yorke is among the names under consideration, along with Pape Thiaw, Aliou Cissé and Walid Regragui, the former Morocco head coach, according to Ghanaian media.

He is tired of the shorthand way he is described during these high‑profile job hunts. People focus on his playing accolades, including calling him the treble winner, a label he says belongs to one season in a run of three Premier League titles at Manchester United from 1999-2001. “I’m a manager,” he says.

Over the past four years, Yorke has quietly built a coaching résumé far removed from Old Trafford. His first taste of management came in May 2022, taking charge of Australia’s A-League All Stars against Barcelona in front of more than 70,000 people in Sydney. They lost 3-2. “I realised then that I enjoyed it,” he says.

Macarthur, based in Sydney, followed that July. “We went to a relatively unknown team in the A‑League and managed to win the cup.” The Australia Cup success, three months into his spell, was the club’s first trophy.

It did not last. Macarthur sacked him in January 2023, with the club sixth, after a post-match address when he reportedly called the club a “pub team” run by “muppets”.

A Fifa chamber found Macarthur had dismissed him without just cause. The court of arbitration for sport upheld that in 2024 and ordered the club to pay AU$212,500 (£111,300 at current exchange rates).

“I wish I’d had the opportunity to finish what I started,” Yorke says. The job taught him communication and the importance of being transparent. “There was a disagreement. It was resolved amicably and I’ve moved on.”

Between Macarthur and his next role, he kept his hand in. He worked as a pundit for beIN Sports and on the Saudi Pro League and travelled the world watching coaches at work.

In November 2024, Trinidad and Tobago came calling. The Soca Warriors finished third in their World Cup qualifying group behind Curaçao and Jamaica, missing out on the finals tournament, and Yorke’s tenure ended soon after. The Trinidad and Tobago Football Association said talks over revised terms had not produced an agreement and the contract was ended by mutual consent, announced in March. “The decision to end things was out of my control,” Yorke says.

He does not accept that missing the World Cup defines his time in charge. “People can be blind to success because they focus on whether you qualified.”

He lost once in six qualifiers, 2-0 in Jamaica in September 2025. He had been handed a brief: make people care about Trinidad and Tobago again, boost recruitment and qualify. “I was given three tasks and completed two,” he says.

With Yorke as the head coach, crowds came back and a number of US-born players with Trinidadian and Tobagonian heritage, such as the CF Montréal winger Dante Sealy and Real Salt Lake’s Kobi Henry, opted in and made their debuts. Yorke gave a new generation a fighting chance to emulate the team he captained to the finals in 2006. “I left Trinidadian football in a far better place than I found it,” he says.

The TTFA president at the time described Yorke’s mandate as qualification alone. The team were ranked 102nd by Fifa when Yorke was hired, behind Curaçao (91st) and Jamaica (62nd). “I miss management,” Yorke says. “I miss what we were creating.”

The players are understood to want him back because his successor, Derek King, is struggling with two draws and eight defeats from his 10 games. Ghana, though, is the live opportunity. “It’s a job I’d be interested in, given Ghana’s history and the players available.”

The Ghanaian FA has not confirmed any approach. The team are bottom of their 2027 Africa Cup of Nations qualifying group with no points, after a 2-0 defeat in Côte d’Ivoire and a 4-2 home loss against the Gambia in which they led by two goals. It is a sharp decline for a side that reached the last 32 of the World Cup this summer, losing 1-0 against Colombia.

“You look at the squad and your eyes open even wider,” Yorke says. He says an agent called to tell him Ghana were interested. “The conversation started there and then reached the media before I really knew it.”

From the outside, he says, Ghana “sometimes looks disjointed”. The GFA has said its women’s head coach, Kim Björkegren, will take temporary charge of the men’s qualifying double-header against Somalia next month. In Yorke’s view, outside noise unbalances things, from the administration to the players. So how would he fix it? Organisation, transparency and everyone speaking the same language. “The talent is there. Build the culture and get the players to buy in and the football will follow.”

He has considered the effect that diaspora players could have in the role, pointing to Ghana’s Antoine Semenyo and Brandon Thomas-Asante as proof that countries can benefit immensely from UK-born talent. It reflects a broader trend: at the World Cup, 23.6% of the 1,248 squad players represented countries outside their place of birth. For Yorke, the approach is simple: identify the players, establish their heritage and quality and sell them a vision. “I’d make sure that what I say is what I do.”

Older managers might have screamed and shouted to get messages across, but he says: “I’m not sure that approach can last in the current environment.” He would not berate a player in public. “That can be done behind closed doors, while still being assertive.” Respect and punctuality are non‑negotiable. He has sat in dressing rooms with big egos and says nobody gets special treatment. “Everyone starts equally.”

He borrows, but he does not copy. Sir Alex Ferguson, who managed him at United, is the shadow over any such comparison. Among contemporary managers, Pep Guardiola offered him the best advice. “He said, in effect, that I can’t be him or any other manager. I have to be myself.”

Other labels have stuck anyway. “People still call me a ‘party boy’. That stigma has been overused and has followed me since I retired from football. I’m not saying I didn’t enjoy myself as a player.” But he says: “You never hear that I didn’t turn up for training or wasn’t fit.”

In Dubai, he tends to fast for nearly 20 hours a day and eats one meal. “I’m a fitness fanatic who trains every day.”

Ask Yorke how he wants to be remembered as a coach and he sits up and answers with a list. Committed. Transparent with players. Decisions made for the team, never for an individual. “I’m straightforward. I don’t mess around, take shortcuts or do the job halfway.”

Yorke has the interest and, by his own account, the answers. “If anyone doubts me, I can prove them wrong.”

#935. How good is Birdbuddys species ID feature? I put it to the test with my exotic parrot.

Birdbuddy claims the AI model powering its species identification feature can recognize about 11,000 different types of birds, or "nearly every bird species in the world." As I was setting up its two new smart feeders for testing, I wondered: Does that include Melon, my Alexandrine parakeet?

Not to sound like a total bird hipster, but you've probably never heard of Alexandrines, an older and lesser-known species of chatty green parrots native to southern and southeastern Asia. They're not available at chain pet stores and are very often confused with Indian ringneck parakeets, their much more popular relatives with smaller beaks, shorter tails, and no crimson wing patches. (There are a lot of ringnecks on TikTok — see Hamlet of "what doing???" fame.)

Needless to say, I figured Melon would give Birdbuddy's AI a real challenge.

Disappointingly, but not fully unexpectedly, Birdbuddy guessed wrong. Right after I got Melon in view of the next-gen Birdbuddy Home, the Birdbuddy app sent me a notification saying that I was just visited by a "rose-ringed parakeet" — another name for an Indian ringneck. I submitted feedback within the app to let Birdbuddy know that its guess was close but ultimately incorrect.

The good news is that most people won't have exotic parrots visiting their house on a regular basis. I'm hoping Birdbuddy will have an easier time recognizing my native wild birds; the Home is currently mounted in the front yard of my Chicago apartment, if someone could kindly alert the area pigeons.

#931. 2026 Nobel Prize winner Anne Carson: Where to start reading

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

The works of this year's Nobel Prize-winning poet and essayist Anne Carson can feel slightly daunting. Here are some keys to understanding her universe.

This quote is from the long, lyrical poem "The Glass Essay" published in Anne Carson's 1995 collection "Glass, Irony and God" — an entry point into the works of the 2026 Nobel Prize in literature laureate.

In the poem, the narrator explores the burden of memory and obsessive desire after a failed love affair, all while regularly reflecting on Emily Brontë — an author renowned for her intense literary depiction of destructive and passionate love in "Wuthering Heights."

The poem brings several of Carson's recurring obsessions together into one work. She has repeatedly written about desire, abandonment and jealousy, and has often used other literary figures as emotional doubles, as historical anchor points to her ideas.

In a way, "The Glass Essay" encapsulates Carson's method; the Canadian poet, essayist, translator and classicist has often intellectualized emotional themes by intertwining her personal reflections with analysis, etymology, translation, literary history and philosophical thought.

Another trademark of her work is the genre fluidity found in "The Glass Essay," which — as the title indicates — is partly a literary criticism essay, even though it is also a poem due to its form.

"Almost all her literary production has centered upon the intersection between the philologist's obsession with words and the poet's desire for new meaning," noted Anders Olsson, chair of the Nobel Committee, in his description of the Nobel laureate on October 8.

Born in the Canadian city of Toronto, Carson first developed her fascination for ancient Greek myths as a teenager. She told literary journal The Brick that she spotted a bilingual edition of "Sappho" by Willis Barnstone in the display of a bookstore of a shopping mall in Ontario, and that had led her to ask a high school Latin teacher to teach her ancient Greek.

She went on to publish her doctoral thesis in Classics, entitled "Odi et Amo Ergo Sum" (I hate and I love, therefore I am).

Carson's first book of criticism, "Eros the Bittersweet," was published in 1986. It lays the foundation of her approach, as she draws on ancient Greek writers such as Sappho and Plato to examine how desire — and the obstacles between the lover and the beloved — creates emotional tension.

The book suggests that the ache of wanting is not separate from love; it is one of the things that makes desire so powerful.

Because "Eros the Bittersweet" moves between Greek poetry, philosophy and literary criticism and personal interpretations, it is not considered a conventionally easy read. A more immediately engaging work and good starting point to discover Carson is the verse novel "Autobiography of Red," published in 1998.

"Autobiography of Red" was inspired by the ancient Greek myth of Herakles (or Hercules) and the red-winged monster Geryon, which the hero kills as his 10th task in his series of 12 monumental labors.

An otherwise minor figure from mythology, Geryon is turned into Herakles' lover in Carson's book. She reimagines the myth as a modern, queer coming-of-age story, with the monster experiencing love, jealousy, sexuality and heartbreak.

"We all feel we're monsters most of the time," explained Carson.

The work is accessible, funny and moving, and no deep knowledge of Greek mythology is required to enjoy it.

The key to reading Carson is not to expect every connection to be immediately clear. Her books reward curiosity rather than certainty, and her sprawling, non-linear web of knowledge can be compared to going down a hyperlinked rabbit hole of narrative ideas.

"Men in the Off Hours" (2000) is another intellectually playful collection of poems. Here Carson collides the modern with the ancient, with references to figures as diverse as Beethoven, Virginia Woolf, Emily Dickinson, Jean-Luc Godard, Sigmund Freud, Homer and Hokusai.

The poems bring together fragments of history, mythology and ordinary life, allowing unexpected emotional and intellectual connections to emerge.

In the spoken and sung performance piece "Norma Jeane Baker of Troy" (2019), Carson similarly brings together classical and pop culture, reimagining the story of Helen of Troy alongside the life and cultural image of Marilyn Monroe.

How do people become the victims of desires they cannot control? This is a core theme Carson explores in "The Beauty of the Husband: A Fictional Essay in 29 Tangos," published in 2001.

It features the Canadian author's wry humor in a powerful and moving way, with erotic, painful and heartbreaking scenes from a doomed marriage: a 30-year obsession with a pathological liar whose singular redeeming quality is his sheer physical radiance.

The collection of poetry, which won her the T.S. Eliot Prize, is dedicated to the English Romantic poet John Keats, challenging his famous aphorism, "Beauty is truth."

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Infantino needs to reach the magic number of 106 votes to secure re-election next March

Gianni Infantino is facing a battle to cling onto his Fifa presidency heading into next year’s election after his botched bid to sell off stakes in the World Cup to private investors resulted in a significant loss in support.

England and Wales have been among the football associations to join European governing body Uefa in leading the charge for Infantino’s removal from power, formally withdrawing their endorsement of the Swiss-Italian for re-election in March 2027.

But as the dust has settled on his privatisation ploy, Infantino’s bid for a third term has strengthened. His allies have surfaced, with the likes of Mexico and Argentina joining Caf, the African football governing body, in reaffirming support for the under-fire president, and now the Asian Football Confederation (AFC) has reversed its position and backed Infantino with AFC president Sheikh Salman bin Ibrahim Al Khalifa underlining that there is “a true understanding between us”.

Infantino needs 106 votes from the 211 member associations to be re-elected as president. Here is every country and confederation to have expressed their opposition or support for Infantino:

Uefa, the governing body for Europe, has staunchly opposed Infantino’s leadership. They threatened a boycott of Fifa competitions before Infantino’s eventual climbdown but has continued to assert that his position is untenable, even taking legal action against the Swiss-Italian.

The body points to how there have not been “assurances” that attempts like the privatisation of the World Cup “to disfigure the game in this way will never be made again”.

More recently, Uefa general secretary Theodore Theodoridis has written to his Fifa counterpart Mattias Grafstrom setting out a series of questions on behalf of European members of the Fifa Council, demanding documents covering a series of Infantino controversies. Fifa has until 2 November to answer these questions.

Concacaf, the Confederation for North America and the Caribbean, came out in opposition of Infantino after being left stunned at the lack of consultation during the Fifa sell-off plan, which they went on to reject.

After the plan was aborted, the confederation said: “This recent unilateral and egregious act of poor governance and leadership follows a pattern of missteps and similar behaviour. A comprehensive reckoning with this presidency is imperative.”

Concacaf president Victor Montagliani was initially seen as a front-runner to challenge Infantino in next March’s election but he now looks set to pursue another term as Concacaf chief.

The English FA is one of many European member associations to send a letter to Fifa withdrawing its support for Infantino’s re-election in 2027.

Infantino’s apology letter that was sent around on Wednesday night changed nothing for the FA, who was “deeply concerned about the lack of process and governance” surrounding his sell-off scheme.

The Football Association of Wales was one of the first to send its letter withdrawing support for Infantino.

“The ‌recent failures in good governance, processes, ‌leadership, values, stakeholder management, communications and sound judgment ​have led us to a position ‌where Mr Infantino has lost the confidence of the FAW to ‌remain at the helm of world football,” a statement read. “Failing to put the best interests of football first is a failure we cannot accept.”

The SFA’s chief executive Ian Maxwell told the BBC that the governing body’s position has “been clear” since Uefa’s collective statement.

“We have aligned ourselves with the Uefa position and 55 nations are stronger than one voice.

“Their statement since then has talked about a lack of confidence in the Fifa leadership and the Scottish FA agree completely with that decision.

“The SFA will not be voting for Gianni Infantino come the re-election in early 2027.”

The Football Association of Ireland (FAI) has followed in withdrawing its support for Infantino while backing Uefa’s stance.

"Following an FAI board meeting, the decision was taken to rescind the letter of support that the Association supplied earlier this year," it confirmed. "It has since written to Fifa to explain its reasoning. The Association is grateful for the support that Fifa provides to Irish football, but recent events have raised significant concerns regarding governance, transparency and the lack of meaningful consultation.”

The Italian Football Federation (FIGC) took a few weeks to take official action but it has now withdrawn its support for Infantino's candidacy in the next Fifa presidential election scheduled.

"This decision is intended to clearly and transparently express the FIGC's position - in full agreement with Uefa and its president Aleksander Ceferin - regarding the recent initiatives promoted by the FIFA president concerning the governance and development of international competitions," FIGC said in a statement.

Finland kickstarted this process of formal withdrawals, with it being revealed that the Scandinavian member association was the first to send a letter officially withdrawing support for Infantino.

The Football Association of Serbia then sent a similar letter to Fifa.

“We would like to remind you that we provided our support to Mr Infantino in writing on 25 May of this year, but after carefully considering the events that have seriously damaged the image and reputation of both Fifa and its president in the past period, this is the only logical and rational course of action,” a statement read.

Sweden’s federation (SvFF) followed suit: "At an extraordinary board meeting on Monday, SvFF has chosen not to support Fifa President Gianni Infantino ​in the upcoming re-election.

Here's what stood out to me as I unboxed them and set them up in the front yard of my Chicago apartment.

The Nook is a small, minimalist hopper feeder that's ideal for tight spaces like balconies and patios. Its modular design comes in a cute sage green finish.

The Nook is much more affordable than the Birdbuddy Home, but it requires more maintenance. It can only fit about three and a half cups of birdseed, so it needs more frequent top-ups. Thirdly, the USB-C port where its roof plugs into its battery is located right in the middle of its reservoir, and it's a pain to clean tiny seeds and dust out of it.

The Home is an all-in-one hopper feeder with two built-in solar panels, dual-band WiFi for a more reliable connection, and a taller reservoir that can fit around five and a half cups of seeds. A bright red-orange perch accents its slate blue finish.

Between the Nook Solar and the Home, I prefer the latter. It has a hatch in the back that makes seed refills easier, whereas you have to remove the entire top of the Nook to access its reservoir. Additionally, the Home has two solar panels on either side of its roof, so it's more likely to get ample sun exposure. The Nook's roof attachment only has a solar panel on one side.

The Home also comes in an Extended Edition ($249.99) with a bigger perch and a Family Premium membership.

It's faster at pairing with the Birdbuddy app (I can attest to this — it only takes a few seconds)

It has a wider field of view (135 degrees versus 122 degrees)

It "wakes up" faster once the feeders' motion detectors sense a bird

There's no longer a delay on its livestream

#922. Finland, the world's happiest nation, faces a glum winter

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Finns have many reasons to feel content, but the country's pile of debt is not one of them. Russia's war in Ukraine has added to the fiscal burden. Can the Nordic nation absorb another shock?

There's an uneasy mood in Finland, the country that's ranked the world's happiest for nine consecutive years. A colder-than-usual winter is on the way and so is the toughest austerity budget in years.

With an election due in April, the argument is no longer how much to cut, but which services, benefits and pensions will face the biggest cuts.

Finland's debt and deficit levels are in their worst state since the 1990s, when a banking crash and the collapse of the Soviet Union — a key export partner — pushed the Nordic nation into its own Great Depression.

Last month, Finland's national statistical institution confirmed that the national debt had reached 90.3% of gross domestic product (GDP) in the second quarter. Before the pandemic, that figure was around 65%.

Trouble to the east is, again, part of the reason. Finland ramped up defense spending after joining NATO a year after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine. Helsinki, which used to rely on Moscow for about a third of its energy, also faced much higher costs as it transitioned to other suppliers.

The Finnish government has spent more than it earns since the 2008/9 global financial crisis, partly due to an aging population and sluggish economic growth. Last month, the State Treasury projected the fiscal deficit would reach 4.2% of GDP in 2026.

Brussels has piled pressure on Helsinki to bring down the borrowing gap, as European Union rules require member states to stay below 3% of GDP. In January, the European Council opened an excessive deficit procedure and gave Finland until the end of 2028 to reach the desired target.

Prime Minister Petteri Orpo's government, which took office in June 2023, said it would aim to save about €9 billion ($10.1 billion) during this parliamentary term. But whoever wins the next election will have to cut much deeper, economists warn.

"The amounts currently estimated range from €8 to €11 billion," Jarkko Kivisto, an advisor to the Bank of Finland's monetary policy and research department, told DW.

Earlier this year, all but one political party agreed to support a debt brake, where the next government commits to tightening the country's deficit to about 2% of GDP by 2031.

To date, Orpo's center-right National Coalition is the only party to promise a further €9 billion in cuts if he wins a second term. As well as further savings from health, social care and welfare, Orpo also plans to target workplace pensions, without reducing current payouts.

While the Social Democrats plan to split the reforms between spending cuts and tax hikes, Orpo plans to achieve his target without raising taxes — meaning public services will bear the brunt.

Kivisto, however, cautioned: "The deficit is so large that it would need a package that includes tax increases and expenditure cuts."

While Finland's at-risk-of-poverty rate is lower than the EU average, unemployment is higher than anywhere else in the bloc. In August, Finland's jobless total stood at 10.3%, just above Spain's 10%, according to Eurostat, the bloc's statistics agency. Youth unemployment reached 23.3%, against an EU average of 15.4%.

With household spending already falling, Lauri Holappa, director of the Finnish Centre for New Economic Analysis, warned that a new round of austerity would be a hard blow for the domestic economy.

"More than a quarter of the population works in the public sector, so if those workers face the threat of being sacked, the savings rate will rise and private spending will drop again," Holappa told DW.

Successive pressures ranging from the COVID-19 pandemic, higher interest rates and the global financial impact of two current wars have hurt efforts to cut the growing gap between income and borrowing. The 2027 budget proposal still counts on a spending gap of €12.4 billion.

Before the Ukraine war, Finland had already committed to buying 64 F-35A fighter jets from the United States, in a deal worth about €8.4 billion.

During more than four years of Russia's war in Ukraine, the Nordic nation raised military spending from $4.5 billion to over $8 billion per year, according to the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI).

In April, the government said it would further increase defense spending to 3.2% of GDP, close to NATO's 3.5% target.

With oil prices remaining close to $100 a barrel due to the Iran war, the government has so far resisted pressure to increase fuel subsidies. That's despite the Bank of Finland recording a 50% rise in the cost of imported energy in the early months of the Iran war.

While Finland’s shift to renewables, along with the opening of the Olkiluoto 3 nuclear power plant in 2023, has helped avoid a worse energy crisis, the central bank warned this summer that sustained high oil and gas prices would mean "lower economic growth and higher inflation" next year.

On another positive note, parts of Finland's export sector are booming, thanks to strong metal and shipbuilding orders, which Holappa described as strong as "the boom years of Nokia," when the once market-leading phone maker carried Finnish economic growth.

"There is a possibility that the export sector can steer us out of this recession," he told DW, as long as demand from the rest of Europe remains strong.

Despite the multiple roadblocks to bringing down the deficit, market reaction to Finland's debt woes has, so far, been muted. Rating agencies have kept the country's outlook stable, apart from S&P, which turned negative in April without cutting the rating.

On October 6, the premium the Finnish government pays over Germany to borrow for 10 years was 38 basis points. That compares to 128 for France and 107 for Italy. In other words, investors still treat Finnish debt as among the safest in Europe.

That is, as long as the next shock is not another extended oil-price spike, a fresh escalation from Russia, or a Europe-wide debt crisis centered on France.

Next March, Finland could still be named the planet's happiest nation for the 10th year in a row. How long the smiles last, once the bill for decades of borrowing arrives a month later, is anyone's guess.

Charles Leclerc showed early promise in an upgraded Ferrari car to pip Red Bull's in-form Max Verstappen to the fastest time of first practice at the United States Grand Prix.

Ferrari, McLaren and Red Bull all introduced fresh revisions to their cars for the opening hour of running at the Circuit of the Americas, which is staging the first leg of the sport's second of three end-of-season triple headers.

Ferrari's package features changes to their car's floor, sidepod and diffuser and showed speed on both the medium and soft tyres around the undulating COTA lap, with Leclerc ultimately setting the session's pace on the latter with a best lap of 1:36.052.

Verstappen, the winner of the last two races, finished 0.084s behind Leclerc although lost a quicker lap on the softs which was two tenths faster than the Ferrari due to a track limits violation at Turn 19.

Lando Norris was third in a McLaren car which also features changes as they bid to return to form after a difficult run of races saw them slip into the clutches of Red Bull in the Constructors' Championship battle for third.

World championship leaders Mercedes do not have fresh upgrades in Austin. But both of their drivers are back to running the package first introduced two races ago in Malaysia after George Russell reverted to the older-spec last time out in Singapore.

Russell finished fourth, 0.326s behind, in opening practice with title-leading team-mate Kimi Antonelli fifth on a weekend the Italian could clinch 2026's world title if he wins on Sunday and his team-mate and Lewis Hamilton do not score sufficient points to stay in mathematical contention.

Hamilton was only sixth fastest in Friday's first session, half a second away from team-mate Leclerc, after his soft-tyre run did not come together. Oscar Piastri was seventh in the second McLaren.

Sky Sports F1's Martin Brundle said: "It does look so close at the front.

"McLaren look in there too, Mercedes found some time. Ferrari you'd have to say just had an ounce of an advantage but it is early doors."

Audi's Gabriel Bortoleto was the top midfield runner with the eighth-fastest time although he faces a back-of-the-grid start in Sunday's race after taking two engine elements beyond the season's penalty-free allowance.

Arvid Lindblad was ninth for Racing Bulls in a session the team introduced a sixth internal combustion engine into his car, triggering a five-place grid penalty for the race. Liam Lawson was 10th quickest in the second Racing Bulls.

Williams finished 19th with Carlos Sainz and 22nd with Alex Albon, who did not set a lap time, as the team opted to spend the session running an aerodynamic testing programme to aid car development for this and next year.

Second practice follows on Friday night at 10pm, live on Sky Sports F1 and Sky Sports Main Event.
