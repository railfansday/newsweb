#1057. Paramount and Warner Bros merge into one Hollywood giant

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Paramount has completed an $81 billion purchase of Warner Bros. Discovery, as its owner Skydance creates a new entertainment behemoth. Billionaire David Ellison's hostile takeover bid defeated rival efforts from Netflix.

Paramount on Tuesday finalized its takeover of Warner Bros. Discovery, completing a hostile acquisition that was months in the making and almost faltered at several hurdles.

The takeover cost a total of around $81 billion (roughly €72 billion), or almost $111 billion including the Warner Bros. debts passed on as part of the deal.

The merger brings together two of the oldest US filmmaking studios and will form a new giant called Skydance Corporation.

The new name Skydance hails from billionaire investor David Ellison, who first bought out Paramount and its subsidiaries and then moved on to target the much larger fish that is Warner Bros.

The bid faced stiff competition in a bidding war with Netflix, and later scrutiny from US regulators and politicians, but ultimately went through.

"Today is a historic day, not just for Skydance but for our entire industry," said Ellison, whose family has ties to US President Donald Trump.

Skydance shares began trading on the New York Stock Exchange on Tuesday under the ticker SKYD, while Warner Bros. Discovery shareholders will receive about $31 per share.

The deal closed less than a week after a federal judge in California approved a settlement between Paramount and 12 US states that had sued to block the transaction on antitrust grounds.

As well as the two giant film studios, Paramount Pictures and Warner Bros. Pictures, the deal also brings together the CBS News and CNN news networks, and the HBO and CBS television brands. Ellison says he plans to merge the subscription streaming platforms Paramount+ and HBO Max.

Ellison will serve as chairman and CEO of the new company. Former Mattel boss Ynon Kreiz is operating as co-CEO in charge of day-to-day operations.

Skydance Corp. looks set to have a total revenue in the region of $70 billion, based on the performance to date of its component parts. It says it's targeting cost savings of around $6 billion within three years as it takes advantage of overlaps created by the merger.

#1055. 7 apps that look gorgeous on the iPhone Duos display

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Why would anyone want a foldable phone? That's easy: You want a pocketable phone that still has a display large enough to truly enjoy books, magazines, games, and perhaps do some work without squinting.

With the iPhone Duo (read our review here), Apple did an amazing job with the user interface, as well as optimizing its own apps to look great on the Duo. But it's up to third-party developers to bring their apps up to speed and create versions that play nice with the Duo's dual displays and all that screen estate on the inner display.

The news here is mixed: Many apps, even some very popular ones (we're looking at you, Google), still haven't been redesigned to look great on the Duo. But among those that have, there are a couple that really stand out in terms of design and creativity. Here's our list of the best iPhone Duo apps so far.

Two notes: I've skipped Apple's own apps. They look universally great, but it's to be expected, and everyone who gets the Duo gets all of them anyway. Second, some of my experience comes from beta versions of these apps that were shared with me in advance for the purpose of testing; your experience on finalized apps might be slightly different (hopefully even better).

One of the first apps to receive a complete Duo overhaul, Netflix is the one you'll show to friends on your new foldable iPhone. Not only does the larger inner display show a ton of content, but the app does different tricks depending on how you fold and position your iPhone Duo. Not all of it will be useful, but ultimately, video streaming apps are perfect companions for the Duo, and we're glad to see Netflix looking ahead here.

Instagram's video editing app was one of the first to get thoroughly optimized for the Duo, and it's the type of app that really takes advantage of the extra screen space. Video editing can quickly get complex, and handling a video project's minutiae on a tiny phone display simply isn't as good as doing it on the Duo's large inner screen.

As a side note, Instagram, too, has been revamped for the Duo, but given its native vertical bias, it looks a bit odd when you hold the Duo horizontally. It's not bad, but I think Instagram's designers will probably do some more tinkering to really make use of the extra screen real estate on the Duo.

Weather app makers took the Duo seriously. I've gotten numerous pitches from several app makers that have updated their apps to play nice with the Duo. The one that looked nicest to my eyes was RainViewer, whose updated iOS app is currently under review by Apple. The app offers an incredibly clean, beautiful weather map focusing on incoming precipitation.

Other weather apps that have updated their apps for the Duo include Carrot Weather and Mercury Weather.

The Deck-man mimics a walkman on the Duo. Yes, an actual, analog walkman, with cassette tapes that you virtually drag from a catalog on the left to the player on the right. The visual trick that Deck-man does is that the playback doesn't start until you've folded the iPhone Duo, as if you were closing an actual Walkman. This is way too annoying to actually use as a serious music player, but it's a nice novelty idea that you just cannot have on a normal, candybar phone. Your friends that were born in the 80s or earlier will probably be delighted with this one. Anyone younger will probably give you a puzzled stare.

This meditation app is an example of how you can create a very effective, Duo-friendly app with simple graphics and smart design choices. When I opened the app on the Duo for the first time, the smiling sun logo rising across the Duo's entire display brought a smile to my face.

Finalist is a beautiful daily planner/journal combo which has been thoroughly optimized for the Duo and looks absolutely beautiful in all orientations. This is where the fact that the Duo's inner display is matte really shines; spread out the phone on your desk with Finalist on, and it'll look like a wonderfully themed notebook holding all your tasks and meetings.

The iPhone Duo has a special side-by-side mode which allows you to have two apps opened next to each other in landscape mode, and it immediately makes even the most pedestrian apps so much better. On a video call with someone and need to check out the weather? Pull up the Weather app next to your video call, and you'll feel like a multitasking machine, handing out weather reports like a pro. Numerous apps like email and calendar work so well together that you can even opt to glue them together persistently.

The club's longest-serving first-team player who was purchased from another club is Portuguese centre-back and club captain Ruben Dias, who joined from Benfica in 2020.

Norway striker Erling Haaland is the next longest-serving arrival, joining from Borussia Dortmund in the summer of 2022.

Midfielder Phil Foden is the only player to have played for the club during the period in which they breached the league's financial rules, but was not a senior signing having come through the club's youth ranks.

Foden, 26, made his first-team debut in a Champions League match against Feyenoord in November 2017.

Like Foden, versatile midfielder Rico Lewis is also a product of the club's academy but did not make his senior debut until August 2022.

All of the club's other senior players were signed between 2023 and 2026.

It is a good time to play Crystal Palace. That, at least, is the crumb of comfort being nibbled at on Tyneside this week. The rather more uncomfortable truth is that it is also a good time to play Newcastle United.

Just two months in and, unfairly or otherwise, Matthias Jaissle’s future is being measured by some in days and weeks. Not by those inside St James’ Park, nor inside the PIF towers of Riyadh, and not by those on the outside who make allowance for the difficulty of the situation he inherited in August.

Newcastle sold their best players and replaced them with, to be brutally candid, inferior models. It is hard, of course, to upgrade on stars who leave for Barcelona and Arsenal, and what was billed as a brave new era has, in the short term, looked like a backwards stumble.

But Jaissle’s job, as is that of any manager, is to manage what he has, not lament what has been lost. At present, too many square pegs are being hammered into round holes in the hope that they will fit, which they have not. During Saturday’s 1-0 home defeat by Aston Villa, three players started in a position they would not consider their best.

Among them was £51million marquee signing Matias Fernandez-Pardo, who arrived on the promise of reinventing himself as a striker. After three starts without a goal, the 21-year-old was shifted to the wing, albeit the opposite flank to where he spent much of his time at previous club Lille.

Meanwhile, at No 10, in the absence of an actual No 10, midfielders and strikers continue to moonlight.

Jaissle’s ploy was criticised, but the sobering thought for Newcastle, a club who have spent £175m trying to replace Alexander Isak, is that the Hail Mary was their only prayer.

Mitigation for a head coach, however, only extends into so many matches. Another defeat at Selhurst Park – Newcastle have lost three of the last four – and the trauma of summer will be overtaken by the anguish of today. That means more focus on Jaissle and less on the deeper legacy issues as to why his side are struggling.

As opposite number Pierre Sage has found, when there is no easily reachable body of work to use as a shield against scrutiny, judgment can be quick and unforgiving.

Jaissle, at 38 years old and having landed from Saudi Arabia via a successful period with Red Bull Salzburg in Austria, is learning that harsh reality in the harshest of surrounds.

By any reasonable measure, the young German should be afforded time, patience and understanding. He is bright and, increasingly, likeable.

In the Premier League, however, what you deserve you don’t always get. Supporters, especially those amplified by football’s anger-rithm, demand immediate evidence of competence and progress. In other words, results.

Yet the concern when it comes to Jaissle and Newcastle, among those not governed by such outcome-driven emotion, is that the underlying numbers and observations of the eye are more troubling than a far-from-disastrous league position of 11th.

The manager is not blind to that reality, either. He is aware of his side's critical ball losses and unforced errors ranking too highly in the division. ‘Expected Points’, also, has them in the bottom three.

Jaissle has held meetings with his players over the past fortnight, the first in the wake of last week’s 4-1 defeat by Coventry, when issues over the implementation of his ‘vertical football’ philosophy were addressed. His 4-2-3-1 formation has consistently left them exposed in midfield.

Before that, following another 4-1 loss at Leeds last month, Jaissle determined that the poor performance was not a consequence of his tactical approach.

He felt helpless at Elland Road when his players failed to compete for first balls, never mind second, and were bullied into submission.

Working alongside a dozen analysts in a war room at the club’s training ground, he decided it was no time to abandon his strategy when basic application was more so to blame.

But now that effort is present – few could question Newcastle’s commitment against Villa – the spotlight returns to shape, game-plan and preparation. The basics of a manager, if you like.

That has been the source of debate this week. The doubters have likened the coach to Ruben Amorim or Ange Postecoglou, stubbornly wedded to a style that will likely lead to divorce. Jaissle believes otherwise but says he needs time.

‘We have for sure a lot of potential still,’ he said. ‘This is something I mentioned to some of the club’s decision-makers, that sometimes we still feel in pre-season when we train with the group. He looks at what countryman Hansi Flick is achieving at Camp Nou, with a 4-2-3-1 set-up, and he wants that energy and incision at Newcastle. His conviction is to be admired, but there is a fine line between sticking to your principles and being held hostage by them.

Astrocytes perform a diverse range of tasks that facilitate brain physiology, raising the possibility that these diverse astrocytic roles are enabled by functionally distinct subtypes1,2. Recent studies identified molecular diversity among astrocytes3; however, whether this molecular heterogeneity reflects specialized astrocyte subpopulations with distinct functions remains unclear. Here, on the basis of previous molecular and functional studies4, we generated a CD51-FLP mouse line that enables direct comparison of the functional properties between CD51+ and CD51− astrocyte subpopulations in neural circuits in vivo. We show that CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes have unique morphological, physiological and developmental properties in both the cortex and hippocampus. Functional studies demonstrate that CD51+ astrocytes selectively promote synapse formation in the developing cortex and circuit plasticity in the hippocampus. Mechanistically, we found that CD51+ astrocytes function through glypican 6 to promote synapse formation in the cortex, while CD51 itself drives circuit plasticity in the hippocampus. Applying these findings to brain injury, we found that CD51+ astrocytes promote functional recovery after stroke through enhanced synaptic remodelling. Taken together, our studies identify specialized astrocyte subpopulations that exhibit distinct functional properties in the developing, mature and injured brain.

Cellular diversity is an operational necessity of all organ systems, in which diverse cell types participate in a division of labour that orchestrates essential physiological activities. Astrocytes are the most abundant type of glial cell in the central nervous system (CNS) and have vital roles in all facets of brain physiology, including neurotransmission, synaptogenesis, circuit function, metabolic support and maintenance of the blood–brain barrier, along with responses to injury and degeneration1,5,6,7. Advances in single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) technologies have facilitated the identification of several prospective astrocyte subpopulations, suggesting that these diverse roles may be conducted by subsets of distinct astrocytes8. However, whether specialized astrocytes are capable of executing a subset of these diverse functions remains very poorly defined, as experimentally examining the extent to which molecular heterogeneity reflects functionally distinct subtypes of astrocytes in active neural circuits remains a major challenge. While several previous studies identified astrocyte populations that express unique markers and demonstrated roles for these specific genes in astrocytes in the context of neural circuits, whether these populations have unique functional properties remains unclear9,10. Unambiguous demonstration of functionally distinct astrocyte subpopulations at the cellular level requires a direct comparison of the functional properties between two populations in vivo. Critically, tools that enable selective labelling and manipulation of defined subsets of astrocytes, which would facilitate the direct comparison between different subpopulations of astrocytes, do not exist. Thus, whether functionally distinct subtypes of astrocytes exist in neural circuits has remained unresolved. Here we identify CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes as subpopulations with distinct properties and diverse functions in regulating neural circuits across postnatal cortical development, learning and memory, and brain repair.

Using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)-based approaches in the adult mouse brain, our previous studies identified CD51, the αV integrin subunit encoded by Itgav, as a cell surface marker capable of subdividing subsets of astrocytes with unique molecular properties4. Whether CD51-expressing astrocytes are endowed with unique properties in vivo in functioning neural circuits remains unclear. We therefore generated a mouse line that enables the selective targeting of CD51+ astrocytes in the mouse brain. To achieve this, we engineered a CD51-FLP BAC (bacterial artificial chromosome) mouse line and intercrossed the CD51-FLP founder line with Rosa-FSF-tdTomato mice (where FSF denotes FRT-stop-FRT), such that the reporter is expressed in the presence of FLP activity (CD51-FLP;Rosa-FSF-tdTomato) (Fig. 1a). Analysis of this mouse line revealed expression of CD51 protein in tdTomato-expressing cells, indicating successful targeting of this population (Extended Data Fig. 1c–e). tdTomato expression was also evident in a subset of endothelial cells, but rarely in other cells types (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). Importantly, tdTomato expression in astrocytes was highly concordant with immunoreactivity to an anti-CD51 antibody (Extended Data Figs. 1c–e and 2c,d). To assess the extent to which the CD51-FLP;Rosa-FSF-tdTomato line targets astrocytes in the brain of 8-week-old mice, we co-stained for the pan-astrocyte marker SOX9 and evaluated the constituency of CD51+ (tdTomato+SOX9+) and CD51− (tdTomato−SOX9+) astrocytes across a host of brain regions (Fig. 1b,c and Extended Data Fig. 1a,b). This analysis revealed the presence of CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes in varying proportions throughout the brain, with CD51+ astrocytes representing approximately 19% of hippocampal astrocytes, 13% of olfactory bulb astrocytes and 38% of cortical astrocytes (Fig. 1c). Notably, our finding that 19% of astrocytes in the hippocampus are CD51+ is consistent with publicly available scRNA-seq data from the adult mouse hippocampus11 (Extended Data Fig. 1f). These data indicate that the CD51-FLP mouse can distinguish CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes in the adult brain.

We next sought to assess the cellular properties of CD51+ and CD51− astrocyte populations in the cortex and hippocampus, focusing on cell morphology and Ca2+ activity. The CD51-FLP mouse line allows us to use the FLP-FRT system to conditionally express reporters using intersectional viral approaches in CD51+ or CD51− astrocytes12,13. Combining these FLP-based viral tools with the CD51-FLP mouse, we can label CD51+ and CD51− astrocyte subpopulations with tdTomato (Fig. 1d). Accordingly, we injected the cortex or hippocampus of 4-week-old CD51-FLP mice, collected mouse tissue at 8 weeks of age and assessed expression of the conditional tdTomato reporter. Using the tdTomato reporter to assess morphology, we found that CD51+ astrocytes in the cortex exhibit enhanced morphological complexity compared with CD51− astrocytes; by contrast, CD51+ astrocytes in the hippocampus exhibited comparatively decreased complexity (Fig. 1e,f). Using similar approaches, we generated conditional adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) that enabled us to selectively target CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes with GCaMP6, an optical sensor that reliably reports Ca2+ activity14 (Fig. 1g). Using the same workflow as described above, we used two-photon imaging to measure Ca2+ activity in ex vivo slices from the cortex or hippocampus and found that CD51+ astrocytes in the cortex exhibit enhanced spontaneous Ca2+ activity compared with CD51− astrocytes (Fig. 1h,i). By contrast, in the hippocampus, CD51− astrocytes exhibited elevated spontaneous Ca2+ activity compared with CD51+ astrocytes (Fig. 1j,k). Together, these studies indicate that CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes are endowed with unique morphological and physiological properties within and across the cortex and hippocampus.

The identification of unique properties for CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes in the adult brain led us to next examine whether they also exhibit unique developmental characteristics, such as proliferation and timelines of expansion. Using postnatal cortical development as a model, in conjunction with the CD51-FLP;Rosa-FSF-tdTomato mouse line, we evaluated the constituency of CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes during the postnatal day 1 (P1) to P28 developmental interval when astrocytogenesis occurs in the cortex15,16,17. This analysis revealed a steady increase in the proportion of CD51+ astrocytes between P1 and P21, with the relative number of tdTomato+SOX9+ astrocytes peaking at about 40% by P21 (Fig. 1l–n). This finding was conserved across individual cortical layers (Extended Data Fig. 2e). These observations suggest that CD51+ cells undergo a proliferative expansion during this interval. Given that around 50% of cortical astrocytes are generated by local proliferation outside the germinal zones and in the brain parenchyma between P1 and P21 (refs. 15,16,18), we next assessed the relative proliferation rates of these populations. Using EdU-labelling approaches (Fig. 1l), we found that CD51− astrocytes are highly proliferative at P1, which is followed by enhanced proliferation of CD51+ astrocytes at P7–21 (Fig. 1m–o). Immunostaining for the cell cycle markers Ki-67 and phosphorylated histone H3 yielded the same pattern of results (Extended Data Fig. 2f–k). These data are consistent with our observation of increased numbers of CD51+ astrocytes during this interval, indicating that CD51+ astrocytes proliferate and expand in the brain parenchyma during the P1–21 interval. By contrast, CD51− astrocytes appear to be predominantly generated earlier in development and exhibit less proliferative capacity during the later stages of the P1–21 interval.

We next sought to understand the origin of CD51+ astrocytes. Analysis of developing astrocyte RNA-seq data19 revealed that expression of Itgav emerged after birth in astrocyte lineage cells (Extended Data Fig. 3a). Concordantly, using CD51-FLP;Rosa-FSF-tdTomato mice, we found that SOX2+ precursors within the ventricular/subventricular zone did not express tdTomato (CD51) at the onset of cortical gliogenesis at embryonic day 16.5 (E16.5), but a subset was tdTomato positive at P1 (Extended Data Fig. 3c–e). These data suggest that CD51+ astrocytes are specified after the onset of gliogenesis. Notably, we identified binding motifs for the glial development transcription factor nuclear factor I-A (NFIA) in upstream regulatory regions of Itgav (Extended Data Fig. 3g), suggesting that NFIA could be involved in regulating CD51 expression and forming CD51+ astrocytes. To test this, we generated Aldh1l1-creER;NFIA-flox;Rosa-LSL-tdTomato mice, which enabled tamoxifen-inducible deletion of Nfia and expression of tdTomato in astrocytes (hereafter, Nfia conditional knockout (cKO); Extended Data Fig. 3h). Aldh1l1-creER;Rosa-LSL-tdTomato mice were used as controls. We injected pups with tamoxifen within 24 h of birth, when relatively few CD51+ astrocytes are present in the cortex (Fig. 1n). At P28, we found that Nfia-cKO mice had a significantly smaller proportion of CD51+ astrocytes in the cortex compared with the control mice (Extended Data Fig. 3h–l), indicating that NFIA regulates the development of CD51+ astrocytes.

Postnatal astrocyte development is regulated in part by extrinsic cues through neuronal-activity-dependent mechanisms20,21. To test whether neuronal activity affected the proportions of CD51+ or CD51− astrocytes, we used Gq-DREADD (designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs) stimulation of cortical excitatory neurons from P7–21 adapted from our previous study21 (Extended Data Fig. 3m–r). However, we found that this manipulation did not affect the proportions of astrocytes that were CD51+ or CD51−. Thus, the origin of the diverse CD51+ and CD51− astrocyte populations that we have identified appears to be predominantly dependent on cell-intrinsic genetic programs rather than neuronal activity.

Having found that CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes exhibit unique properties, we next examined whether these populations demonstrate distinct molecular profiles. To achieve this, we intercrossed the CD51-FLP;Rosa-FSF-tdTomato line with the Aldh1l1-GFP mouse line (Fig. 2a). This approach enabled us to FACS-isolate CD51+ (GFP+tdTomato+) and CD51− astrocytes (GFP+tdTomato−) from the cortex and hippocampus of 8-week-old mice (Extended Data Fig. 1c) and perform transcriptomic analysis using RNA-seq. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that the isolated CD51+GFP+ and CD51−GFP+ populations exhibit molecular signatures of astrocytes and demonstrate transcriptomic profiles that are distinct from one another (Extended Data Fig. 4a–d,f and Supplementary Table 1). Moreover, comparison with FACS-isolated astrocyte populations from our previous studies4 revealed a strong correlation between CD51+GFP+ astrocytes and population C, which also expresses CD51 (Extended Data Fig. 4e). Next, we assessed Gene Ontology (GO) terms enriched in CD51+ astrocytes from the cortex, finding genes associated with synaptic function, synapse assembly and interactions with neurons enriched in this population (Fig. 2b,c and Supplementary Table 2). Given that astrocytes have essential roles in synapse formation22,23, we next assessed the expression of known synaptogenic genes in CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes, finding enriched expression of glypican family members in CD51+ astrocytes (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 5). In summary, these data indicate that CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes have unique molecular features.

The foregoing transcriptomic analyses indicate that CD51+ astrocytes are endowed with unique synaptogenic properties. Moreover, our previous in vitro studies suggest that CD51+ populations engender enhanced synapse formation on cultured neurons4. Together, these observations led us to examine the capacity of CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes to promote synapse formation in vivo in the developing postnatal cortex. Previous studies have shown that chemogenetic, DREADD-based (hM3Dq) stimulation of astrocytes promotes neuronal circuit activity24,25. These collective observations led us to examine whether DREADD-based stimulation of CD51+ or CD51− astrocytes can promote synapse formation during postnatal cortical development. To test this, we combined conditional AAV tools with FLP-on or FLP-off elements regulating hM3Dq expression with the CD51-FLP mouse lines, such that, in one condition, hM3Dq is expressed exclusively in CD51+ astrocytes and, in the other condition, it is expressed exclusively in CD51− astrocytes (Fig. 2e–h). Following our established protocol for chemogenetic stimulation in the developing cortex21, we injected AAVs at P0–2, treated the mice with 5 mg per kg of clozapine N-oxide (CNO) (or saline as a control) from P7–21 to stimulate astrocytes and collected mouse tissue at P21 (Fig. 2e). We next used immunostaining to assess synapse numbers in layers II/III of the cortex, finding that DREADD-based stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes promoted a robust increase in the formation of excitatory synapses (Fig. 2i,j), as well as inhibitory synapses (Extended Data Fig. 6a–d). Notably, stimulation of CD51− astrocytes did not significantly enhance excitatory or inhibitory synapse formation (Fig. 2i,j and Extended Data Fig. 6a–d). In parallel, we performed electrophysiological recordings from layer II/III at P21 and found that stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes led to a concordant increase in synaptic activity, while stimulation of CD51− astrocytes did not (Fig. 2k,l). Notably, after CD51+ astrocyte stimulation, we observed an increase in synaptic current frequency, but not amplitude, which is consistent with an increase in the number of synapses (Fig. 2k,l). Critically, our astrocyte stimulation paradigm did not alter neuronal numbers or layering, indicating the observed phenomenon is not due to alterations in the constituency or organization of cortical neurons (Extended Data Fig. 7). hM3D+ astrocytes robustly expressed FOS 90 min after the last CNO injection, validating that our repeated stimulation paradigm enabled persistent astrocyte activation (Extended Data Fig. 8a–c). Moreover, repeated CNO administration in the absence of hM3D did not affect synapse numbers (Extended Data Fig. 8d–f). These data indicate that stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes selectively promotes synapse formation in the developing cortex.

These observations led us to investigate the mechanisms by which CD51+ astrocytes promote synapse formation in the developing cortex. Our transcriptomic analysis identified members of the glypican family as demonstrating enriched expression in cortical CD51+ astrocytes26,27 (Fig. 2d). Using viral approaches in CD51-FLP mice to label CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes, we found that glypican 6 (GPC6) protein expression is enriched in CD51+ astrocytes (Extended Data Fig. 5c). This led us to next examine whether Gpc6 expression in CD51+ astrocytes is necessary for the enhanced synaptogenic effect we observed (Fig. 2i–l). To achieve this, we intercrossed the CD51-FLP line with the Rosa-LSL-Cas9 line generating the CD51-FLP;Rosa-LSL-Cas9-eGFP mouse line (where LSL denotes loxP-stop-loxP, for Cre-dependent Cas9-eGFP expression). Using a similar paradigm as described above, mice were injected with an AAV containing single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting Gpc6 (omitted in the controls), an AAV containing an FLP-inducible astrocyte-specific Cre and the abovementioned AAV driving FLP-dependent astrocyte-specific hM3Dq expression (Fig. 2m). Together, this system enabled us to delete Gpc6 from and subsequently stimulate CD51+ astrocytes. Mice were injected at P1, treated with CNO beginning at P7 and collected at P21 (Fig. 2m); subsequent analysis of GPC6 expression revealed robust and selective KO in CD51+ astrocytes expressing Cre (Extended Data Fig. 6e–g). We next assessed synapse formation through immunostaining and found that KO of Gpc6 eliminated hM3Dq-induced increases in excitatory synapse formation (Fig. 2n,o). Furthermore, overexpression of Gpc6 with AAV-GFAP-Gpc6 injected intracortically between P1 was sufficient to increase synapse numbers measured at P21 (Extended Data Fig. 8g–k). RNA-seq analysis of hM3D–mCherry+CD51+ astrocytes following our CNO or saline injection paradigm (Extended Data Fig. 9) revealed an upregulation of pathways related to protein trafficking to distal processes, suggesting that Gq stimulation may promote delivery of GPC6 to astrocyte leaflets where it drives synapse formation. Collectively, these data indicate that CD51+ astrocytes selectively promote synapse formation and use GPC6 as part of this mechanism.

The preceding finding established that CD51+ and CD51− astrocyte populations have distinct functional properties towards synaptogenesis in the developing cortex. We next sought to expand our functional comparison between CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes to other brain regions, associated circuits and stages of life, focusing on the adult hippocampus. Transcriptomic analysis of hippocampal CD51+ astrocytes indicates that they are also enriched in synaptogenic and synaptic transmission-related genes when compared with CD51− astrocytes (Fig. 2c,d and Extended Data Fig. 4a–c). These observations, coupled with the synaptogenic function of CD51+ astrocytes in the developing cortex, led us to hypothesize that they may also selectively influence hippocampal circuits and associated memory behaviours. To test this, we combined the CD51-FLP mouse with the conditional AAV tools that enable selective expression of hM3Dq in CD51+ or CD51− astrocytes (Figs. 2f and 3a), injecting the hippocampus of 8-week-old mice. Key controls included using AAVs containing hM3Dq that did not selectively target CD51+ or CD51− astrocytes, as previous studies have shown that pan-astrocytic stimulation of hippocampal astrocytes can enhance hippocampal circuit function24,25. As a frontline experiment, we collected mouse tissue 4 weeks after injection, generated hippocampal slices and evaluated long-term potentiation (LTP). Using subthreshold stimulation (40 Hz, 10 pulses) of the Schaffer collateral, we found that stimulation of pan-astrocytes, CD51+ astrocytes and CD51− astrocytes were all capable of inducing LTP (Extended Data Fig. 10c,d). In parallel, we generated another set of these experimental groups and performed a series of memory behaviours, finding that DREADD-based stimulation led to enhanced memory performance in each group (Extended Data Fig. 10e,f).

In the hippocampus, around 80–85% of astrocytes are CD51−, while about 15–20% of astrocytes are CD51+ (Fig. 1c). Our observation that stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes yielded the same enhancement of LTP and memory performance as CD51− astrocytes, despite four to sixfold less representation in the hippocampus, suggests that CD51+ astrocytes are significantly more effective at enhancing hippocampal circuit plasticity than CD51− astrocytes. To test this, we repeated these experiments using a dilution of the GFAP-FRT-hM3Dq-mCherry-FRT AAV that targets CD51− astrocytes. We found that a 1:50 dilution restricts expression in CD51− astrocytes to around 15–20% of all astrocytes, matching the proportion of CD51+ astrocytes in the hippocampus (Extended Data Fig. 10a,b). Similar to the above studies, we injected CD51-FLP mice with undiluted versions of pan-astrocytic and CD51+ targeting virus, as well as the 1:50 dilution of the CD51− targeting virus at 8 weeks and collected mouse tissue 4 weeks later for LTP studies, while also conducting memory behaviour analysis on a separate cohort at the same timepoint. Notably, these studies revealed that stimulation of around 20% of CD51− astrocytes was incapable of inducing LTP at subthreshold levels or enhancing memory performance, while stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes induced LTP and enhanced memory performance (Fig. 3d–g). Consistent with these findings, analysis of basal synaptic activity of CA1 neurons revealed that stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes promotes synaptic activity, while targeting around 20% of CD51− astrocytes did not (Fig. 3b,c). Additional studies revealed no difference in paired-pulse ratio, indicating that stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes enhances synaptic input and connectivity (Extended Data Fig. 11). Furthermore, the levels of LTP, synaptic activity and memory performance after stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes was comparable to pan-astrocyte stimulation (Fig. 3c–g and Extended Data Fig. 10c–f). Collectively, these data indicate that CD51+ astrocytes are more effective than CD51− astrocytes at promoting hippocampal circuit plasticity and associated memory behaviours.

Our studies in the developing cortex and adult hippocampus indicate that CD51+ astrocytes have specialized functions that facilitate neural circuit activity. To further understand the mechanisms by which this population engenders circuit function, we focused on CD51 itself, which is encoded by the gene integrin alpha V28. We used the adult hippocampus as a model system in which to assess how manipulation of CD51 affects circuit function. To delete Itgav in hippocampal astrocytes, we used similar approaches used in the GPC6 experiment (Fig. 2m), except in these studies we injected Rosa-LSL-Cas9 mice with AAVs to express (1) a gRNA targeting Itgav; and (2) cre under control of the Gfap promoter (Fig. 4a). Littermates not carrying the Rosa-LSL-Cas9 allele were used as controls. When put together, this system enabled us to selectively express Cas9 and the Itgav-targeting gRNA in hippocampal astrocytes. Accordingly, we injected the hippocampus of 8-week-old LSL-Cas9 mice (and wild-type littermates) and collected mouse tissue 1 month later, confirming selective deletion of Itgav in hippocampal astrocytes (Fig. 4b). Using a similar workflow to that described above, we generated mice in which Itgav has been knocked out in hippocampal astrocytes, collected mouse tissue 4 weeks after injection and assessed LTP and basal synaptic activity. Using theta-burst stimulation to evoke LTP, we found that deletion of astrocytic Itgav led to a loss of potentiation (Fig. 4c,d), coupled with a significant reduction in the basal levels of synaptic activity in CA1 neurons (Fig. 4e–g). Using a parallel cohort of mice generated using the same workflow, we conducted a series of memory behavioural assays, finding that mice with Itgav-KO exhibited significant defects in memory performance (Fig. 4h,i). We complemented these KO studies with Itgav-overexpression experiments, using an AAV-based system to overexpress HA-tagged CD51 (or mCherry control) in hippocampal astrocytes from Aldh1l1-GFP mice (Fig. 4k). To test this system, we injected the hippocampus of Aldh1l1-GFP or wild-type mice at 8 weeks of age, collected mouse tissue 1 month later and confirmed robust overexpression of HA–CD51 specifically in astrocytes (Fig. 4l and Extended Data Fig. 12). We next repeated this experiment on wild-type mice, using LTP and basal synaptic properties as our frontline experiments on mouse tissue collected 1 month after AAV injection. For the LTP studies, we used subthreshold stimulation (40 Hz, 10 pulse) of the Schaffer collateral and found that overexpression of CD51 in hippocampal astrocytes elicited LTP (Fig. 4m,n). Analysis of synaptic properties under these conditions revealed an increase in synaptic activity as well, coupled with no changes in paired-pulse ratio, consistent with enhanced synaptic input and connectivity (Fig. 4o–q and Extended Data Fig. 11). Parallel studies on memory behaviours revealed a congruent increase in both spatial- and fear-memory behaviours after CD51 overexpression (Fig. 4q,r). Taken together, these studies indicate that astrocytic CD51 protein has a direct role in regulating hippocampal circuit activity and contributes to the specialized synaptic functions of CD51+ astrocytes.

To further understand how CD51 influences hippocampal circuit function, we overexpressed HA–CD51 in both hippocampal and cortical astrocytes, and performed immunoprecipitation–mass spectrometry (IP–MS) to identify prospective interacting partners specific to each region. Analysis of the protein interactome for CD51 revealed region-specific interactions (Extended Data Fig. 13 and Supplementary Table 3). Focusing on CD51-interacting proteins that are specific to the hippocampus, we found that CD51 interacted with NMDA receptors (GRIN1), GABA transporters (SLC32A1), Na+/K+ channels (HCN1) and SLC16A7 (also known as MCT2). Interactions with these proteins is consistent with a role for CD51 in modulating hippocampal circuit function.

Astrocytes have central roles in every form of neurological pathology29. As CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes exhibit unique functional properties during cortical development and in functioning neural circuits, we next examined whether these populations are endowed with distinct characteristics under pathological conditions. We focused these studies on cortical stroke injury because astrocytes are known to contribute to the repair process, of which synaptic remodelling is a key component30,31. We first evaluated core features of CD51+ and CD51− astrocyte populations in a cortical photothrombotic model of ischaemic stroke32,33. Using our FLP-dependent AAVs in conjunction with the CD51-FLP mouse (Fig. 1d), we assessed the morphological properties of peri-infarct CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes after stroke. These studies revealed that peri-infarct CD51+ astrocytes displayed an enhanced morphological complexity compared with CD51− astrocytes at 7 and 21 days after stroke (Fig. 5a,b). Moreover, we observed an accumulation of CD51+ astrocytes at the lesion border, suggesting that CD51+ astrocytes preferentially form the astrocyte border (Extended Data Fig. 14a). This finding was further supported by spatial transcriptomics after stroke in which elevated expression of Itgav is observed at the lesion border34 (Extended Data Fig. 14b). These observations, coupled with our findings that CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes exhibit different proliferative capacities during postnatal cortical development (Fig. 1n,o) led us to assess their proliferative response after stroke, which is part of the astrocyte response to injury32. We induced strokes in CD51-FLP;Rosa-FSF-tdTomato mice, labelled proliferating cells with EdU from days 3–7 after stroke and assessed proliferative responses at 21 days after injury (Fig. 5c). We found that CD51+ astrocytes (SOX9+tdTomato+) were more proliferative (EdU+) at the infarct border compared with CD51− astrocytes (SOX9+tdTomato−) (Fig. 5d,e). We observed similar results when assessing proliferation by staining for the cell cycle markers Ki-67 and phosphorylated histone H3 7 days after stroke (Extended Data Fig. 14c). Furthermore, we again observed an accumulation of CD51+ astrocytes at the lesion border (Fig. 5d). Together, these data indicate that CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes exhibit unique cellular responses to cortical stroke, which parallel their distinct properties in the developing cortex.

Given the distinct properties of CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes responding to stroke, we next examined whether these two populations differentially interacted with synapses in peri-infarct cortex. To accomplish this, we used the Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET)-based neuron-astrocyte proximity assay (NAPA), which enables measurement of astrocyte–synapse interactions at synaptic distance scales35. NAPA involves expressing extracellular membrane-targeted GFP (FRET donor) in astrocytes and presynapse-targeted mCherry (FRET acceptor) in a defined neuronal afferent projection. In this assay, FRET signals are reflective of synaptic-scale proximity between GFP-expressing astrocytes and mCherry-expressing synapses. We injected the cortex, targeting regions that would later surround the infarct, of two groups of CD51-FLP mice with AAVs encoding the astrocytic component (NAPA-a) controlled by Flp-dependent elements to specify expression in either CD51+ or CD51− astrocytes (Fig. 5f,g). In both groups, we labelled thalamocortical projections with the neuronal component (NAPA-n). We subjected mice to strokes 3 weeks after AAV injections and prepared tissue for FRET imaging in peri-infarct cortex 7 days later (Fig. 5f). Peri-infarct CD51+ astrocytes had significantly greater FRET signal than CD51− astrocytes (Fig. 5g,h). These data suggest that CD51+ astrocytes exhibit closer interactions with synapses in remodelling peri-infarct regions after stroke.

The preceding studies indicate that DREADD-based stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes selectively promotes synapse formation in the developing cortex (Fig. 2), leading us to examine whether such a manipulation would similarly affect synaptic remodelling after stroke, which is linked with functional recovery31. Using the CD51-FLP mouse line and FLP-dependent AAVs previously described (Fig. 2e,f), we expressed hM3Dq (or lck-smV5 as an inert control) in CD51+ or CD51− cortical astrocytes in regions that would later surround the infarct, and induced strokes 4 weeks later (Fig. 5i,j). Beginning 7 days after stroke, we treated mice with CNO or saline twice daily until 21 days after stroke (Fig. 5i,j). During this timeline, we assessed motor function using the grid walk assay at three key timepoints: (1) before stroke; (2) 1 week after stroke before commencing CNO or saline treatment; and (3) 3 weeks after stroke (after 2 weeks of CNO or saline treatment) (Fig. 5i). We found that stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes resulted in enhanced recovery of motor function in comparison to the controls and stimulation of CD51− astrocytes (Fig. 5k). Importantly, there was no difference in the lesion volume between groups (Fig. 5l and Extended Data Fig. 14d). We next assessed synapse numbers in the peri-infarct cortex after stimulation, finding that CD51+ astrocyte stimulation increased numbers of excitatory and inhibitory synapses, while there were no observable differences in synapse formation after CD51− astrocyte stimulation (Fig. 5m–o and Extended Data Fig. 14e–g). We observed no differences in per-infarct vessel density, neuron numbers, GFAP+ immunoreactivity or IBA1+ immunoreactivity (Extended Data Fig. 15). These observations suggest that the functional recovery observed after CD51 stimulation is the result of increased synapse remodelling leading to enhanced restoration of circuit function. Collectively, these data indicate that stimulation of CD51+ astrocytes selectively enhances functional recovery after stroke and that the unique synaptogenic properties of CD51+ astrocytes are conserved across postnatal development and repair after brain injury.

The nature of astrocyte diversity in the CNS has remained enigmatic owing to the paucity of tools that enable targeting of specific subpopulations, coupled with a lack of robust and clearly defined functional benchmarks1,2. In our study, we generated a mouse line and associated viral tools that enable selective targeting and manipulation of CD51+ and CD51− astrocyte subpopulations in the developing, mature and injured brain. We found that CD51+ astrocytes are functionally distinct from CD51− astrocytes and have specialized functions that support synaptogenesis in the developing and injured cortex, along with memory circuit function in the hippocampus. Critically, comparing the functional properties between subpopulation of cells is essential for operationally defining distinct subpopulations and our newly developed tools enable such direct comparisons between CD51+ and CD51− astrocytes. Inferenced heterogeneity from single-cell transcriptomic or developmental patterning data does not incorporate functional evaluation, as mRNA profiles cannot predict physiological outputs for astrocytes1,2,3. Parallel studies identified genes that exhibit restricted expression in subsets of astrocytes and demonstrated roles for these genes in core astrocyte functions; however, they do not directly compare the functional properties between prospective astrocyte subpopulations9,10. In contrast to neuronal diversity, which has clearly defined physiological, molecular and morphological criteria, defining astrocyte diversity is not as clear cut. Our findings indicate that astrocyte subpopulations exhibit graded functional properties, with CD51+ astrocytes being more efficient at promoting interactions with functioning neural circuits under normal and pathological conditions. These observations suggest that the specialized properties of CD51+ astrocytes are driven by interactions with neurons, insinuating that additional, functionally distinct astrocyte subpopulations may arise from interactions with vascular or immune populations.

Our finding that CD51+ astrocytes engender synapse formation in the postnatal cortex, in part through elevated expression of GPC6, and exhibit unique proliferative properties suggests that these functional properties are specified early in development. Indeed, we found CD51+ populations initially appear in the ventricular/subventricular zone around P1 and their genesis is tied to intrinsic transcription factor programs, further suggesting that they represent a specified astrocyte subtype with distinct functional properties. These findings are consistent with observations in the spinal cord indicating that developmental patterning mechanisms generate distinct subtypes of astrocytes36. By contrast, clonal analysis of cortical astrocytes suggests that morphological diversity of astrocytes is linked to environmental cues, occurring after migration from the germinal zones37. The extent to which the functional properties of CD51+ astrocytes are shaped by environmental cues or are pre-ordained is an important future direction, where clonal analysis of this population may provide additional insight. While neuronal activity did not affect the generation of CD51+ astrocytes in the cortex, it remains possible that their functional properties exhibit plasticity and are tied to specific neural circuits in other brain regions. In the mature brain, CD51+ astrocytes and Itgav itself robustly support hippocampal circuit function, while also executing parallel roles in repairing synapses and circuit function after cortical stroke injury. Roles for astrocytes in hippocampal circuits and memory are well known38,39,40; however, our findings suggest that a select subset of astrocytes are sufficient to promote memory. These results parallel recent findings demonstrating that ensembles of learning-associated astrocytes function within memory engrams and are sufficient to promote memory recall13. Collectively, these observations raise the possibility that CD51+ astrocytes are preferentially recruited to memory engrams in the hippocampus and, more broadly, that CD51+ astrocytes may specifically participate in engrams associated with other forms of learning41.

#1049. Ousmane Dembélé quietly becomes the main man after long journey to the top

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

The Frenchman, who has been named the best male footballer in the world by the Guardian, has benefitted from PSG’s focus on the team rather than individuals

What makes a good player great, and a great player the best? This question has been occupying me since 2014, when the Guardian first asked me to contribute to its inaugural Next Generation feature. My job was to look for a France-based talent born in 1997 who could go on to have a stellar career.

After a great deal of research, I narrowed it down from my shortlist of five by asking questions not about the players’ football ability, but about other attributes: resilience, adaptability, decision-making, creativity, work ethic, response to feedback and willingness to learn. Qualities we cannot see, and are harder to measure.

Based on those answers, one player stood out above all the others: a kid called Ousmane Dembélé, then a youth player yet to make a first-team appearance at Rennes. Eleven years after appearing in these pages as one to watch, Dembélé has been voted the best male player in the world by the Guardian’s 219-strong voting panel.

Those intangible qualities were in full effect on the night Dembélé made good on his years of promise. The defining image of Paris Saint-Germain’s 5-0 win over Inter in the 2025 Champions League final was not the trophy lift, or any one of the goal celebrations, but Dembélé, poised on the edge of the opposition penalty area, with hunched demeanour and furrowed brow, his face a picture of focus, ready to press. Just how did he get to this point?

One lesson to learn from the Dembélé story: the path to greatness is not always linear. He started off like a train, winning France’s young player of the year award in his first season as a professional. The following season, he helped Borussia Dortmund win the German Cup.

Around that time, in the course of research for my book, Edge, I spoke to Thomas Tuchel, his coach at Dortmund. Tuchel told me that Dembélé’s talent came with an obligation and responsibility to improve. He bracketed his players into one of three “ABC” categories, depending on their dominant motivation, and would change his man-management style accordingly.

The A category stood for “aggressive-motivated”, and referred to players motivated by individual glory and awards (in Tuchel’s eyes, this was not necessarily a negative: think Neymar at Paris).

B stood for “binding-motivated”, those players who love being part of the group and bringing people together (often captains, such as César Azpilicueta at Chelsea).

C stood for “curious-motivated”. These were the players capable of greatness, who needed to be trained in a slightly different way. Their talent could take them far; and on a good day, they were capable of anything. Tuchel loved coaching these players. Dembélé, he felt, was a C.

But the journey was not always smooth. In 2017, he moved to Barcelona, who were flush with money from selling Neymar for €222m (£194m). It was a sliding doors moment. That same summer, Barcelona changed coach: in came Ernesto Valverde; out went Luis Enrique. In six injury-laden years at Barcelona, Dembélé started only a third of the league games. He scored a total of 24 league goals. His most memorable moment might have been missing a last-minute one-on-one in the 2019 Champions League semi-final against Liverpool, when Barcelona were already 3-0 up (remember what happened next? They lost the second leg 4-0). When he did leave, amid mutterings of wasted talent and blackmail, it felt like a relief for all parties.

Kick off your evenings with the Guardian's take on the world of football

There is some symmetry to Dembélé’s exceptional 2025. Of the 30 goals he has scored this calendar year at the time of writing, the most decisive and emblematic was probably against Liverpool in the second leg of their Champions League last-16 tie. With PSG 1-0 down from the first leg, Dembélé drifted into his own half, losing his markers who didn’t want to follow him that far. He picked up the ball and spread it wide to the right-winger Bradley Barcola, then sprinted into the box and made it to the cross to score. (He also scored a penalty in PSG’s successful shootout after they won 1-0 at Anfield that night). Dembélé did something similar against Arsenal in the semi-final, dropping deep early in the game, switching the play to the left-sided Khvicha Kvaratskhelia and receiving the return ball, unmarked, on the edge of the area, before scoring. Even when teams knew it was coming, they couldn’t stop him.

At the start of last season, Luis Enrique’s plan was for the whole team to replace the goals of Kylian Mbappé, who had joined Real Madrid. After all, Dembélé had not scored more than 10 league goals in a season since his debut campaign at Rennes. Luis Enrique wanted the team to be the star. And so, with no star player to serve, Dembélé was liberated. This curious-motivated talent who once scored penalties with either foot, and even took corners with either foot, quietly became the main man. He starts the press; his vision, movement, pace, control and positioning set the tone; and his finishing, once considered wasteful, is ruthless.

I still don’t know exactly what makes a good player great but I am closer to an answer. Part of the equation remains what we cannot see: the chemistry within a team, the relationship with a specific coach, playing in a system that brings out the best in every player. But also those other qualities that Dembélé had at 17, and still has now. This well-deserved prize is as much for those attributes as it is for his goals and the trophies he helped Paris Saint-Germain win.

#1045. Munich up against at least six competitors for 2036 Olympics

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

The International Olympic Committee has revealed the six other countries intending to bid for the 2036 Olympic Games. This comes after Germany selected Munich as its Olympic candidate.

Munich's bid for the 2036 Summer Olympics will be up against at least six candidates from Europe, Asia and Africa. On Tuesday, the International Olympic Committee (IOC) announced that, in addition to Germany, Hungary, Turkey, India, Qatar, South Korea and South Africa all intend to bid for the Games. All are currently engaged in the Continuous Dialogue stage of the bidding process with the IOC.

However, there could be further applicants, as the deadline for interested parties to submit a bid is October 31. The candidates are to present their plans virtually to the Future Host Commission in November. In March 2027, the IOC is to deliberate, analyze, and narrow down the field of applicants to a group selected to enter into the Strategic Dialogue stage.

A subsequent Targeted Dialogue stage with one or more bidders is planned for 2028, with the final decision on the 2036 Olympic host expected at the IOC Session in the summer of 2029.

However, the IOC did not disclose exactly which cities, alongside Munich, are in the running – although Ahmedabad, Doha, Budapest and Istanbul are seen as possible hopefuls.

Munich secured its bid late last month after beating the Cologne/Rhine-Ruhr region in a vote by delegates of the German Olympic Sports Confederation. The city last hosted the Games in 1972. Berlin had withdrawn its bid in the days leading up to the vote.

"Wherever the Games take place, one thing must never change: Athletes have to be at the heart of everything we do," IOC President Kirsty Coventry said.

"As an athlete, you spend years working to make your Olympic dream come true. The experience you have when you finally reach the Olympic Games stays with you for the rest of your life," added the former swimmer who won gold in the 200-meter backstroke for Zimbabwe at the 2004 Athens and 2008 Beijing Summer Games.

"That is why, for every future host, delivering the best possible experience for the athletes must always be the starting point."

The IOC is to evaluate the bidders' proposals based on "technical factors." These include a master plan for competition venues and the athlete experience, the financing strategy, political and public support, the capacity to deliver the event, and a sustainability strategy. The German Olympic Sports Confederation believes Munich is well-positioned in all these areas.

The Munich bid is not focused solely on 2036 but was also designed with 2040 and 2044 in mind. If it misses out on 2036, Munich can expect to face a similarly large pool of competitors for the following two Summer Games. According to an IOC statement, there are National Olympic Committees "taking part in noncommittal talks with the IOC about hosting future Olympic Games beyond 2036."

Players will receive a one-match ban should they receive four yellow cards before the semi-finals of the Champions League.

This rule runs from the start of the league phase until the end of the quarter-finals. After then, all yellow cards are wiped out and players cannot be suspended for multiple yellow cards.

The same rules apply for both the Europa League and the Conference League.

During the opening three rounds of Champions League, 16 players received two yellow cards, meaning they are now two cautions away from a one-match ban.

Shakhtar Donetsk midfielder Marlon Gomes is on three yellow cards, putting him on the brink of a ban.

Players can also be suspended if they pick up six, eight or 10 yellow cards. This would also result in a one-match suspension every time a player hits an even number of yellow cards above four.

Red cards result in a one-match suspension, with Bayern Munich's Michael Olise missing his side's clash with Arsenal after being sent off against Viking in the previous round of fixtures.

Uefa may increase the length of a red card ban for serious offences such as violent conduct.
