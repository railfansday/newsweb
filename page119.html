#1294. What do Manchester City's charges mean for German football?

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Manchester City have reportedly been found guilty of major financial rule breaches. What does this mean for German football?

Manchester City have reportedly been found guilty on 114 of the 115 Premier League charges over alleged breaches of financial regulations. They relate to a period between 2009 to 2018 and include allegations around financial reporting, disclosure of player and manager payments, compliance with UEFA and Premier League financial rules, and cooperation with the investigation. The Premier League has yet to comment, with the news having first been reported by The Athletic.

The process started when the charges were brought in February 2023. The review was started in 2024 before the reported verdict of guilty on almost all charges arrived on September 25, 2026.

The English champions, owned in practice by the United Arab Emirates' ruling family, deny all charges and insist their case is supported by a "comprehensive body of irrefutable evidence."

The Premier League has long had a reputation for rampant capitalism, but it has started to enforce financial regulations that control spending in recent years.

In 2026, Chelsea were fined €11.6 million ($13.8 million) and given a suspended ban from registering new players for two transfer windows for breaching rules relating to payments to agents between 2009 and 2022. The club did avoid a six-point deduction though. In the recent years before, Everton and Nottingham Forest were docked Premier League points for breaking profit and sustainability rules (PSR).

City previously won, for the most part, a similar battle with UEFA. The 2023 UEFA Champions League winners were hit with a huge fine by UEFA in 2014 over a breach of the organization's FFP (Financial Fair Play) rules. Then, in February 2020, UEFA handed City a €30-million fine and banned the club from competing in the Champions League for two years for falsely inflating sponsorship revenues between 2012 and 2016. But in July of the same year, the Court of Arbitration for Sport (CAS) overturned the ban and reduced the fine to €10 million. City denied all charges and did not miss a game in Europe's premier club competition.

The key difference in City's current case to Chelsea's earlier in the year is that Chelsea self-reported and admitted guilt, which allowed them to negotiate with the Premier League over the sanction. City have stressed that they are innocent throughout.

German football is by no means immune to scandals or corruption. The most prominent examples are the 1971 match-fixing scandal in which players took bribes to throw matches, and the 2005 match-fixing scandal involving referee Robert Hoyzer, who confessed to fixing and betting on matches in the second division and German Cup.

The majority of financial breaches have come from clubs not being able to prove they were financially viable. Both 1860 Munich and Uerdingen are examples of this, but major systematic abuses or breaches have not happened because the structure of German football is designed to prevent it.

Mainly because of the 50+1 rule. In place since 1999, the rule stipulates that the parent club, for example Bayern Munich, must hold at least 50% plus one vote in its spun-off professional football company – in this case, FC Bayern München Fußball AG. This ensures that the majority of voting rights always lie with the club and its members.

The rule prevents external investors from taking complete control of a club and managing it solely according to financial criteria. It is also meant to ensure a certain level of equality, as it is not possible for a club to invest disproportionately more money than other clubs thanks to its powerful investor with a controlling stake.

The vast majority of organized fans in Germany oppose the commercialization of football. The 50+1 rule makes many fans in Germany not just paying spectators but active club members who can, for example, have a say inimportant club decisions.

Critics argue that the rule puts German football clubs at a financial disadvantage. Spending in the Premier League proves that to be true, but City's reported offenses are also proof of why the league's sustainable structure matters. The Bundesliga has been approached by DW for comment.

Not until the ruling and punishment are final. However, one of the main talking points is what City's success prevented other teams from claiming, whether it be prize money, Champions League places or transfer opportunities. These are compensatory claims that might be explored should the charges remain after appeals.

Manchester City face possible points deductions, expulsion, financial penalties or the stripping of their titles. It is unclear when the decision on what the punishment will be will come, especially given that City are expected to appeal.

The outcome also has significant impacts for the Premier League. In taking on one of its biggest clubs, having successfully punished two smaller ones, they risk appearing toothless. Certainly the eyes of European football will be on them as the impact of the decision will put the integrity of the competition into focus.

It may also see more English fans call for a model like the 50+1 rule to be integrated. Some voices were raised in England after the Saudi sovereign wealth fund PIF took over Premier League club Newcastle United in 2021, and others continued during the attempted establishment of a European Super League in 2021.

#1293. What to watch in UEFA Champions League: Arsenal to test Bayern Munich's depth

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

Follow that, Wednesday! Tuesday brought an incredible 40 goals across nine games with big wins for Liverpool and Manchester City on the undercard. The main event did not disappoint either, Paris Saint-Germain scoring twice late on to beat Barcelona 4-2 despite the loss of Ousmane Dembele and Khvicha Kvaratskhelia to injury.

Thank goodness then that there's plenty more to look forward to today. Real Madrid's record signing Yan Diomande is reunited with RB Leipzig while Inter look to recover from their shock defeat to Club Brugge, whose star striker will have many in Europe intrigued. The place to start, however, has to be the clash at the Allianz Arena.

When these two sides met 11 months ago Arsenal delivered as emphatic a vindication of their recruitment policy as they have at any stage in Mikel Arteta's tenure. Missing all three senior center forwards as well as defensive anchor Gabriel, it was impressive enough that the hosts continued to roll out competitive lineups, all the more so when they lost Leandro Trossard with just over half an hour played. Arsenal did not just hold their own in strained circumstances, however. They improved as the game went on, particularly when Arteta turned to his bench. There's a reason why he calls his substitutes finishers. Riccardo Calafiori charged on to deliver a brilliant cross for Noni Madueke to score before a third substitute, Gabriel Martinelli, turned on the afterburners to fly past Manuel Neuer.

A tight game broken open by Arteta's strength in reserve: it's the story of Arsenal since the start of last season. Twenty-eight goals across all competitions have been scored by substitutes, including winners against Leeds United and Nottingham Forest in the last fortnight. More than that, however, Arteta's dizzying depth ensured that his team was able to weather an injury crisis that robbed them of their first-choice frontline until the night before they won the Premier League title. As the season went on, standards dropped from that brilliant win over Bayern. Viktor Gyokeres and Eberechi Eze were no Kai Havertz and Martin Odegaard, but they were good enough for Arsenal to do just enough in the Premier League. At the other end of the pitch, Arsenal have been without William Saliba all year long and are still probably the best defense in Europe. It certainly helps when his backup is England international Ezri Konsa.

Bayern have few such insurance policies. They aren't really needed. Standards in the Bundesliga have not (yet?) dropped to those of Ligue 1 such that Vincent Kompany could get away with giving his strongest XI more minutes off than on domestically. Equally, the German game, with four fewer league matches and only one domestic cup, does not insist on flaying its top clubs like the English. So far 18 Bayern players are in three figures for minutes played this season, come the end of it you could imagine that only Serge Gnabry has joined their number. By way of comparison, Arsenal should comfortably hit 23 or 24 come May.

As for now, the depth Arsenal have built up could be particularly impactful at the Allianz Arena. The Bavarians' limited numbers are stretched thinner with Michael Olise suspended and Jamal Musiala out injured. That means Ismael Saibari and Lennart Karl likely having to start, Gnabry perhaps the only senior forward who could come off the bench to change a tight game. Joshua Kimmich missed training on Tuesday for "private reasons" but is expected to play. If his involvement is in any way limited, it might be 21-year-old Tom Bischof to enter the fray.

None of these are players unworthy of Bayern, nor are backup full backs Josip Stanisic and Nathaniel Brown. They just don't have the look of players who could change the game against top-level opposition. Meanwhile, Arsenal will probably be able to call on the likes of Bruno Guimaraes, Martin Zubimendi, Ben White, Gyokeres and Eze. For the first 60-70 minutes this promises to be an even game. What follows could be where Arsenal win it.

The sample size at an elite level is not exactly huge, but given his form in the Champions League, it is easy to see why there are multiple tugs of war for Club Brugge striker Nicolo Tresoldi. Wanted by Germany and Italy at international level, he has been linked with the likes of Inter, AC Milan, Barcelona, Bayern Munich, Arsenal, Tottenham, Liverpool and Everton. Their interest will surely not have been quelled over the last week. It is not every striker who manages to net a hat trick against Inter. That does not look like a one-off either. Since signing for the Belgian side at the start of last season, the 22-year-old has eight goals in the Champions League, two of them penalties in this season's competition and another in a qualifier against Rangers last.

Nor is Tresoldi on a heater, indeed he is scoring at a little bit under his 8.8 xG. That's nearly an expected goal per 90 minutes and nearly four shots too. These are elite numbers against frequently elite opposition. Best of all, they pair nicely with impressive performances in his domestic league too. According to Wyscout, Tresoldi is averaging 1.03 xG per 90 in the Belgian league this season; watching some of the shots he gets, you see a player who seems to have that uncoachable poacher's instinct.

While he might have more time to react against La Louviere than he would against opponents in Europe's top five leagues, buying attackers from Club Brugge has tended to pay off for the buying clubs. Lois Openda, Antonio Nusa, Igor Thiago and Christos Tzolis have all made the leap and done so quickly. Given that Tresoldi already looks like he can mix it with the big boys, you suspect the Champions League will be seeing a lot more of the young striker. In whose colors that might be is likely to be the subject of a hotly contested race.

The last few weeks at Real Madrid have been kind to Yan Diomande. Seeing the pitch with increasing frequency, the 19-year-old has looked like a player worthy of the praise garlanded upon him for his sparkling performances with RB Leipzig and the Ivory Coast at the World Cup. Well, that's the assessment of most observers. His manager sees it differently.

After a purposeful run and ferocious finish earned Diomande a goal to go with his assist in Sunday's 4-1 win over Sevilla, Jose Mourinho said of the winger: "He has a lot to improve and a lot of room for improvement. He's young, naive, and an unfinished product. At Real Madrid, the level of demand is very high.

"Instead of playing with the number 25 on his back, he's playing with the 125 [million euros] they paid for him." Truly a man who has never seen a carrot whose qualities might not be better fulfilled with a stick. "He's growing and needs to improve his finishing," Mourinho added. "He often reaches dangerous areas and struggles to relax in the final moment, make better assists, and lift his head to see his teammates."

In Mourinho's defence, he did then express his confidence that Diomande will fulfil his potential, comparing his teen winger to an early Vinicius Junior. That might be underselling Diomande somewhat. In Vinicius' first three seasons with Madrid the Brazilian scored 15 goals in 118 games. At the 11-game mark, Diomande has two with three assists as well.

These are early days indeed, but starting out at 1.59 non-penalty expected goals and expected assists per 90 is impressive. Among players with 400 minutes across Europe's top five leagues, Diomande is the leader for successful one-on-ones at 4.74 per 90. Again, there are caveats around minutes, particularly those as a substitute, and allowances have to be made for the level of opposition. Then again, if this is Diomande at a level that does not satisfy his manager, imagine what he might be doing when/if he reaches Mourinho's exacting standards.

Single-crystal materials are indispensable for high-performance applications, yet their deployment remains constrained by conventional manufacturing techniques that depend on strict quasi-steady thermal conditions1. This requirement fundamentally conflicts with the intense cyclic thermal fluctuations inherent to additive manufacturing2, historically preventing the 3D printing of large or architected single crystals. Here we present a free-form additive manufacturing approach that resolves this contradiction by using delicately controlled alternating and pulsed heat inputs to govern and direct single-crystal growth. Using this method, we demonstrate the fabrication of metre-tall, centimetre-diameter single-crystal Ti-6Al-4V alongside, to our knowledge, the first single-crystal lattice structures containing complex zigzag ligaments, effectively eliminating theoretical size constraints for single-crystal components. The resulting bulk single-crystal Ti-6Al-4V exhibits an order-of-magnitude increase in creep resistance over standard grain-size scaling predictions, driven by the synergistic elimination of grain boundaries and an additive manufacturing-induced hierarchical α/β-architecture that regulates dislocation density, mobility and void evolution. Furthermore, we demonstrate the broad applicability of this technique by printing single crystals of hard-to-process refractory metals, including W, Mo, Nb and MoNb alloys. Our work introduces a large-scale method for single-crystal additive manufacturing, expanding the accessible materials library and enabling high-performance, complex geometries previously unattainable by classical methods.

#1282. Spain grows fruit and vegetables for Europe, but it's running out of water

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Spain's southern coast produces up to 25% of Europe's fruit and vegetables. But amid intensive farming, climate change and illegal wells, the water it takes to grow them is drying up.

Donana National Park in southwestern Spain — one of Europe's most important wetlands, home to millions of migratory birds and threatened species like the Iberian lynx — is running dry. But rising temperatures and drought caused by climate change are only part of the problem.

The UNESCO-protected region on the Atlantic coast relies on an underground aquifer that's been tapped by hundreds of illegal wells. The reason: Europe's fruit and vegetable supply. Spain provides one-quarter of Europe's fresh produce, supplying colder parts of the continent with fruit and vegetables year-round.

The scale of water extraction in Spain is staggering: In 2016, WWF Spain estimated that the Donana wetland was home to over 1,000 illegal wells and more than 1,700 ponds illegally irrigating an area as big as 4,200 football fields.

"It's gotten hotter every year. And the water dries up so quickly," one local resident in the town of El Rocio, just north of the wetlands, told DW.

"For me, day to day, the main issue is my animals," said another, hitching his horse up to a tourist carriage. "I have to draw water for them, but the well doesn't have enough water to provide it all at once."

The Donana area used to be royal hunting grounds, consisting of marshes and ponds. In the early 20th century, parts began to be used for dryland farming, agriculture in arid regions that relies heavily on rainwater.

But in 1971, Spain — still under the dictatorship of General Francisco Franco — declared the region's irrigation to be of national interest. The Almonte-Marismas Plan, named after the aquifer it tapped into, was part of a massive, state-sponsored effort to increase food production.

Authorities decided to bet on strawberries, which they called "red gold." According to calculations based on FAO data, strawberries can yield up to €150,000 (about $170,000) in gross revenue per hectare per year, compared to roughly €20,000 for potatoes or €15,000 for grapes.

But growing strawberries at scale also meant using a lot more water. Authorities handed out generous quotas and allotments, seemingly without considering how much extraction the aquifer could handle. Even those legal quotas weren't enough for the ever-expanding fields.

Farmers turned to illegal wells and reservoirs to make up the difference. They built them to stockpile water, draining supplies from local residents and the UNESCO World Heritage Site next door.

"There was extreme drought and lack of attention from the administration," said Pedro Banez, of the Condado Water Users' Association. "And we found ourselves with a number of farms — or parts of farms — that fell outside the land classified as irrigable agricultural land."

The turning point came in 2019 when, following numerous reports of unlawful wells, a 2-year-old fell into deep, reportedly illegal borehole on farmland near Malaga and died. In the aftermath, Spain's Civil Guard police force created dedicated units focused on water theft.

Two years after that, in 2021, the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled that Spain had failed to protect the Donana aquifer, finding the country in violation of EU law.

Today, police and NGOs like WWF Spain track illegal extraction — near Donana, and other overtaxed agricultural regions across the country — using a mix of old and new tools. They start with satellite images of the region, comparing them against land rights maps to flag areas that appear greener than their legal status allows. Drones follow, giving investigators a closer look at where the water is actually coming from.

Some wells are completely illegal. Others are legal but extract more than they're allowed, which requires closer, on-site inspection to prove.

Investigators check the flow meters that measure how much water a well is pulling. Tampering is common, often using a powerful magnet which disrupts the internal mechanism of the flow meter, allowing water to pass through unrecorded.

Farmers caught extracting illegally can be charged with crimes against the environment and fraud. Most face fines, but sentences can run up to five years in prison.

In a bid to undo decades of overuse, local and national authorities have negotiated a €1.4 billion joint plan. The government has continued shutting down illegal wells, sealing almost 300 in the Donana region alone.

Officials have also started buying up farmland, rewilding it and turning it into protected areas, while setting up funds to create jobs in other sectors like tourism and renewable energy.

Farmers, meanwhile, have had to change how they water their crops.

Many have switched to drip irrigation systems, where each plant gets its own individual dripper, paired with moisture sensors that report exactly how much water a plant needs. That cuts down on the overuse of both water and fertilizer.

Farmers have also begun planting less water-intensive crops and shifting their growing season to colder months.

Spain isn't alone in confronting this problem. As economies mature, fruit and vegetable production starts being outsourced to places with cheaper labor, fewer restrictions and more sun.

Mexico, Brazil and Colombia grow food for parts of the US, while countries in southeast Asia export a lot of produce to China or Japan. Some countries in northern Africa, including Morocco and Egypt, also supply fresh fruits and vegetables to their European neighbors to the north. But, as in Spain, intensive agriculture has strained local water supplies.

Morocco has tried to turn the tide by using satellite comparisons to monitor irrigation and offering subsidies for farmers who switch to drip irrigation. In India, the government is trying to move farmers toward less water-intensive crops.

And in California, the US state has paid farmers to halt certain types of irrigation and introduced legislation to monitor and allocate water more sustainably.

Securing water, no longer seen as an unlimited resource, will increasingly be a challenge as the effects of climate change intensify. Addressing overextraction will take a coordinated effort from governments, farmers and the industries that depend on their water.

#1281. PSG star Khvicha Kvaratskhelia to undergo surgery after suffering ruptured ACL in win over Barcelona

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

Khvicha Kvaratskhelia will undergo surgery for the ruptured anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury he suffered in Paris Saint-Germain's 4-2 win over Barcelona on Tuesday, the French champions have announced.

Kvaratskhelia went down heavily on his right leg following a challenge from Xavi Espart in the 59th minute of the match at the Parc des Princes, fears of a major injury immediately fueled as he had to be carried off the pitch. Though no timeline has been set for his return after an operation, which PSG confirmed will take place "in the coming days," such a major injury will fuel doubts as to whether Kvaratskhelia will play again this season.

ACL injuries may not often be the career enders they once were but a rupture to the knee ligament remains one of the most significant a player can face, usually incurring an absence of at least nine months. It could take even longer before Kvaratskhelia is back to the brilliant form that helped propel PSG to two straight Champions League titles, the Georgian being named player of the tournament for the latter of those wins and also earning a nomination for the Ballon d'Or.

PSG will also be without forward Ousmane Dembele and center back Marquinhos for a "few weeks" after the injuries they suffered in an attritional win over Barcelona. It is, however, Kvaratskhelia's absence that will be giving Luis Enrique sleepless nights.

It is not, however, the case that PSG want for options in attack. This summer saw the Parisians make significant changes to their attacking depth, bringing in combined fees in excess of $350 million for the sales of Goncalo Ramos, Lee Kang-in, Randal Kolo Muani, Bradley Barcola and Ibrahim Mbaye. In their place came the triumvirate of Maghnes Akliouche, Ferran Torres and Mika Godts, each costing around $50 million.

Akliouche would seem the most likely to step in. After all, it was the former Monaco man who got the starting spot against Manchester City over Kvaratskhelia, whose form had faltered in the early weeks of the season. The 24-year-old has two goals and an assist so far in 11 appearances for his new club and has won praise from Luis Enrique for his versatility and ability to play across the frontline. The latter is a critical quality for PSG, whose starting front three of Kvaratskhelia, Dembele and Desire Doue interchange positions almost at will.

It is easy to see how Ferran Torres might be able to pick up that role too. Generally used as a center forward in his final years at Barcelona and so far at PSG, the 26 year old first broke through as a winger with Valencia and Manchester City. Torres has already started brightly with seven goals to his name for Les Parisiens. The same cannot be said for Godts, whose only start of the season so far came against Le Mans earlier this month.

So PSG do not lack for options even if none of them are quite at Kvaratskhelia's level. The trouble for Luis Enrique might be how many more minutes he has to spread around. A driving force in the back-to-back triumphs has been the opportunity to rest key players in Ligue 1 games, ensuring they are fresh for the critical Champions League games at the back end of the season. With doubts as to whether Kvaratskhelia can make them and Dembele clearly requiring careful management throughout the season, it looks tougher than ever for PSG to keep the rest of their attack fresh and firing.
