#780. Inside Alexander Skarsgård's & Tuva Novotny's Private Family World

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Alexander Skarsgård is pretty out there with his work and red carpet looks, but the Wicker star is extremely private when it comes to his home life with actress Tuva Novotny and their son.

Alexander Skarsgård isn't a wicker man in real life, but he's made of all the right stuff as far as Tuva Novotny is concerned.

And at least one key component of the Swedish actors' romance is that they're on the same page about keeping their careers separate from their personal business.

"For many years, I wouldn’t do interviews, I wouldn’t watch my own stuff," Novotny, who shares a 4-year-old son with the Wicker star and has two daughters from a previous relationship, told Vogue Scandinavia in 2024. "I don’t see myself as a public person. My job is public and then what I do outside of work is super private."

And that's obviously Å-OK with Skarsgård. Back when he was single and living out of a suitcase, he told The Guardian in 2018, he didn't talk about relationships as "a way to protect it."

“When I was a kid, that’s how my father dealt with it," he explained. "I wouldn’t say it kept him sane, because he’s pretty crazy, but somewhat. The fact that he protected his private life so much, and protected my mum who is not an actor or a public figure.”

Skarsgård is the eldest of actor Stellan Skarsgård and physician My Skarsgård's six children, plus he has two half-brothers from his dad's second marriage.

"Eventually when it’s time to have kids and stuff, I want to have nine," the Big Little Lies alum quipped. "I want to beat my dad.”

Skarsgård's own branch of the sprawling tree sprouted toward the end of 2022, but he didn't confirm that he had become a dad until March 2023, when he accepted the congratulations of a reporter—and a stuffed animal from a well-wisher—on the Succession fourth season premiere red carpet.

They've never shared how they met, but he and Novotny have now been together for seven years and they make their home in the Södermalm district of Stockholm, where they're surrounded by members of Skarsgård's "very big, very tight family."

"Lots of drinking, lots of smoking, lots of crazy, eccentric people," he told The Times in an interview published Oct. 17. "But a very fun environment to grow up in. To this day my entire extended family live within a 10-minute walk from here."

As for his own household, the 50-year-old quipped that his partner's teenage daughters think he's "deeply uncool, of course." But since becoming a dad, his "priorities have changed."

Skarsgård explained, "It was easy, before, to be spontaneous. I'm quite adventurous, so if an opportunity arose to go to the South Pole or Greenland, or sail across the Atlantic, I did it."

He now saves most of his risk-taking for the screen, as his role in Wicker—about a lonely fisherwoman who has the town weaver craft a perfect husband out of the titular material and he proceeds to fulfill her every need—would attest.

Skarsgård is still interested in exploring the world, he noted, "but spending time with my boy is incredibly precious. It's made me more discerning. I don't feel the need to work nonstop the way I did before. If I take on a job it has got to be special enough to warrant being away."

When his father starred in 2025's Sentimental Value as a self-absorbed filmmaker who's estranged from his adult daughters, Stellan and his kids had a good time poking fun at the assumptions that the role was at least somewhat true to life.

Skarsgård told The Times that, while he didn't talk to his dad much about work when he was younger, preferring to make his "own mistakes and stupid decisions," the 75-year-old now "feels like a friend who's also a colleague that you're shooting the s--t with."

But when he was a kid the True Blood alum did wish for a more average upbringing.

"My dream was for my family to be normal and fit in and be like everybody else’s family," Skarsgård said during a sit-down with Stellan for Variety last December. "For you to have a briefcase, that would’ve been fantastic, rather than a weird tote bag that you found in India."

Now it's Skarsgård's son who will one day have thoughts about his father's beguiling see-through Saint Laurent footwear, but it's the chaotic portion of his childhood the Emmy winner wants to avoid passing on, not the creative part.

On her end, Novotny "wasn't really interested in performing at all" when she was a child, she told Vogue, but growing up in her own big, artistic family with her actress mom, director dad and five siblings, "the apple doesn’t fall far from the tree. I wasn’t pushed, I was inspired.”

And though the Annihilation actress could do without the interviews, red carpets and other public-facing parts of her career, she "always worked through parenthood," she noted. "It’s super important that you keep yourself intact."

The 46-year-old explained, "Work for me is a place where I feel inspired and I meet people—there’s that social aspect. Bringing that back home is a good thing for parenting—for me, at least."

And while Skarsgård still loves the "traveling circus" aspect of being an actor, he told Variety in February it's now time for him "to balance that with family life and making sure I can be present for my kid. I can’t be as selfish and narcissistic as I was before."

Because now he has a son to not push, but inspire, and let the apple fall where it may.

Born in Gothenburg, Sweden, on June 13, 1951, Stellan Skarsgård starting acting as a teenager in his home country, where he's long since reached national treasure status.

He's a familiar face in Hollywood thanks to films such as The Hunt for Red October, Good Will Hunting, Mamma Mia!, Thor, Dune and Sentimental Value, for which he won a 2026 Golden Globe for supporting actor, as well as roles on HBO's Chernobyl and Disney+'s Andor.

He married doctor My Skarsgård in 1975 and they had six children together—Alexander, Gustaf, Sam, Bill, Eija and Valter—before divorcing in 2007.

"My mom’s gone through a lot," son Bill Skarsgård told Esquire in 2024. "I’m very proud of who she is today. She’s devoted her life to help recovering alcoholics and addicts, which she’s gone through herself." #778. Flat-track bullies Celtic continue descent into European irrelevance

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Callum Osmand and Celtic had a frustrating night in Glasgow

When Mika Baur scored early in the second half against Celta Vigo, so much in Celtic's garden was rosy.

The team had an intensity now that they didn't have before. They had momentum. Belief. They had a Liam Scales header hopping off the top of the crossbar. The La Liga visitors were under the pump.

It was easy to conclude in those naively optimistic moments that Celta Vigo were all passing and no penetration. They passed it to death and got pretty much nowhere.

More than 350 passes in the opening half and one attempt on target. All very pretty. All very technical. All very tippy-tappy.

The question that Celtic fans will be asking now, some with pitchforks in hand, is what the hell happened in the last half-an-hour of this game. Particularly, what happened from minutes 68 to 71.

From three points to no points. From building on the hiding dished out to Hearts to another loss and getting shunted back to bitterness and rancour.

Celtic stopped playing and stopped believing. They were hit with a bit of adversity and they were bowled over by it. Knocked to the ground, never to rise up again.

They'll complain - as they already have - about a foul on Camilo Duran that wasn't picked up just before the equaliser, but there is no talking to the errors they made thereafter.

The inability to deal with a free-kick, the failure to pick up the goalscorer in the first instance and then multiple calamities in the concession of the second goal. How many chances does one side need to get a ball away to safety?

Long before the end, Celtic were gone. Psychologically spent. This is nothing new. Something new would have been the sight of them kicking on at 1-0, but in Europe such strength is a dying art form in this team.

A third straight loss in Europe, 35th out of 36 teams in the Europa League table, only a side from Armenia keeping them off rock bottom. This is their place in the grand scheme now.

Chris Sutton calls them flat-track bullies. How many Celtic fans would disagree with that depiction? Not many.

Thursday was yet another example of domestic football being a lousy barometer of Celtic's capacity to win - or even draw - games in Europe.

In the wake of the 7-1 trouncing of Hearts, some Celtic folk with desperately short memories rounded on a few commentators who were critical of Martin O'Neill, his players and this Celtic board following their losses to Rangers and their failures against LASK, Ferencvaros and Benfica.

As if beating up Hearts made it all better. The message, after the goal deluge, was 'what are you saying to it now?' The answer: Exactly as we were saying before.

O'Neill said on Thursday that there are still loads of points to play for - and there are. But it was a claim made from a position of serious weakness. Desperation, almost.

"A bit of adversity and the lights go out"

Even at their best in recent decades, Celtic have struggled awfully to win European games on the road. Right now, that looks way beyond them. Winning at home is too much for them these days, too.

So, yes, there are enough points on offer for Celtic to fight their way out of their Europa League dungeon and into the play-off, but in their current state it's more a philosophical thought than an attainable reality.

If you have lost three out of three, have scored three goals and have conceded nine, then banging on about points you might win down the line is an exercise in wishful thinking.

In the here and now, Celtic's standards are plummeting. That's on everybody.

It's on O'Neill and his players, though it's easy to have some sympathy for the manager when he not only has to field questions about the stuff he's responsible for but also come up with answers about stuff that he has no responsibility for - the ongoing circus that is Celtic's relationship with large elements of their own support.

And the blame is also, in greater part, on a board who are happy to have O'Neill and - when he's not around - Shaun Maloney, talking about the disconnect between fans and club, as was the case on Wednesday.

No chief executive, no chairman, no major shareholder and no major shareholder's son are anywhere to be seen when those issues are raised at news conferences. Just a manager or an assistant who, let's face it, have a few other things on their mind.

Just after the television coverage ended on Thursday there was a programme of highlights of big Celtic Park Champions League nights. Remember them?

Celtic beating the Manchester United of Paul Scholes, Ryan Giggs, Cristiano Ronaldo and Wayne Rooney; Celtic beating the AC Milan of Alessandro Nesta, Andrea Pirlo, Clarence Seedorf and Kaka; Celtic beating the Barcelona of Jordi Alba, Xavi, Iniesta and Lionel Messi.

A stadium literally shaking to its very foundations. Packed solid. Deafening. Unforgettable.

Look at it now. Empty seats and people leaving early. A game against the eighth best team in Spain, in a second-tier European competition - defeat and apathy. How they have fallen.

Of course, it might seem a little tiresome looking back at how things were 20 years ago, so let's not. Let's look at how things were in February last year.

Bayern Munich versus Celtic. Ninety-three minutes gone in the second leg in Germany and it's 2-2 on aggregate.

Bayern have started with a front four of Michael Olise, Jamal Musiala, Serge Gnabry and Harry Kane. Thomas Muller has replaced Musiala, Leroy Sane has come on for Gnabry, Kingsley Coman for Kane.

It took Bayern until the fourth minute of added time to see Celtic off. From there to here is some descent into European mediocrity and irrelevance.

'We have what we have' - O'Neill speaks after Celta Vigo collapse

O'Neill said that he "can't ask anything more" from his players. He can - and he must.

More must be asked of O'Neill and more - much, much more - must be asked of the board. Bringing in new players and a new manager is easily done, if they so wish. Whether it's the right players and the right manager is a different story, but it can be done in a heartbeat.

Changing a board, and a way of thinking, is a whole lot harder. There's little doubt all of these people want success for Celtic. They might get more of it domestically. They might win another league, another cup, maybe two. That is all highly possible, if not probable.

This question has been asked so many times now - and it's grim that it still has to be asked. What do Celtic stand for? Flat-track bullydom or something more, something more adventurous, something in keeping with the 'great European story' they so often talk about.

Because of the Lisbon Lions it is and always will be the greatest of all Scottish football stories. Nothing is ever likely to surpass it.

But there are other stories, in the present day, that are tiresome for Celtic folk, stories that are playing out now, almost on a loop. From Champions League to Europa League. What's next? Conference League. And after that?

Everyone in power at the club cannot look at that 36-team table and not feel an enormous sense of embarrassment. If they don't, they're really not in the right job.

The question is - do they have any kind of vision to make it better? Many observers of the club might see that as a rhetorical question.

#777. Scientists discover that male and female skin play by different rules

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Female mice heal skin wounds faster than males, and scientists have uncovered a surprising biological difference that may help explain why. By watching skin stem cells in living mice for five months, researchers found that female cells work together in a more coordinated way, while male cells tend to behave more independently. These differences are regulated by sex hormones and could eventually help scientists develop more personalized treatments for wound healing and skin diseases.

The discovery comes from researchers at the Université libre de Bruxelles (ULB), working with scientists at the University of Cambridge. Their findings suggest that biological sex influences how skin renews and repairs itself, even though the skin is not part of the reproductive system.

Historically, much of the research into biological differences between males and females has focused on reproductive organs. However, sex can also influence other tissues and organs throughout the body. Understanding these differences could be important for improving the prevention and treatment of inflammatory diseases and cancer, yet researchers have often overlooked their significance.

Published in Nature, the study was led by Prof. Cédric Blanpain, MD/PhD, an investigator at the WEL Research Institute, director of the Laboratory of Stem Cells and Cancer, and professor at the Université libre de Bruxelles. The research was conducted in collaboration with Prof. Benjamin Simons and his team at the University of Cambridge, along with scientists from several other institutions.

Using an advanced microscopy technique, the researchers were able to record individual cells through the skin of living mice. This allowed them to follow the same stem cells and their descendants for five months, an unprecedented period of observation. Although their original goal was to understand the mechanisms behind skin renewal, the team unexpectedly discovered striking differences between females and males. Further experiments revealed that sex hormones were responsible.

The skin's outermost layer is constantly replacing itself. This process begins with stem cells located at the bottom of the layer, where they divide to produce new cells. Some of the resulting "daughter" cells retain their ability to function as stem cells, while others begin developing into specialized cells.

These specialized cells form the protective barrier that shields the body from its surroundings. As they mature, they lose the ability to divide (a process called differentiation), gradually move toward the surface, and are eventually shed.

Scientists have debated the precise organization of this renewal process for decades. By repeatedly observing the same areas of skin over five months, the researchers found that stem cells do not immediately become fully mature skin cells.

Instead, they pass through an intermediate stage in which they can divide only a limited number of times before completing their development into specialized cells. This finding helps clarify a long-standing question about how the skin maintains itself.

Female Skin Cells Work in Surprising Synchronization

One of the most unexpected findings involved the behavior of newly formed cells.

In female mice, two "sister" cells produced by the division of a single parent cell frequently make the same developmental decision at the same time. Both may divide again, or both may begin specializing into mature skin cells.

Their behavior resembles two rowers moving their oars in unison. In male mice, however, sister cells are less closely synchronized and tend to follow more independent patterns.

"We initially set out to understand how the skin renews itself, and whether there is a hierarchy with stem cells at the top. We were surprised to discover strong differences in the way stem cells renew the skin in females and males," explains Doctor Dan Engelman, the first author of the study.

Sex Hormones Control How Skin Cells Behave

To determine what caused these differences, the scientists investigated the influence of male and female sex hormones.

They first examined what happened when male mice were castrated, removing the primary source of testosterone. Without this hormone, their skin cells began behaving more like those in females. When the researchers administered dihydrotestosterone (DHT), an active form of testosterone, the cells returned to their typical male pattern.

These experiments showed that male sex hormones help regulate how rapidly skin cells mature and move out of the stem cell layer.

The team also investigated the role of female sex hormones. Removing the ovaries (ovariectomy) reduced the rate at which stem cells multiplied and almost completely eliminated the characteristic synchronization between sister cells.

Treatment with estradiol, a form of estrogen, reversed the reduction in stem cell proliferation.

Together, these findings revealed that testosterone, which is generally more abundant in males, influences the speed of skin cell maturation. Estrogens, which are generally more abundant in females, promote stem cell proliferation and help coordinate the behavior of newly produced cells.

The researchers also tested testosterone on human skin reconstructed in the laboratory. They found that the hormone accelerated stem cell differentiation, suggesting that at least part of the mechanism observed in mice may also operate in humans.

Why Skin Wounds Heal Faster in Female Mice

The differences in stem cell behavior were accompanied by another important finding: female mice recovered more quickly from skin injuries.

When researchers examined wounds on the animals' backs, they found that wounds closed substantially faster in females, particularly during the first few days after injury.

This early advantage was partly associated with skin stem cells becoming active sooner and working in a more coordinated manner. As healing progressed, the difference between males and females gradually became smaller.

The results suggest that the hormonal regulation of stem cells may help explain why skin repair differs between the sexes.

What the Discovery Could Mean for Wound Healing and Skin Cancer

Doctors have long recognized differences between men and women in wound healing, the severity of certain inflammatory conditions, and the frequency of some skin cancers. However, the underlying biological explanations have remained incomplete.

By identifying how sex hormones influence stem cell activity, the new research provides a possible explanation for some of these observations.

The findings could eventually contribute to medical treatments that account for a patient's sex. Potential applications include improving recovery from burns and surgery, as well as treating chronic wounds in people with diabetes, a problem affecting millions worldwide.

Understanding these differences may also help researchers develop new approaches to preventing skin cancer, one of the most common forms of cancer.

For now, faster wound healing and coordinated stem cell behavior were demonstrated in mice. Additional research will be needed to determine how closely these processes match those in human skin and whether they can be used to improve medical care.

Beyond their potential clinical importance, the findings also provide new insight into the fundamental process of skin renewal, helping address a question that has challenged scientists for decades.

"This study is a technological tour de force, and the discovery of differences between male and female skin stem cells is fascinating and entirely unexpected. It sheds light on a series of clinical observations that previously lacked a biological explanation. It opens up a wide range of new questions, spanning from fundamental stem cell biology to clinical issues such as wound healing, cancer development, and autoimmune diseases. Sex is a parameter too often overlooked in biology and medicine. We will now investigate the differences between males and females during the formation and progression of skin cancers, as well as the molecular mechanisms responsible for these differences," says Prof. Cédric Blanpain, who led the study.

The project brought together Prof. Blanpain's team (ULB, Belgium) and Prof. Simons' group (University of Cambridge, UK), along with research teams led by Michele De Luca (University of Modena and Reggio Emilia, Italia), Yulia Shwartz (Broad CIRM Center, University of Southern California, Los Angeles, US) and Esther Hoste (VIB-Ghent University, Belgium).

On Oct. 19, 2016, the European Space Agency's ExoMars mission arrived at Mars!

ExoMars 2016 consists of the Trace Gas Orbiter and the Schiaparelli lander. The orbiter and lander traveled there together and separated on Oct. 16, three days before the orbiter entered orbit around Mars, and the lander touched down on the planet's surface. But something went wrong during the lander's descent, and it crashed into the ground.

Schiaparelli did not survive the accident, but the Trace Gas Orbiter is still functional. It is currently circling the planet, looking for methane and other gases in the Martian atmosphere that could be evidence for possible biological activity.

ExoMars 2016 was a landmark mission for the European Space Agency and designed to be the vanguard of a two-flight ExoMars program to send an orbiter, lander and rover to Mars.

Launched in March 2016, ExoMars 2016 aimed to achieve two goals for ESA and Russia. First, the mission aimed to use the Schiaparelli lander to test landing techniques that would be vital on a planned ExoMars 2018 rover. Second, the mission would deliver a critical orbiter around Mars to study the planet's atmosphere and serve as a communications link for that future Mars rover.

In the end, ExoMars 2016 was just a partial success, with ESA officials calling it "96% successful." due to the loss of the lander.

While Schiaparelli separated smoothly from the ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter spacecraft, it stopped communicating with Earth just 1 minute before its planned landing on Oct. 19. Photos from NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter at Mars later revealed what appeared to be its crash site and, nearby, its jettisoned parachute. Officials suspect Schiaparelli did not fire its descent thrusters as designed in order to slow itself enough for a safe landing. A data glitch was cited as its cause.

The Trace Gas Orbiter, meanwhile, continues to study Mars to this day and has spent 10 years and counting studying the Martian atmosphere.

The launch of ExoMars 2018, which includes the Mars rover Rosalind Franklin, was delayed by the Schiaparelli failure, as well as the loss of its Russian Proton rocket when ESA severed its partnership with Russia over that country's war on Ukraine.

The ExoMars rover is now expected to launch no earlier than 2028, with a landing on Mars expected in 2030.

In 2026, another country took aim at Mars on this day.

The Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency attempted to launch the ambitious Martian Moons eXploration mission (called MMX) to land a probe on the Mars moon Phobos and return samples to Earth.

#772. Germany's China calculation shifts as import pressures eclipse export benefits

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Germany has long resisted tougher action against China, fearing retaliation. But rising pressure on key industries has pushed Berlin to seek stronger EU trade-defense powers to protect its industrial base.

Germany's cautious approach toward Beijing is cracking. For years, Berlin blocked a harder line on what the European Union describes as China's  unfair trade practices.

In the last few months, that caution — a fear of Chinese retaliation that could hurt German exporters — has given way to a willingness to escalate.

Last week, Chancellor Friedrich Merz and French President Emmanuel Macron wrote jointly to European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen seeking a new tool to cut a trading partner off from the EU's single market if unfair practices continue.

Clearly aimed at Beijing, the letter warned the measure was necessary against countries that "deliberately seek to undermine ... fair market conditions by political or economic means."

China's Commerce Ministry warned Europe would "suffer the consequences" if it adopted such a "kill-switch" plan, which was timed to coincide with crucial EU-China trade talks in Beijing last week.

Noah Barkin, a senior adviser with research consultancy Rhodium Group's China practice, told DW that the threats to Germany's export powerhouse "have convinced Merz and his closest advisers that the benefits of action outweigh the costs of inaction."

Before the COVID pandemic, the two economies rose in tandem and German industry benefited from China's ascent.

Since 2021, however, China has fallen from Germany's second-largest export market to its ninth, according to data from Germany Trade & Invest for the first half of 2026.

Chinese competitors have quickly closed the technology gap and benefit from much lower costs and state subsidies. That pressure is being felt especially in Germany's automaking, precision engineering and chemicals industries.

German vehicle makers have suffered a large sales drop in China, which was once their second-largest market. According to data from Rhodium Group, German car exports to China plummeted by 66% between 2022 and 2025.

Big German brands now face heavy rivalry from a new wave of Chinese electric vehicle (EV) manufactures in other major markets, including Europe, which is forcing a wave of layoffs and possible factory closures.

In Germany alone, imports of new Chinese-made cars rose 121% in the first seven months of the year, according to Destatis, the federal statistics agency. Overall, Chinese-made vehicles accounted for nearly 14% of total vehicle imports.

"After continuously losing market share in China ... it [the rivalry] seems to be hitting their business within the European automotive market," Andreas Mischer, an associate fellow at the Berlin-based Mercator Institute for China Studies (MERICS), told DW.

Mischer said pressure from Germany's powerful automotive lobby was key to Berlin's "shifting attitude toward trade defense instruments."

Among the many German business leaders who have demanded a more hard-line approach in recent months, Oliver Richtberg, head of foreign trade at VDMA, Germany's mechanical engineering association, was perhaps the most blunt:

"We are currently being deindustrialized through unfair measures — and most companies are no longer willing to accept this," Richtberg told Welt am Sonntag newspaper in August.

According to Eurostat, the wider EU's goods deficit with China was around €360 billion ($402 billion) last year — effectively €1 billion per day — and widened again in the first half of 2026.

While Brussels has been pushing back against the dumping of cheap Chinese goods, a separate industrial protection law and other anti-coercion measures are running into trouble from Beijing and at home.

The EU's Industrial Accelerator Act, proposed in March, aims to pull manufacturing investment back to Europe by favoring EU-based firms in public contracts and subsidies.

Foreign investments in key sectors would only be approved if European partners keep majority control, which Beijing has framed as discriminatory.

The European Automobile Manufacturers' Association (ACEA) warned last month that the draft act does little to counter the threat from cheaper Chinese rivals. In a position paper, it said the incentive to produce within the bloc has to be large enough to "cancel out the extra cost of producing locally."

Berlin's chief concern was that China could place new export curbs on rare earth minerals and the magnets made from them. China has a stranglehold on these elements, which are essential for German automotive, renewables and defense manufacturing.

Bloomberg last week cited unnamed German officials as saying that a new EU kill-switch could be a "second-strike weapon" if Beijing blocked those exports. Brussels is also considering unilateral limits on how many hybrid cars China can export to the EU, according to the same Bloomberg report.

Perhaps to avoid such an open confrontation, the EU and China reached a "shared understanding" last week at the EU-China trade talks in Beijing that could cut Chinese exports of hybrid and plug-in hybrid vehicles to the European Union by more than half, according to EU Trade Commissioner Maros Sefcovic, who headed the European delegation.

Reports say the agreement would cap such Chinese imports at 350,000 vehicles annually, with the limit increasing by 6% each year over four years.

Overall, new hybrid-electric cars in the EU reached nearly 37% of all new cars registered from January to August, according to ACEA data.

MERICS, meanwhile, calculated that the EU is a more critical export market for China than the US. In the first half of the year, Chinese exports to the EU were $254 billion (€354 billion), versus $172 billion to the US.

This could give EU negotiators a firmer hand as they seek to avoid a trade war while protecting Europe from China's latest export glut.

"The EU has more leverage than it sometimes thinks," MERICS' Mischer told DW.

#770. A Talent for Murder review: Alden Ehrenreich gives sexy menace in this Patricia Highsmith-inspired thriller

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Carol. Deep Water. Strangers on a Train. The Talented Mr. Ripley. American author Patricia Highsmith has inspired filmmakers for decades with her lurid tales of sex, deception, and murder. Now, she herself is a character in such a thriller, thanks to A Talent For Murder.

Adapted for the screen by Australian playwright Joanna Murray-Smith from her own play, Switzerland, this film imagines what might have happened if Highsmith's biggest fan got the chance to meet his hero. Enter Edward Ridgeway (Tony winner Alden Ehrenreich), a handsome young man with carefully coiffed dark hair, a strong jaw, a pressed shirt, and polished glasses, but little poise. On a train through Switzerland, he seems like he stumbled out of the first chapter of a Tom Ripley novel, sure to be felled or corrupted by the iconic con man.

Now, the girl. Clementine Harriet Balfour (Olivia Cooke) is pretty, with a bouncy brunette bob, and a fashion sense that hints at an old-money family and independent ambitions. They meet as strangers on a train, but are sure to cross paths again in the small Swiss village where she's visiting a miserly dowager aunt (Juliet Stevenson), and he's tasked with getting Highsmith to sign a contract to write a sixth Ripley novel.

Director Anton Corbijn expertly lays out a spring-loaded trap that's sure to capture the interest of Highsmith fans. A handsome couple flirts, while wealth and opportunity glitter on the horizon. What might they do to get there?

Enter a surly Highsmith, played with gnarly certainty by the one and only Helen Mirren.

The English Dame has done Shakespeare and Prime Suspect; she's played Queen Elizabeth II and a Fast and Furious baddie with equal aplomb. Plus, just last year, she played a sultry spy in the cozy crime treat The Thursday Murder Club. But there's no lightness in Mirren's Highsmith.

In 1995, atop a Swiss mountain, the heralded author has become an eccentric recluse, rumored to have poisoned a neighbor's dog for barking too much, and to have driven an American man so mad he fled her home while only wearing his pajamas. Dressed in men's slacks, a crisp men's dress shirt, a cravat and vest, paired with a fuck-off bob, Mirren's Highsmith is imposing, even introduced ironing a shirt.

When Edward appears, eager with compliments, gifts, and talk of opportunity, she regards him with such scorching disdain the audience at the Toronto International Film Festival gasped and guffawed. The ruthless wit of the Ripley novels is sharply replicated in Murray-Smith's screenplay, and Mirren makes the most of every line, savoring the sharp barbs. To his credit, Ehrenreich doesn't balk in the face of this storied actress. And thank the gods, because failing to match her onscreen would make A Talent for Murder a miss. Instead, it's a twisted thrill: sexy, salty, and wickedly fun.

Earlier this year, Ehrenreich earned praise and a Tony award for his phenomenal performance in the Broadway revival of Becky Shaw. Watching A Talent for Murder, I thought often of this stage performance, as his characters have a lot in common. Young men who've come from a family with no means and no reputation worth a damn, who both cozy up to a wealthy family, determined not just to enter their world but to rule it.

At first glance, Edward looks like Matt Damon's version of Ripley, from the juicy 1999 adaptation The Talented Mr. Ripley. His hair floppy but fashionable. His smile earnest but awkward. The eyeglasses, a perfect twin. This feels intentional, as if Corbijn wants Highsmith fans to immediately wonder if this eager social climber might have the root of evil in him, which could make Highsmith's criminal mastermind real.

Smartly, the screenplay doesn't pitch Edward as some rube. He's come ready to impress Highsmith, even if that means tantalizing her with twisted tales from his past, and the possibility of criminally corrupting him. Enticed by his game, the two begin to collaborate on a murder mystery about a blue-collar boy who meets an old-money girl, and is determined to make her family fortune his own. Their thought experiment gets joltingly real as Highsmith haunts him in the night and inspires him to treat the reckless heiress Clementine as source material.

From there, A Talent for Murder begins to blur what's real and imagined in pursuit of bringing fresh blood to Highsmith's typewriter. And it just wouldn't work if Ehrenreich weren't on fire. Where Mirren is delicious in her snarling cruelty (think Tilda Swinton in Problemista), Ehrenreich is intriguing in his slipperiness. Edward and Highsmith's imagined novel plays out as we watch, recasting the former as her anti-hero, with Clementine playing the role they write for her. In these sections, Ehrenreich has the smoothness of Ripley, his confidence edged with a misanthropic disgust at all who aren't as clever and calculating as he. But as Edward, Ehrenreich trembles with insecurity, particularly in a scene where he's delicately called out by an Irish lady (Brid Brennan), who spots him as an outsider from his shoes alone.

To her credit, Cooke is not outshone in the slim role of Clementine. Whether the imagined version where she is a yearning virgin or the real one, where she is a spoiled rich girl looking for a cheap thrill, she brings a full-blooded verve to this girl. Together, this trio weaves a tale of sex, death, and desire that touches on the best of Highsmith's works, without feeling like a shallow retread of them. Instead, A Talent for Murder plays like a superb fan fiction, imagining the inner world and grisly desires of the seminal author, without idolizing her or whitewashing her well-documented defects.

A Talent For Murder is darkly funny, freshly thrilling, and unrepentantly seductive. It's not a Ripley sequel, but it's damn close.

“It was almost as if I had to hide myself to be able to do what I love to do,” he says. “It wasn’t a very nice feeling, having to worry about getting from A to B. It was just part of my world. I had to be more aware of my surroundings and what I was doing.”

Ameobi’s “A” was often the family home, situated within a few pitch lengths of Newcastle’s St James’ Park, and “B” was frequently the club’s base at the time in Benwell, where he would head for academy training.

“It built incredible resilience in me,” he says. “That really helped me in a sporting context in my career, but also as a person, understanding that, irrespective of what people thought of me, what really mattered was the value I put on myself.”

#768. Scientists uncover 3 key brain pathways linked to lasting depression relief

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Scientists at Mount Sinai have identified three important brain connections associated with long-term improvement in patients receiving deep brain stimulation (DBS) for severe depression that has not responded to conventional treatments. The discovery could help researchers make this experimental therapy more precise and effective.

Published October 1 in the American Journal of Psychiatry, the research examined the largest group of patients assembled across multiple independent cohorts to date who had received subcallosal cingulate deep brain stimulation (SCC DBS) for treatment-resistant depression.

The researchers found that lasting improvements were consistently associated with electrical stimulation reaching three specific white matter pathways: the cingulum bundle, forceps minor, and medial uncinate fasciculus/ventral amygdalofugal pathways.

These findings strengthen the case for using detailed maps of the brain's connections to determine exactly where electrodes should be implanted, rather than choosing their positions based primarily on anatomical landmarks. Known as connectome-based targeting, this strategy has already helped shape the ongoing Abbott TRANSCEND clinical trial, a randomized, controlled study that uses individualized brain connectivity maps to guide SCC DBS treatment.

"This study underscores the importance of individualized surgical targeting for achieving optimal outcomes with deep brain stimulation for depression," said Helen Mayberg, MD, Mount Sinai Professor in Neurotherapeutics and founding Director of the Nash Family Center for Advanced Circuit Therapeutics at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai. "In any randomized controlled trial of a neuromodulation treatment, precise lead placement and confirmation that the intended brain target has been engaged are essential first steps. We still have much to learn about which patients are most likely to benefit from stimulation of this particular target, but developing imaging-based signatures that can match patients to their optimal treatment remains a central goal of our research."

How Deep Brain Stimulation Targets Severe Depression

For people with treatment-resistant depression, standard therapies have failed to provide adequate relief. Researchers are investigating deep brain stimulation as a possible treatment for individuals living with severe, persistent symptoms.

The procedure involves surgically placing electrodes inside the brain to deliver controlled electrical signals to circuits involved in depression. However, positioning the electrodes correctly remains one of the greatest challenges.

Electrical stimulation does not simply affect the brain tissue closest to an implanted electrode. It can also influence larger networks through the white matter pathways that connect different brain regions. As a result, two patients with electrodes implanted in nearly identical locations may experience very different outcomes depending on which connections are actually stimulated.

Earlier research from Mount Sinai and Emory University helped establish the importance of these networks. In studies published between 2014 and 2023 in Biological Psychiatry, Molecular Psychiatry, and Nature, the teams identified white matter pathways associated with successful SCC DBS treatment and developed a method for planning electrode placement using brain connectivity maps.

The latest investigation sought to determine whether those connections were also important in patients treated at other research centers. Scientists also wanted to understand whether differences in the pathways reached by stimulation could help account for patients who did not benefit.

Three Brain Connections Linked to Lasting Improvement

To investigate, the researchers examined information from several clinical studies, including the BROADEN trial and a patient cohort at the University of Toronto, where SCC DBS was originally developed.

They used computer models of the electrical fields generated by implanted electrodes alongside maps of brain connectivity. This allowed them to estimate which pathways received stimulation and compare those patterns across different patient groups.

The results showed a consistent relationship between sustained improvement and stimulation of the cingulum bundle, forceps minor, and medial uncinate fasciculus/ventral amygdalofugal pathways.

One finding was especially notable. Across the study sites, patients who had not responded to treatment after two years showed a lack of stimulation engagement with the left cingulum bundle.

The researchers did not investigate whether moving electrodes to reach this pathway would improve those patients' symptoms. Nevertheless, the pattern offers a possible explanation for why apparently similar electrode placements can produce different results. An electrode might be positioned in the correct general region but still fail to activate all the connections associated with lasting improvement.

"This study addresses an important question in DBS: not simply which area of the brain to stimulate, but which connections within that area need to be engaged," said Brian Kopell, MD, Director of the Center for Neuromodulation at Mount Sinai. "For a network disorder such as depression, millimeters can determine whether stimulation reaches the pathways we intend to modulate. Identifying and reproducibly engaging those pathways gives us a more precise surgical target and a framework for understanding why otherwise similar electrode placements may produce different clinical outcomes."

Toward More Personalized Depression Treatments

Although reaching these three pathways appears important, the researchers emphasize that electrode placement alone is unlikely to determine whether a patient improves.

The investigation concentrated on identifying the brain connections that should receive stimulation. Additional studies are needed to determine which individuals are most likely to benefit from SCC DBS and how electrical stimulation should be adjusted as treatment continues.

There were also limitations to the brain imaging data. Individual diffusion MRI scans, which can provide information about a person's white matter connections, were not available for every participant. Consequently, much of the analysis used generalized maps of brain connectivity rather than maps created specifically for each patient.

For the Emory patient group, the researchers were able to compare results obtained from generalized connectivity maps with those based on individual diffusion imaging. Both approaches produced consistent findings, providing further support for the importance of the identified pathways. However, more research is needed to establish the value of patient-specific imaging.

Future investigations will explore whether additional brain imaging measurements, including white matter integrity and functional connectivity, can help identify suitable patients, improve electrode placement, and guide treatment settings.

"Our findings show precisely engaging these white matter pathways is a critical first step toward sustained response," said Ki Sueng Choi, PhD, Associate Professor, Imaging Core Leader at the Center for Advanced Circuit Therapeutics at Mount Sinai, and corresponding author of the study. "The next step is to characterize abnormal functional and structural brain states along these pathways to build more detailed models for setting patient-specific stimulation parameters, including amplitude, frequency, and pulse width, for precision medicine."

By combining increasingly detailed brain maps with personalized stimulation settings, scientists hope to better understand why some people experience lasting relief while others do not. The findings represent another step toward developing more targeted treatments for severe depression, although further clinical research is necessary to establish how much these approaches can improve outcomes.

#761. The Only Living Pickpocket in New York review: John Turturro gets the movie he deserves

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

John Turturro has a storied career that stretches across 45 years, and boasts unforgettable performances in iconic films like Spike Lee's Do the Right Thing and the Coen Bros' The Big Lebowski and O Brother, Where Art Thou? More recently, he awed streaming audiences with his bittersweet romance as Irving Bailiff in Apple TV+'s Severance, for which he's earned two Primetime Emmy nominations.

Across more than 100 films and television series, Turturro has played clowns and villains, lovers and bowlers. But in his latest, The Only Living Pickpocket in New York, this heralded character actor and celebrated New York City native gets the role that he was born to play.

Writer/director Noah Segan entrusts Turturro with the leading man role in a crime thriller that evokes 1970s classics like Taxi Driver, The French Connection, and The Taking of Pelham One Two Three — movies that predate Turturro's screen career. And yet, The Only Living Pickpocket in New York doesn't feel like a throwback. Instead, this big-hearted and hard-nosed thriller bridges the gap between past and present with a modern awareness of how things change, even for a classic New York character.

It's a cliché to call a movie a love letter to New York. The Only Living Pickpocket in New York is more of a confession of affection scribbled on the back of a way-too-long CVS receipt. It's passionate and a bit grimy, just like the city itself.

Turturro stars as Harry Lehman, an aging Bronx resident who's been a career criminal specializing in pickpocketing for decades. This day begins as any other, lifting a wallet from a finance bro on the subway. But times have changed. The city has moved away from paper money and toward Apple Pay and cryptocurrency. So, it's harder than ever for Harry to provide for his paralyzed wife, Rosie (Karina Arroyave). So, it seems like a boon when he successfully boosts the wallet of a smug 20-something named Dylan (Will Price). But while Harry is fencing the goods to his pawnbroker buddy Ben (a pitch-perfect Steve Buscemi), he learns this mark is practically made. Okay, so he's more of a nepobaby-gangster than a goodfella. But this is still bad news.

Harry's got to hustle to get back what he sold, or else Dylan and his posse of puffed-up Gen Z goons will kill Rosie. In this quest, Harry rides the train from the Bronx to Chinatown, to Sheepshead Bay in Brooklyn, and up into cozy Queens, where big houses and yards shake up the presumed urban landscape. (Sorry, Staten Island, no time for you!)

In each place, Harry collides with a quintessentially New York figure, be it a no-nonsense neighbor (Echaka Agba), a shop owner (Kelvin Han Yee) more focused on his TV than a pesky customer, a wise-cracking detective (Giancarlo Esposito), a finger-wagging grannie (Lori Tan Chinn), or a slick-fingered could-be protégé (Mark Cayne). Like in classic '70s New York City cinema, each of these folks adds a new texture to this portrait of this messy and magical city. The cinematography by Sam Levy showcases this setting with a understated wonder B-roll of rumbling subways and bustling streets reflects Harry's never-say-die energy, as he strides out of pawn shops, police precincts, and community centers with an easy confidence. Before he dashes off, Levy's camera will zoom in, catching him as if he were a native animal on the run. Or else, a simple pan as Harry pauses to think reflects the big, urban world around him that won't stop for a moment.

This is not the romantic landscape of Manhattan showcased in countless rom-coms. The only view of the skyline or even NYC's rivers in this film is tinged with a sinister tone, as Harry and his detective friend recall an old thief whose head was found "bobbing in the Hudson!" The Only Living Pickpocket in New York instead captures a city that is both repulsive and beautiful, heartbreaking and inspiring. Segan even kicks off with a gut-punch of an opener, playing LCD Soundsystem's "New York I Love You, But You're Bringing Me Down" at such an intensity that you can't escape the raw vulnerability in its lyrics or the trembling voice of James Murphy.

Before the title card hits, Segan shows he understands this city like a New Yorker. This is a place that will build you up and knock you down, over and over. And somehow — for those of us who truly love it — it feels like a privilege every time you get back up to try again.

This is a story of a man who's lived past his prime. A brassy score of trumpets and plinking piano puts Harry Lehman in the space of Travis Bickle, Popeye Doyle, or Transit Police Lt. Zachary Garber. But Segan's film willfully lacks the intensity of these reference points. That's because the Fear City days are behind New York, and Harry's hard edge has softened over the years of caring for his wife.

Harry, Rosie, Ben, and even Detective Warren are relics of a "bad days" era that they miss, because the days were rough, but the wild nights still make them smile. Now, kids half their age are talking about how the only worthwhile terrain for theft is through computers. But Harry doesn't even have a cell phone. A friendly stranger marvels when he knows a phone number "by heart." A crypto-bro chides him for not understanding he's part of "planned obsolescence." Over and over, the youth of this city are telling Harry that is time is over. There's a quiet resignation in his response, but even that doesn't mean he'll go down without a fight.

Turturro, who has famously seethed, snarled, and caterwauled to dramatic and comedic effect, plays closer to the vest here. When not lifting wallets, his long, agile fingers hide in the pockets of his tweed overcoat, its salt-and-pepper herringbone mirroring the color of his silvered, curly hair. Harry strives to not stand out, all the better to ply his craft. But he is coolly dashing. And in that, he has a facade of composure. But cuddled next to his wife, his passion burns. For her, he will coo and dance, showing that this old man still has moves. In front of old friends, he is gruff but caring. To their grandchildren, he is warm and paternal. And when confronted by mercurial thugs, he is alert and patient. Turturro offers a phenomenally nuanced performance that subtly speaks to what it takes to survive decades in this city.

When the Knicks won the NBA Championship this year, Turturro went viral for a dance he did at the celebration parade, candidly caught on camera. I thought of that scene throughout The Only Living Pickpocket in New York, not because his genuine joy in that moment is reflected in the movie, but because in both of these on-camera moments, Turturro succinctly captures what it is to be a New Yorker. It is grappling with grief and joy in the same crazy day. It is making a family of the misfits you treasure. It is embracing this city — and the outer boroughs — with appreciation and respect. It is building a life few can understand, and frankly, they don't need to. This is a space for all kinds of dreams, all kinds of people, and all kinds of ways to lose and win.

As a crime thriller, The Only Living Pickpocket in New York is a solidly entertaining ride, being sharply paced, thoughtfully shot, and stacked with a terrific cast that also includes Tatiana Maslany. But more than just another fun crime movie set in New York City, this one is also resolutely aware of the greats who came before, paying tribute without aping them. And best of all, Turturro offers a turn that showcases his humor, sexiness, pathos, and a cutting subtlety that's absolutely riveting. In a career full of outstanding performances, The Only Living Pickpocket in New York offers one of Turturro's very best.
