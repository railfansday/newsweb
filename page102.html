#1629. Body-clock disruptions contribute to one in four cases of depression, suggests large study

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Disruptions to the body’s sleep–wake cycle can trigger ‘circadian depression’ in some people, say researchers.Credit: Viktoriya Skorikova/Getty

One in four people with depression could have ‘circadian depression’, in which disruptions to the body’s internal clock contribute to a person’s mental illness, according to a large population and genetics study1 published today in the British Journal of Psychiatry.

The study found that people with circadian depression, which is characterized by disrupted sleep, low energy and reduced physical activity during the day, tended to have more severe symptoms that start at a younger age than those with other forms of depression. They also tended to be female and less likely to respond to conventional antidepressants.

Researchers in the field say that although the findings add to clinicians’ understanding of the possible causes of depression, they are preliminary and more evidence is needed before circadian depression can be formally recognized as a disorder or treatment guidelines are changed.

Major depressive disorder is the second most common mental-health disorder globally, but its severity and response to treatment can vary substantially between individuals. Although research has sought the underlying mechanisms to explain these differences, this has had a limited impact on prognosis and treatment so far, say the authors.

Previous studies have linked a disrupted circadian rhythm to depression2. The 24-hour internal clock, which is controlled in the brain, regulates the sleep–wake cycle, hormones and digestion. A disrupted body clock can make people feel jet-lagged, gain weight, and have poor concentration and sleep. The latest study sought to establish circadian depression as a subtype and identify key symptoms.

The study has taken the field “one step further” to uncovering the underlying biological mechanisms in some people with depression, says Colleen Loo, a clinical psychiatrist and researcher at the University of New South Wales in Sydney, Australia. “It’s a really useful contribution to the science of understanding depression,” she says.

The study of 11,380 people of European ancestry with severe depression found that nearly one-quarter — 2604 people — met at least three of the study’s six proposed criteria for circadian depression. These include excessive daytime sleepiness, feeling tired in the morning and preferring to stay up late. Seasonality (when changes in light and the length of the day affect mood), delayed sleep and social jet lag (a mismatch between a person’s biological clock and their social schedule) were also proposed criteria. The researchers determined that meeting at least three would classify a person as having this type of depression.

The team found that circadian depression was associated with higher genetic risk factors for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder and other mood disorders, such as anxiety and major depressive disorder. Co-author Emiliana Tonini, a circadian researcher at the University of Sydney, says that this could mean that people with circadian depression are more likely to have their depression symptoms triggered by disruptions to their circadian rhythm or sleep–wake cycle changes, such as after daylight saving time or having a child.

They also found that circadian depression was linked to an increased risk of inflammation and disrupted metabolism. Co-author Jacob Crouse, also a circadian researcher at the University of Sydney, says that the findings suggest that metabolic dysfunction could affect a person’s mood, energy levels and sleep.

Tonini says that the team hopes that identifying whether a person’s circadian rhythm is contributing to their depression will help clinicians to suggest treatments that are more likely to work. It is common for people to try multiple antidepressants before their symptoms are effectively treated — and up to 68% of people do not see an improvement in symptoms after receiving standard antidepressants3.

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Published: 00:02 BST, 22 October 2026 | Updated: 00:09 BST, 22 October 2026

Neil Lennon has raised concerns about the psychological welfare of modern-day football managers amid the ‘instant’ and ‘over the top’ scrutiny of their role.

The 55-year-old Dunfermline boss questioned whether those in his profession are receiving enough mental health support as they strive to deliver results while dealing with criticism, abuse and the ever-present threat of being sacked.

‘I do worry about managers sometimes, are they getting enough support, because they are thrown out there, we bare our soul every weekend in front of the public,’ said former Celtic and Hibernian boss Lennon, speaking to Dr Jagdish Basra on Unmasked, a podcast by Glasgow-based psychiatry practice Diverse Diagnostics.

‘That’s our livelihood, our team is a personification of us. If they do well, yeah, you get a pat on the back. If you don’t do well then the abuse, the criticism comes.

‘And a lot of it is wrong. A lot of it is way over the top now in this day and age. And a lot of it is instant.’

Lennon, who has spoken previously about his own experiences of depression, believes there needs to be more focus on the wellbeing of managers.

Dunfermline boss Neil Lennon believes criticism of managers is 'over the top'

Lennon has taken advice from his former Celtic manager Martin O'Neill

The former Northern Ireland international acknowledged support for players had improved considerably over the past two decades, with clubs increasingly providing access to counsellors and psychological services. However, he is not convinced managers are receiving the same consideration. ‘In terms of looking after manager welfare, there’s not a lot up here in Scotland,’ Lennon said. ‘The League Managers Association is very good that way if you need to speak to someone or if there’s counselling or if it’s legal advice…

‘But in terms of clubs, I think that’s down to the relationship you have with your owner or your director of football or your chief executive.

‘If you’ve got a strong bond then they might see something. You might be able to have conversations behind closed doors, privately to say “I’m struggling here, I might need a bit of time, can you accommodate that?” or whatever.

‘I think that all depends on the relationship you have with the people upstairs.’

The shelf-life of a manager appears to be reducing by the season as owners and supporters become increasingly reactive to setbacks.

Lennon acknowledged that being sacked has now simply become an occupational hazard of football management as he recalled advice he received from his former boss and mentor Martin O’Neill.

‘When you first lose your job, you’re thinking, “oh, this is…” You want everything to be perfect, you want to have a clear run (without things going wrong),’ he said.

‘But Martin said to me at one stage, “there’s one thing you’re guaranteed about this job, Neil, you’re going to get the sack”. And then once you get the first one and you realise it’s not the end of the world, life still goes on.

‘What I’ve learned to do is find that work/life balance a lot better and that’s why I’m a lot happier and more comfortable in my own skin as a manager, but that comes with experience.’
