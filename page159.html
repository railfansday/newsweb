#490. Honey bees are bullying other bees, scientists discover

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Honey bees are proving to be formidable competitors for food in the UK, and scientists have discovered that they frequently use aggression to drive other pollinators away from flowers. New research has documented how honey bees chase, displace, and intimidate rival insects, providing the first detailed evidence of how extensively they interfere with other species' access to food.

The study, conducted by researchers at Anglia Ruskin University (ARU) and published in Ecological Entomology, followed pollinator activity over two years at five locations in and around Cambridge, England. Each site was situated within 300 meters of an established honey bee colony.

Researchers observed insects visiting bramble (Rubus fruticosus) and ivy (Hedera helix), two flowering plants that provide important supplies of nectar and pollen. Both attract large numbers of insects during their flowering periods, making them useful places to investigate competition for food.

Honey Bees Dominate the Competition for Flowers

To examine how different pollinators interact, the team divided flower visitors into five groups: honey bees, bumble bees, solitary bees, hoverflies, and an 'other' category containing butterflies, beetles, and wasps.

Over 93 hours of observation, the scientists documented encounters in which one insect interrupted another's feeding or prevented it from reaching a flower. Each interaction was classified according to its outcome. A 'win' occurred when one insect gained access to a flower at the expense of another, which experienced a 'loss' of feeding opportunities. A 'share' meant both insects were able to access the flower without conflict.

The results revealed a clear pattern. Locations with greater concentrations of honey bees generally had fewer other pollinators and experienced more competition for flowers. Honey bees also emerged victorious in a larger proportion of their encounters with other insects.

On bramble flowers, doubling the density of honey bees was associated with a 14% decrease in bumblebee abundance. On ivy, higher honey bee numbers were linked to reduced numbers of solitary bees and hoverflies.

The honey bees did not simply reach flowers before their competitors. They frequently initiated aggressive encounters, driving other insects away to secure feeding opportunities.

Other pollinators actively avoided honey bees in 28% of observed interactions on bramble and 36% of those on ivy. In at least 20% of the encounters honey bees won on either plant, they physically pushed aside or chased away competing insects.

Aggressive behavior initiated by species other than honey bees was uncommon. However, one insect stood out as an exception.

The ivy bee, a solitary species not native to Britain that was first documented in the country in 2001, was the only pollinator observed actively challenging honey bees. Unlike most other insects, ivy bees sometimes defended their feeding spots by chasing or displacing honey bees, successfully competing for access to flowers.

Climate Change Could Make Competition Worse

The findings are part of a broader research effort to understand competition among pollinators and identify ways to reduce its ecological effects.

The results also come as beekeeping continues to expand across the UK. The number of registered beekeepers increased from 19,000 in 2010 to 47,000 last year, reflecting growing numbers of managed honey bee colonies.

Researchers warn that rising temperatures could add to the pressure on wild pollinators. Their observations showed that competitive encounters became more frequent during warmer conditions.

One possible explanation involves changes in flower nectar production. When plants experience 'heat stress', they can produce less nectar, potentially forcing more insects to compete over a smaller supply of food.

Temperature also affects different pollinators in different ways. Bumblebees and hoverflies are sensitive to high temperatures and may forage more effectively in cooler weather. Honey bees, by contrast, generally become more active as temperatures rise. Previous research found that increasing the temperature by 12°C caused a tenfold increase in the number of honey bees leaving their nests.

Together, these differences suggest that continued warming could give honey bees an even greater competitive advantage over some wild pollinators.

Protecting Wild Pollinators as Honey Bee Numbers Grow

Lead author Victoria Buckle, a PhD student at Anglia Ruskin University (ARU) in Cambridge, England, emphasized that the research is intended to improve conservation efforts rather than discourage people from keeping honey bees.

"The aim of our study was not to discourage beekeeping, but to better understand how competition happens between wild and managed pollinator species with the aim of helping to conserve our wild pollinators, which play such an important ecological role.

"We found that most interactions when competing for food were instigated by honey bees and they had a greater number of 'wins', resulting in a loss of forage for other pollinators. Bumblebees, solitary bees and hoverflies would avoid, be displaced by and on some occasions be chased off by honey bees.

"An interesting exception was the ivy bee, which would win contests more often against honey bees. It is the first time, to our knowledge, such behaviors have been observed and shows ivy bees are able to 'hold their own' in such encounters.

"However, honey bees outcompeted all other wild pollinators and based on our findings, future predicted warming in the UK is only likely to further exacerbate interference competition from dominant honey bees. Therefore, it's important to find ways to ensure honey bees and wild pollinators can continue to coexist, particularly in locations with a high density of hives, such as urban areas."

Europe has dropped the ball on the prototype for its first reusable spacecraft. Or, rather, the prototype itself was dropped — on purpose — but the outcome of its fall was not what engineers had hoped for.

The incident occurred during an Oct. 6 drop test of the full-scale Space Rider model, from the European Space Agency (ESA). The robotic vehicle's initial drogue parachute deployed successfully, but its main parafoil detached during deployment, leaving the prototype unable to slow its descent before crashing into the ground, according to ESA officials. The cause of the failure remains under investigation.

"Following several successful tests using representative vehicle masses, the latest system-level drop test in October was not completed as planned due to an anomaly during the parafoil deployment sequence," an ESA spokesperson confirmed in an email to Space.com. "An independent investigation is ongoing to determine its root cause and inform the next steps."

Space Rider is being developed as Europe's first reusable orbital spacecraft — an uncrewed vehicle designed to carry scientific experiments to orbit for up to two months, then return them to Earth for analysis. Its method of return, though, differs from that of any other spacecraft in operation today. Space Rider''s wingless, lifting-body design relies on a steerable parachute, or parafoil, to maneuver toward its landing area. This allows it to perform runway-style touchdowns, as opposed to other, less accurate parachute-aided recoveries in current practice.

This month's drop test was intended to demonstrate that ability, which is a critical requirement for Space Rider's planned reuse after missions in orbit, but the impact of its failure may not end up disrupting its ultimate development timeline. According to Géraldine Naja, ESA's director of space transportation, the vehicle's other remaining qualification tests aren't holding back its path to launch.

"We hope that this test will not damage, or let's say lead us to modify, the remaining schedule and qualification activities," Naja said during an ESA Council media briefing on Oct. 22. "In any case, the remaining qualification activities are not on the critical path of the development of the Space Rider."

Naja described the incident as an unfortunate but important event. "We have had many successful tests before, but testing and validation is part of the development of a space system," she said.

The lost vehicle was a full-scale test model assembled to evaluate Space Rider's descent and landing systems. The minivan-sized mockup reproduced the size and flight characteristics of its reentry module, with avionics to control its autonomous guidance and navigation systems, but the vehicle was far from a space-ready article.

The Oct. 6 test took place at a military range on the Italian island of Sardinia, where the mockup was released from a helicopter at an altitude of approximately 2 miles (3 kilometers). According to Naja, the vehicle's initial drogue parachute triggered as expected and began slowing the Space Rider mock-up's descent before its larger, steerable parafoil was supposed to unfurl. The parafoil, however, detached during deployment, leaving the vehicle in freefall before impacting the ground. ESA has subsequently recovered all the craft's components.

This month's event was the first drop test since an unsuccessful attempt in May, when a sensor flagged an issue during preparations, prompting engineers to abort the test before the model was released. Following that, Space Rider's testing range, operated by Italy's Ministry of Defense, was unavailable from June through September.

Space Rider's descent and landing qualification campaign began in 2025, with previous successes demonstrating its parafoil deployment and autonomous capabilities. Beyond its landing systems, Space Rider also underwent separate thermal protection testing earlier this year, exposing its heat-shield materials to temperatures around 2,900 degrees Fahrenheit (1,600 degrees Celsius) in plasma wind tunnel trials.

ESA has targeted Space Rider's first orbital mission for later this decade.
