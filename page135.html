#980. Cathinka Tandberg: ‘Spurs fans hate Arsenal. That’s how it is’

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Norwegian forward might look to Harry Kane for inspiration on the pitch, but when north London derby comes around, she needs no lessons on what the rivalry means

“It means more than just three points. It’s for the whole club. It’s for the whole place. It’s for the whole of Tottenham.” One only needs to be in the presence of Tottenham Cathinka Tandberg for a few minutes to feel the passion that emanates from her when it comes to this weekend’s north London derby. As a lifelong Spurs fan, the forward understands more than most the intensity of the rivalry that exists between her side and Arsenal, one that she cannot wait to experience once again on Saturday.

“It will just be an incredible feeling,” she tells Moving the Goalposts. “I think you will feel a few nerves that you maybe don’t feel in a normal game but that’s good.

“All the Spurs fans hate Arsenal and Arsenal hate Spurs. That’s how it is. You want to show that you’re the best team – simple as that. You just want to fight for the club, the badge and for the people around you.”

It has been a significant 13 months for the 22-year-old who made her dream move to London in September 2025. After catching the eye as a goalscorer in the Damallsvenskan at first Linköping and then Hammarby, the Norwegian was signed by Martin Ho on deadline day to bolster the team’s attacking options. She made her mark on her first start, scoring a spectacular 40-yard goal at Everton.

“I settled really well,” she reflects. “I’m really enjoying it and want to push myself forward. It’s been a tough start [to this season] and I haven’t really got what I wanted. But there’s loads of games left. I’m still young. I’m still improving and learning. I just have to trust the process and trust what I’m surrounded by.

“It’s a big difference from Sweden,” she continues. “You meet World Cup players week in, week out, so you have to perform every week if you want results. I feel like my physicality has changed a lot. I’ve got stronger and fitter. Playing under Martin has made me think more away from the goals. I think more about how I play with the ball, how I press and all of those things that I maybe didn’t focus on before … it’s a bit like Harry Kane. The older he gets, he has developed his game as well. He needed to be deeper in the pitch because that’s how they wanted to play and then he has to be better with the ball. That’s a bit how I also need to be. I can’t only just stand in the box. I need to be good everywhere else.”

Spurs have been transformed under Ho and finished fifth last season. However, with success comes expectation, ambitions that Tandberg clearly shares but with a sense of perspective.

“Tottenham has always been a Champions League club for me,” she says. “But I’m a fan so I can’t really talk for the other players. When I came to the club, I said that I want to get top three. But you also need to be realistic and to see where the team has been before. The transformation that Martin has done in a season is unbelievable. I don’t think anyone else could do that. That tells you everything about him. He’s one of the greatest coaches I’ve worked with and I think everyone that comes here, one of the reasons is because of him. We all trust him and the process.”

That faith is currently being tested. After an unbeaten start, Tottenham have suffered back-to-back defeats, conceding six goals to both London City Lionesses and Chelsea.

“You can call it a dip in form but I still feel like we also showed our qualities in those games,” Tandberg says. “It’s just about ourselves. We maybe need to change our mentality a bit, find that fire and dig a bit deeper when we play those top teams. I feel like maybe it’s good that it’s come because it had to at some point. It’s about how we respond.

“We also have to realise where this team has been. Two years ago, it was a relegation club. We need to realise how quickly it has [changed]. But, of course, we have expectations and levels that we want to come to and we know that we can. I feel like we’re working so well … we just have to believe in everyone.”

For Tandberg, putting that right against a struggling Arsenal in front of a home crowd would be the perfect tonic. “I’ve always been a passionate player and I’ve been a supporter,” she says. “I play for the supporters because I just enjoy it and I know how it is to be in the stands. I think [that connection] is very important because we have their back and they have ours. To give them something back is important and to show that we see and hear them every time.”

#978. Nobel Prize in literature goes to Canada's Anne Carson

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Carson won for her "bold and inventive oeuvre." The decorated poet and essayist is known for her reverence for Latin and Greek classics.

The Swedish Academy awarded the 2026 Nobel Prize in literature to Anne Carson on Thursday, praising her "bold and inventive oeuvre."

Her work has "in playful dialogue with the classical tradition...created new forms for contemporary literature," said Ingrid Carlberg, permanent secretary of the academy.

The 76-year-old writer is a longtime professor of classics, creative writing, and comparative literature at prestigious institutions like McGill and Princeton universities. She is only the 19th woman of 123 laureates to win the award.

Born in Toronto in 1950, Carson moved with her family among several small towns in Canada during her youth. She has credited a beloved Latin teacher for sparking her interest in language and classics.

Her first book of criticism, 1986’s Eros the Bittersweet, was published to great acclaim and has had significant influence on other classicists, poets, and even film and television. Carson has also published her own poetry such as the 2001 collection The Beauty of the Husband as well as highly regarded translation of ancient Greek poets such as Sappho and Sophocles.

Carson is also heavily influenced by the King James Bible and more modern writers including Emily Dickinson and Virginia Woolf. She has received numerous awards such as the T.S. Eliot Prize for Poetry, a MacArthur fellowship, and the PEN/Nabokov Award.

Deeply private, Carson often declines to speak about her personal life in interviews and discourages autobiographical interpretations of her work. However, she did reveal in 2024 that she has Parkinson's disease.

The winner of the Nobel Prize in literature receives prize money of 12 million Swedish kronor, equivalent to about $1.2 million. The winner also receives a medal and a diploma at an official ceremony in December, and is expected to deliver a speech known as a Nobel lecture.

On Friday, the winner of the Nobel Peace Prize will be announced, followed by the Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel on Monday.

#977. The keeper doing 'Premier League' things for EFL's last unbeaten side

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Jed Ward was an ever-present on loan for Yeovil in the National League last season

New Walsall goalkeeper Jed Ward is producing "Premier League" contributions with both his hands and feet, according to his dazzled manager and team-mates.

The 23-year-old has kept eight clean sheets in 13 games in all competitions - making a string of superb saves in the process - since joining the Saddlers from hometown club Bristol Rovers for an undisclosed fee in the summer.

And he added another string to his bow with an outrageous assist in Tuesday's 3-0 League Two win over Oldham Athletic - pinging a pin-point 50-yard low through-ball for Tai Sodje to score the Saddlers' opener.

"The pass from Jed is different level - I was shouting at him to play Tai in, but I didn't think he'd do it in that way," said impressed Walsall top-scorer Aaron Pressley.

Ward leads League Two in percentage of shots saved - 86% with 43 stops from 50 efforts on target faced, which is almost four saves a game on average.

Only Swindon's Connor Ripley (58) has made more saves while Exeter goalkeeper Jack Bycroft (seven) is the only keeper to have kept more League Two clean sheets than Ward's six.

He has spent much of the past two seasons on loan at National League clubs Yeovil and Forest Green, but did make 18 League One appearances for Bristol Rovers in 2024-25.

In addition to his unexpected assist, Ward made three stops at key moments of Tuesday's game to continue to delight Saddlers boss Lee Grant, himself a former goalkeeper for Sheffield Wednesday, Burnley and Derby during his playing days.

"In terms of what he's bringing at the moment, [Jed] is very, very high level," Grant told BBC Radio WM.

"There's a big save at 0-0, a big save at 1-0. There's an incredible assist, then there's distribution in the second half which is just so well thought-out and executed. His level of performance has been so high."

Grant: 'We got to see both sides of the group'

The Walsall job is Lee Grant's second head coach role after eight months in charge of Huddersfield Town last season

Walsall's collective level of performance under new boss Grant - with a largely overhauled squad - has been just as high as their last line of defence.

After 11 games, they are the only unbeaten team remaining in the EFL, although a surplus of draws compared to wins - seven to four - means they only sit fourth.

Two goals from Pressley, including one from the penalty spot, after Sodje's opener, comfortably saw off Oldham despite Walsall playing the final half hour with 10 men after Elicha Ahui's red card.

"We got to see both sides of the group - we got to see a team that was really going after the game in the first half, dominated and controlled large parts," added Grant.

"Then we got to see the other side of the group which is becoming a clear trend in terms of the character, the fight and the determination and I think in some ways that feels sweeter."

Walsall have led League Two at Christmas in both of the past two seasons, only to fall away and fail to gain promotion on either occasion.

Pressley, who joined in the summer of 2025 and was part of last season's slide, has already overtaken his goal tally from last term with eight so far - including seven in the league.

Both he and his manager, however, pointed to Walsall's strength in depth - Grant has regularly tinkered with his starting XI, never fielding an unchanged side despite their solid results - as a beacon of hope they can last the course this time.

"I've just trusted my whole group," said Grant. "So, for the long run, it's good to have this sort of depth and be able to utilise that."

Pressley added: "We made four changes on Tuesday and that's the strength of the squad. The players coming on have been outstanding and the way we can change the team from game to game is such a strength."

Arguably, Walsall's biggest test of their unbeaten start will come in their next home game when they host leaders Salford on Monday.

"Obviously it's quite a big game next Monday, but we just need to focus on what we can control in a way on the training pitch and prepare properly," Pressley added.

"That should put us in a good place for next week."

Lewis Hamilton has set his sights on staying in Formula 1 into the 2030s in order to fulfil his dream of racing in Africa.

Formula 1 last week announced the addition to its calendar of an African Grand Prix in Rwanda but did not provide a firm date for the first staging of the event, saying instead it will take place in the "early 2030s".

Hamilton, who turns 42 in January, will be 45 at the start of F1's 2030 season. Despite the seven-time world champion accepting there is no guarantee he will still be on the grid at that stage, he is retaining hope of competing in what will be the sport's first race in Africa since 1993.

Speaking ahead of this weekend's United States Grand Prix, Hamilton said: "I'm now forced to stay a bit longer because we have a race in Africa and that has been my dream to race in Africa.

"So, I'm going to have to keep fighting to stay fit. If I can keep punching out results over the next years, we will keep it going."

#973. The damning reality of Michael Carrick’s diabolical Man United

For a man who made an instant impact as interim manager last season, Carrick’s team have looked worse the longer he has been in charge and he has looked powerless to address the slide, writes Richard Jolly

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

It is only eight months since Bruno Fernandes was reflecting on the impact Michael Carrick had made and how he had predicted it. Back in 2021, when Carrick’s first spell in charge of Manchester United ended, Fernandes spoke to him. “I think he still remembers what I told him the last time he was our manager,” he said. “I was sure Michael could be a great manager, and now he’s showing it.”

Then again, it is only six weeks since Carrick was thinking back to United’s 2008 Champions League win and allowing himself to dream. “The best feeling I've had,” he said. “Be nice someday to feel that again.” It scarcely seems an overreaction to suggest he probably will not; or not at the helm of United anyway.

Carrick was part of some of United’s greatest European nights even if, in his understated way, it was rarely all about him. He was in charge for one of the worst, Wednesday’s 4-1 walloping at Como, again insisting it was not about him.

By the end, though, it was. Fernandes’ post-match interview ensured as much. And if the tactical point the captain raised about United’s lack of pressing was relevant – they have looked slow and sluggish for much of the campaign, but perhaps they have too many old and slow players to press – the dissent was still the more notable element of his comments.

In his golden spring, Carrick had his players’ backing. He was the amiable antidote to Ruben Amorim, the United whisperer, the decorated great who seemed more suited to the Old Trafford dugout than the Middlesbrough technical area.

Now no one is happy with anyone else. Fernandes has questioned the game plan and thus the manager, Lisandro Martinez the recruitment and the sporting director, Jason Wilcox. Erik ten Hag’s recent observation that the players have too much power rings true; or that, with the odd notable exception – and Fernandes is one – they seem to accept too little responsibility. In Como, Carrick cast aside his usual talk of “the boys” giving everything to admit he was angry and call the performance “not acceptable”.

A lot of them have been. There have been at least four truly diabolical displays this season – Hull, Manchester City, Brighton and Como – with others of varying degrees of mediocrity. Low-key as Carrick can be, he sounded too upbeat after Sunday’s draw at Leeds. He called that a fresh start.

Instead, he looks nearer the end. He said training ahead of the trip to Elland Road was one of the best sessions they have ever had. A man who has dealt in forgettable rhetoric now finds his comments are memorable for the wrong reasons.

It prompts the question of how long he can last. The usual answer with United managers is longer than they should; in Amorim’s case, at least seven months longer, in Ten Hag’s four, in Ole Gunnar Solskjaer and Jose Mourinho’s several weeks. United’s powerbrokers tend to ignore the signs, hoping against hope a corner will be turned, perhaps conscious that sacking a manager is an indictment of their decision-making in appointing him.

The distinctly underwhelming double act of chief executive Omar Berrada and Wilcox scarcely showed much confidence in Carrick when they only gave him a two-year contract. Perhaps the thought was that he could consolidate before someone else would take them forward. Instead, for the fourth year in a row, United are regressing alarmingly in autumn.

Sometimes with United the surprise is how fast it unravels, not the fact it does. It was evident this year would pose greater challenges for Carrick, with the complications of a more crowded fixture list. That was compounded by failures in the transfer market, whether of a failure to recruit in certain positions or a failure in who they did add in midfield.

Yet it is still startling how bad they have been and how, unlike previous poor United sides, they have struggled to churn out even the odd unconvincing win. They have not won in seven, and their next seven fixtures – Bournemouth, Chelsea, Roma, Aston Villa, Liverpool, Sporting CP and Brentford – suggest any respite could be temporary before the next setback.

Carrick could seem to have entered the endgame, the long goodbye. The calmness that seemed a strength now looks a shortcoming if he lacks the dynamism to turn this around. The damning reality for a man who made an instant impact is that United have looked worse the longer he has been in charge.

His planning can come unstuck quickly. United didn’t feel ready for the start of the season. They haven’t begun in a manner to suggest they were ready for their first two Champions League away games, conceding in the first minute to Atletico Madrid and the second to Como. The Italian city is, famously, home to the Clooneys. United were the team who didn’t have a clue.

And if the problems go from the dressing room to the boardroom, Carrick, who has looked powerless to halt their slide this season, may not join the small band of their great managers but the increasingly large group of their ex-managers.

#968. Breaking down Man Utd's tactics after Fernandes' criticism

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Manchester United captain Bruno Fernandes appeared to question their tactical approach after Wednesday's 4-1 Champions League defeat by Como.

"We started off on the wrong foot," the Portuguese said. "They scored very early, [we had] not much pressure on the ball.

"It's a decision from the staff to not press man-to-man and sometimes leave some space behind, to try and get the ball in the first moment [by] waiting for them, [giving] them the ball.

"Sometimes when you give too much of the ball to these teams, they get confidence. They have so many bodies behind the ball, they make you run a lot, people get frustrated."

His quotes are tactically dense so warrant breaking down, but what did Fernandes mean, and does he have a point?

Bruno Fernandes seems to be at odds with Manchester United manager Michael Carrick's tactics

Since Michael Carrick became United manager in January, they have largely defended in a zonal 4-2-4 or 4-4-2 shape that springs into a press in moments.

When Fernandes said United "leave some space behind, to try and get the ball in the first moment" he was referring to them not immediately pressing across the pitch in a man-to-man fashion.

Holding their 4-2-4 shape a little bit deeper, United allow opposition defenders time and space on the ball, looking to set traps before pouncing.

The overarching idea is with four players narrow and high, United's attackers are well positioned to score from turnovers should they win the ball back.

Here we see United's 4-2-4 shape in the first half against Como yesterday

At times during Carrick's stint as caretaker head coach last season, it worked well - as we saw when Bryan Mbeumo scored in United's 3-2 win at Arsenal in January two weeks after Carrick took over

United didn't press Arsenal in a man-to-man fashion but still found joy

By recognising the right moment to press, they turned this two against two into a goal-scoring opportunity for Mbeumo

Mbeumo put the ball away for United's equaliser

But the same approach has been less effective this season.

On the same night defender Lisandro Martinez questioned United's recruitment - after their 2-0 defeat by Atletico Madrid - he also spoke about the ever-changing tactical landscape.

"You can't stay with what you did last season." he said. "Teams know you, how you play, know every detail, and if you don't change, next season is a big problem."

Last season, the introduction of the 4-2-4 shape as a defensive tactic caught a number of teams off guard, making it more effective.

Pep Guardiola and Pep Lijnders used the same shape at Manchester City - a tweak that underpinned their Carabao Cup final win over an ill-prepared Arsenal.

Having replaced Guardiola, Enzo Maresca redeployed it for the Community Shield in August, but this time Arsenal boss Mikel Arteta had the solutions and the Gunners won.

Within a few games, Maresca had abandoned the 4-24 and moved to a more man-to-man style of defending.

Arsenal were able to create clever overloads around City's 4-2-4 in the wide areas

In this example, they access their winger in space with ease

In their recent game against PSG, City took a far more man-to-man approach behind the ball. Guehi is seen stepping out of defence to track his man.

Perhaps that is the sort of change Fernandes is hoping for - recognising the reduced effectiveness of United's defensive approach, with signs of weakness visible as early as pre-season.

AC Milan - now managed by former United boss Ruben Amorim - repeatedly found joy playing around the 4-2-4, with a back five that outnumbered the Red Devils' front four.

United's two central attackers would look to block passes into Milan's midfield two before pressing the central defenders.

The wide position of one of the central defenders would force a United winger to step out, and Milan's wing-back - dropping deep and wide - gave them an easy way out.

United's full-back would then have to cover a large distance to close down the wing-back but by the time pressure was applied, the ball was gone.

Milan accessed the wide areas well around United's front four resulting in the midfielders or full-back having to cover large distances

The same problems were seen in United's defeat by Atletico Madrid.

Atletico took the risk of employing the same tactic with a back four rather than a back five, which left them an extra attacker high up the pitch.

As United's front two looked to stop passes into the central midfielders, the wide position of the centre-backs drew out United's wingers.

That, in turn, meant left-back Alejandro Grimaldo found himself in space out wide.

This required Diogo Dalot, or if he was pinned back by winger Alex Baena, Youri Tielemans to cover large distances in an attempt to apply some pressure to Grimaldo.

So while Fernandes may have been referring to himself when he said "they make you run a lot, people get frustrated", it could easily have applied to these team-mates.

Atletico Madrid used a back four, bravely splitting their centre-backs, accessing the full-back around United's front four. This forced Dalot, in this example, to jump to press Grimaldo. Given the distance, he was unable to apply meaningful pressure.

When Fernandes' quotes were put to Carrick after the game, he pushed back.

"We actually half-pressed, man-to-man in the second half and then ended up conceding a goal from that," he said.

"It's a little bit on whatever system you play, whatever tactics you play, but if you don't run and you don't run forward and you don't run back, you aren't going to win."

While the less aggressive 4-2-4 has its weaknesses, it often leaves an extra player in the backline, giving United a numerical advantage in defence.

It also aims to funnel attacks down the flanks, where they are - in theory - less dangerous than chances conceded through the middle.

United's backline have also - under both Carrick and Amorim - struggled to track and duel with their opponents in one-against-one situations when they have committed to a full press.

When using a man-to-man press, losing an individual battle, or lacking the intensity to arrive at your opposing player quickly, makes the system fall apart as we saw for Como's second goal.

In the second half, United took a more man-oriented defensive approach

In a man-to-man approach, if an individual battle is lost, the shape quickly becomes disjointed. Como's midfielder rolls Baleba and finds his attacking midfielder. His marker Mazraoui is slow to react, allowing Como to attack through the middle.

United are left two against two at the back, facing a central attack from Como

Como convert this good play into their second goal

Against Atletico, although United didn't start the game pressing in a man-to-man fashion, Lisandro Martinez pressed his attacker in a man-to-man way in the build-up to Atletico Madrid's opening goal

With Lisandro losing his man, Atletico Madrid's attacker was able to find his team-mate in space - resulting in a goal

In the Premier League, United have also had spells during which they have attempted to press in a more man-to-man fashion, but these have also been imperfectly executed.

In fact, against Spurs, it was Fernandes who misread moments and positioned himself incorrectly.

Despite Amorim's flaws, one of his strengths was in improving United's physical base and intensity. There is a question as to whether Carrick's United, with one game a week last season, were beneficiaries of that.

This season they look unable to sustain the intensity to execute a man-to-man press with the effectiveness of, say, Fabian Hurzeler's Brighton - a physically fit squad aligned on how and when to press.

For United, more concerningly, there appears to be a disconnect - at least in how Fernandes sees the game and the messaging from the coaching staff.

This misalignment makes it hard to execute any defensive approach, with good defending built on intensity and co-ordination between players.

"Sometimes you have to be more brave under pressure," said Fernandes. "Take the risk of pressing high and making them uncomfortable."

But bravery is subjective and one might argue that Carrick defending with four forwards - ready to attack quickly - is an incredibly brave, if not naive, decision.

Going for a 'half man-to-man' approach - looking to lock opponents into one area of the pitch - while allowing some players to defend more zonally feels like a fair trade-off from Carrick.

But how United defend in the 4-2-4 feels outdated already, and is being exposed.

So while both Fernandes and Carrick seem to have a point, if United are to win regularly, any solution this season is likely to lie somewhere in between their views.

Moderately cold temperatures extend lifespan and promote health across species1,2,3, yet the molecular mechanisms underlying these benefits remain unclear. Here we define a metabolite-driven, multi-tissue signalling system that links cold temperature to enhanced longevity and proteostasis in Caenorhabditis elegans. Cold temperature upregulates the biopterin-synthesis enzyme CAT-4 (GCH1 in humans), increasing production of dihydrobiopterin (BH2), a transportable reservoir for the bioactive cofactor tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4). BH2 is produced in muscle and transported to distal tissues, including the intestine and neurons, where it is converted into BH4 by the salvage enzyme DHFR-1. BH4 functions as an essential cofactor for phenylalanine hydroxylase PAH-1, promoting tyrosine synthesis and extending lifespan. This BH2–BH4 axis also prevents disease-related protein aggregation and subsequent neuronal dysfunction during ageing. Importantly, increasing BH2 synthesis specifically in muscle is sufficient to promote longevity and suppress pathological protein aggregation in distal tissues, even at normal or elevated temperatures. In human cells, BH2 or tyrosine supplementation reduces aggregation of polyQ-expanded proteins associated with Huntington’s disease, as well as mutant variants of TDP-43 and FUS that cause amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. Together, these findings identify BH2 as a cold-induced metabolite of which the synthesis, intertissue transport and conversion into bioactive BH4 preserve organism-wide proteostasis and extend lifespan.

Emerging evidence demonstrates that moderately cold temperatures delay ageing and promote health across species1. Lowering body temperature extends lifespan in both poikilotherms, including C. elegans, Drosophila melanogaster and various fish species, and homeotherms, including rodents1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8. In adult C. elegans, a shift from standard (20 °C) to moderately low temperature (15 °C) markedly extends lifespan2,9,10,11,12. In rodents, a mild decrease in body temperature by only around 0.5 °C prolongs lifespan, whereas a comparable increase shortens it3,4. Lower body temperature also correlates with longevity in humans13,14. Notably, average human body temperature has declined since the Industrial Revolution, raising the possibility that changes in body temperature may have contributed to increased longevity over recent decades13. Together, these findings suggest that moderate cold engages evolutionarily conserved programs that promote longevity.

Despite being recognized for more than a century15, the mechanisms by which moderate cold temperature promotes longevity remain largely unclear. Historically, cold-induced lifespan extension was interpreted as a consequence of passive thermodynamic effects, including reduced metabolic rate and slower biochemical reaction kinetics. However, studies in C. elegans revealed that cold-induced longevity is an actively regulated process2,9,10,11,12. For example, the temperature-sensitive TRPA-1 ion channel promotes longevity at 15–20 °C but not at warmer temperatures, indicating that low temperature is sensed and transduced into longevity mechanisms11,12. Moreover, cold temperature induces transcriptional programs that enhance proteostasis2,9.

Despite these advances, a fundamental question remains of what systemic signals coordinate cold-induced responses across tissues. Small metabolites are ideally suited for this role because they can act as rapidly diffusible regulators integrating metabolism, enzymatic activity and intertissue signalling. We therefore hypothesized that cold temperature induces metabolite-based signals that coordinate metabolic, proteostatic and longevity pathways across tissues. We further hypothesized that such metabolites could function as temperature-independent effectors capable of recapitulating the beneficial effects of cold temperature even at higher temperatures.

Here we identify BH2 as a cold-induced metabolite of which the synthesis, intertissue transport and conversion into the bioactive enzymatic cofactor BH4 constitute a multi-tissue regulatory system that promotes longevity and maintains proteostasis across tissues. Through metabolomic, genetic and physiological analyses, we find that elevated BH2 synthesis in muscle and its conversion to BH4 in distal tissues promotes tyrosine synthesis, thereby mediating key aspects of cold-induced longevity. This pathway can be leveraged to confer longevity and proteostasis benefits at normal and elevated temperatures in both C. elegans and human cells.

Exposure of C. elegans to moderately low temperature (15 °C) after development induces a considerable extension of lifespan2,10,11,12. To assess metabolic changes associated with this effect, we raised wild-type worms at the standard temperature (20 °C) until adulthood and subsequently transferred them to 15 °C, 20 °C or 25 °C. Untargeted metabolomics analysis on day 5 of adulthood identified 64 metabolites that were significantly increased at 15 °C relative to both 20 °C and 25 °C (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1). An independent experiment confirmed 45 of these metabolites, with strong concordance between datasets (Extended Data Fig. 1a–d and Supplementary Table 2).

Among the 45 reproducibly cold-induced metabolites, several were linked to BH4 synthesis and its function as an enzymatic cofactor, including GTP, BH2, tryptophan, phenylalanine and tyrosine (Fig. 1a,b). As BH2 was among the most strongly induced metabolites at cold temperature (Fig. 1a), we focused subsequent analyses on this metabolite. Cold exposure after development increased BH2 levels from day 1 of adulthood, and BH2 remained elevated at 15 °C relative to age-matched animals at 20 °C until at least day 10 (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Table 3). BH2 induction was not a general consequence of longevity, as reduced insulin signalling, a distinct longevity pathway, did not increase BH2 levels at 20 °C (Extended Data Fig. 1e and Supplementary Table 4).

Loss of the temperature-sensitive trpa-1 channel shortens lifespan under both standard and cold temperatures11. However, worms still exhibit substantially extended lifespan at 15 °C compared with at 20 °C, indicating that cold-induced longevity is not solely mediated by TRPA-1-dependent pathways. Loss of trpa-1 did not reduce BH2 levels at cold temperature (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Table 1), demonstrating that elevated BH2 synthesis is induced independently of canonical TRPA-1 temperature sensing.

We next examined whether BH2 reflects a regulated pathway rather than a passive metabolic consequence of reduced temperature. Quantitative proteomics identified GTP cyclohydrolase I (GCH1 in humans, CAT-4 in C. elegans), the first and rate-limiting enzyme in BH2 synthesis16,17, as one of the most strongly induced proteins at 15 °C (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Table 5). Cold temperature also increased cat-4 mRNA amounts, indicating that CAT-4 induction occurs, at least in part, at the transcriptional level (Extended Data Fig. 2a). MED15 (human)/MDT-15 (C. elegans), a key transcriptional regulator of metabolic responses, is required for cold-induced longevity9. Importantly, knockdown of mdt-15 reduced cat-4 transcript levels at 15 °C (Extended Data Fig. 2b), indicating that cold-induced CAT-4 expression is part of a regulated transcriptional program.

Although BH2 is an inactive metabolite, it functions as a key intermediate in the synthesis and intertissue transport of its reduced, bioactive form, BH416,17. As BH4 is chemically unstable and readily oxidized, the more-stable BH2 form serves as a major organismal reservoir of this bioactive metabolite16,17. Consequently, cat-4-mutant worms are severely depleted of BH418. Consistent with the essential role of BH4 as a cofactor for multiple enzymes, these worms exhibit increased fragility compared with wild-type worms18. Accordingly, cat-4-mutant worms were short lived under both cold and standard temperatures (Extended Data Fig. 2c–e). Knockdown of cat-4 from early development also shortened lifespan at both 15 °C and 20 °C, although the effect was more pronounced under cold temperatures (Extended Data Fig. 2f).

As cold temperature during adulthood was sufficient to induce CAT-4 expression, BH2 synthesis and lifespan extension, we next examined the effects of cat-4 knockdown after development. As BH4 could not be detected by metabolomics because of its chemical instability, we quantified BH4 using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Cold temperature induced a significant but modest increase in BH4 levels (Fig. 1f), consistent with BH2 serving as the major intermediate reservoir for this metabolite16,17. Knockdown of cat-4 after development decreased BH4 levels under both standard and cold temperatures, although this reduction was moderate (Fig. 1f). Importantly, cat-4 RNA interference (RNAi) decreased BH4 at cold temperature only to levels comparable to those observed at standard temperature without knockdown (Fig. 1f). Thus, BH4 levels remained within a physiological range, enabling us to determine whether elevated CAT-4 activity during adulthood is required for cold-induced longevity. Notably, knockdown of cat-4 after development diminished the lifespan extension induced by cold temperature, but had no significant effect on lifespan at 20 °C or 25 °C (Fig. 1g).

To further dissect the adult-specific requirement for elevated CAT-4 in cold-induced longevity, we generated an auxin-inducible degron (AID) strain enabling conditional degradation of CAT-4 protein in somatic tissues (Extended Data Fig. 2g). CAT-4 degradation from early development substantially reduced lifespan at 20 °C (Extended Data Fig. 2h). By contrast, degradation of CAT-4 after development did not affect lifespan at 20 °C, but specifically shortened longevity at 15 °C (Extended Data Fig. 2i). These results further indicate that CAT-4 activity is essential during development for adult viability, whereas increased CAT-4 activity during adulthood specifically contributes to cold-induced longevity.

Prompted by these findings, we next examined whether CAT-4 activity also contributes to lifespan extension in other longevity paradigms. Knockdown of cat-4 in mutant worms with reduced insulin signalling did not affect their extended lifespan at standard temperature, but only shortened lifespan at 15 °C (Extended Data Fig. 3a,b). By contrast, knockdown of cat-4 shortened lifespan in long-lived worms with reduced mitochondrial electron-transport chain activity or reduced food intake at both cold and standard temperatures (Extended Data Fig. 3c–h), suggesting that CAT-4 activity contributes to other longevity paradigms beyond cold temperature.

Downstream of activity of GCH1/CAT-4, bioactive BH4 can be generated through three pathways: the de novo, salvage and recycling pathways16,17 (Fig. 1b). In the de novo pathway, a GCH1/CAT-4-dependent intermediate is converted to BH4 by sepiapterin reductase. In the salvage pathway, this intermediate is instead converted to BH2 by either aldose reductase (AKR1B1 (human), Y39G8B.1 (C. elegans)) or carbonyl reductase 1 (CBR1 (human), DHS-31 (C. elegans))16,19. This pathway operates alongside de novo BH4 synthesis, but is particularly important in mammalian tissues with low sepiapterin reductase activity and in organisms such as C. elegans, which lack a sepiapterin reductase orthologue16,18. Knockdown of the BH2-producing enzyme Y39G8B.1 reduced lifespan specifically at 15 °C, whereas loss of dhs-31 produced a smaller, non-significant effect (Extended Data Fig. 4a). In the salvage pathway, BH2 is further converted into bioactive BH4 by dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR (human), DHFR-1 (C. elegans)). Importantly, knockdown of dhfr-1 after development decreased longevity at cold temperature but did not shorten lifespan at standard temperature (Fig. 1h). These results indicate that, downstream of CAT-4 activity, the conversion of BH2 into bioactive BH4 by DHFR-1 contributes to cold-induced longevity.

A recycling mechanism regenerates BH4 after its use as an enzymatic cofactor16,18 (Fig. 1b). In C. elegans, the recycling pathway is not required to maintain steady-state BH4 levels and becomes rate-limiting only under conditions of low BH4 synthesis, such as in reduction-of-function cat-4 mutants18. Subsequently, knockdown of recycling enzymes did not shorten the lifespan of wild-type worms at cold temperature (Extended Data Fig. 4b). Likewise, mutant worms defective in BH4 recycling were not short lived at 15 °C compared with wild-type worms (Extended Data Fig. 4c,d), indicating that this pathway is dispensable for cold-induced longevity. Together, our findings support a central role of CAT-4 activity and the salvage pathway in elevated BH4 synthesis for cold-induced longevity.

To determine the tissues in which CAT-4 is expressed, we generated a GFP-based fluorescent reporter driven by the endogenous cat-4 promoter. Knockdown of cat-4 reduced the GFP signal, validating the reporter (Extended Data Fig. 5a). At standard temperature, CAT-4 was mostly expressed in the anterior intestine, and cold exposure further increased CAT-4 levels in this region (Extended Data Fig. 5a,b). In contrast to the intestine, CAT-4 was barely detectable in muscle at 20 °C (Fig. 2a). However, cold temperature triggered robust expression of cat-4 in the muscle, an effect that was abolished by cat-4 RNAi treatment (Fig. 2a,b). To assess whether elevated BH2 synthesis in these tissues promotes organismal longevity at cold temperature, we performed tissue-specific knockdown experiments. Notably, knockdown of cat-4 in the muscle alone reduced cold-induced longevity, whereas knockdown in the intestine had no effect on lifespan (Fig. 2c,d). Likewise, knockdown of cat-4 in neurons, epidermis or germline did not shorten lifespan at 15 °C (Fig. 2e–g).

To further investigate the link between cold-induced CAT-4 expression in muscle and longevity, we generated worms in which auxin-inducible degradation of CAT-4 was restricted to muscle. We confirmed that auxin treatment specifically depleted CAT-4 in the muscle of these animals, whereas ubiquitous somatic degradation reduced CAT-4 levels in both muscle and intestine (Extended Data Fig. 5c,d). Notably, muscle-specific degradation of CAT-4 after development was sufficient to fully phenocopy the longevity-shortening effects of ubiquitous somatic CAT-4 degradation at 15 °C (Extended Data Fig. 5e). This effect was specific to cold temperature, as muscle-specific CAT-4 degradation did not affect lifespan at standard temperature (Extended Data Fig. 5f). Together, these results identify muscle as a critical tissue in which elevated BH2 synthesis promotes cold-induced longevity.

BH4 is essential for the function of amino acid hydroxylases (TPH1 (human), TPH-1 (C. elegans); TH (human), CAT-2 (C. elegans); PAH (human), PAH-1 (C. elegans) and alkylglycerol monooxygenase (AGMO (human), AGMO-1 (C. elegans)). However, these enzymes are not expressed in muscle cells18, raising the possibility that BH4 acts in a cell non-autonomous manner. BH2 is the principal transport intermediate for BH4 distribution between tissues, enabling the rapid generation and utilization of bioactive BH4 in distal tissues16,17. We therefore hypothesized that cold temperature increases BH2 synthesis in muscle cells, which is then released and transported to distal tissues, where it is converted into BH4 to activate its target enzymes. In humans, the uptake of BH2 is mediated by the transmembrane nucleoside transporters ENT1 (SLC29A1) and ENT2 (SLC29A2)16. C. elegans expresses seven orthologues of human ENT1 and ENT2 (ent-1 to ent-7). We found that single knockdown of different orthologues, such as ent-1, ent-5 and ent-6, specifically shortens lifespan at 15 °C but not 20 °C (Fig. 2h,i).

Once BH2 is taken up by distal cells, it can be converted into bioactive BH4 through the salvage pathway by the DHFR-1 enzyme16,20. Consequently, BH4 redistribution efficiency depends on DHFR-1 activity, which regulates local BH4 availability. While BH2 synthesis through CAT-4 activity in muscle cells is required for cold-induced longevity (Fig. 2c), we found that muscle-specific knockdown of dhfr-1 does not shorten lifespan at 15 °C (Fig. 2j). Similarly, dhfr-1 knockdown in the epidermis had no effect on longevity at 15 °C (Fig. 2k). By contrast, tissue-specific knockdown of dhfr-1 in neurons, intestine and germline reduced cold-induced longevity (Fig. 2l–n). Moreover, neither neuron-specific knockdown of dhfr-1 nor muscle-specific knockdown of cat-4 further reduced the shortened lifespan of ent-1 mutant animals at cold temperature (Extended Data Fig. 5g,h). These results support the notion that cold temperature induces BH2 synthesis in muscle cells, after which, BH2 is released and converted to bioactive BH4 in distal tissues such as neurons, intestine and germline, where it promotes cold-induced longevity.

To further examine the cell non-autonomous effects of BH2, we focused on the intestine, a tissue that progressively loses structural integrity during ageing21,22. To this end, we assessed the intermediate filament IFB-2 because this protein is expressed exclusively in the intestine and undergoes age-related changes that can be readily monitored22. In young animals, IFB-2 is confined to the apical cytoplasm of intestinal cells. With age, IFB-2 levels increase and the protein becomes mislocalized throughout the cytoplasm, where it forms aggregates that contribute to intestinal decline22. We found that cold temperature prevents IFB-2 mislocalization and aggregation during ageing (Extended Data Fig. 6a). However, loss of cat-4 abolished these protective effects (Extended Data Fig. 6a). Notably, muscle-specific knockdown of cat-4 was sufficient to block the cold-induced suppression of intestinal IFB-2 aggregation (Extended Data Fig. 6b). Collectively, these findings indicate that cold-induced BH2 synthesis in muscle exerts cell non-autonomous effects, such as preserving intestinal integrity during ageing.

BH2 supplementation is often more effective at increasing BH4 levels than direct BH4 administration16,20. However, C. elegans exhibits a limited capacity for biopterin uptake, including BH2, and exogenous supplementation results in only a modest increase in biopterin levels18. Despite this limitation, BH2 supplementation in adult worms led to a slight but significant extension of cold-induced longevity (Extended Data Fig. 7a). Although BH2 supplementation did not extend lifespan at standard temperature, it delayed the age-related decline in motility, a key indicator of healthspan (Extended Data Fig. 7b,c). Although BH2-treated worms did not display overt signs of sickness, we further assessed the possibility that these beneficial effects arise from a hormetic stress response, such as that induced by mitochondrial dysfunction23,24. However, BH2 supplementation did not reduce brood size and did not induce the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (Extended Data Fig. 7d,e), two commonly used indicators of physiological stress.

Given the limited uptake of exogenous biopterins in worms, we used a genetic approach to assess the full potential of these metabolites to extend longevity. Ubiquitous overexpression of cat-4 in somatic tissues resulted in a substantial increase in cold-induced longevity (Extended Data Fig. 7f). Notably, overexpression of cat-4 in the muscle alone was sufficient to increase organismal levels of bioactive BH4 and extend lifespan not only at 15 °C, but also at 20 °C and 25 °C (Fig. 3a,b and Extended Data Fig. 7g–i). Moreover, muscle-specific cat-4 overexpression also extended lifespan in loss-of-function trpa-1 mutant worms across different temperatures (Extended Data Fig. 7j–l). These findings indicate that increasing CAT-4 activity in muscle promotes longevity independently of both temperature and the cold-sensitive channel TRPA-1.

Consistent with a systemic protective effect, muscle-specific cat-4 overexpression prevented age-related accumulation and aggregation of intestinal IFB-2 protein at standard temperature (Fig. 3c and Extended Data Fig. 7m). As IFB-2 aggregation contributes to loss of intestinal integrity and subsequent bacterial colonization during ageing22, we examined whether CAT-4 prevents this deleterious process. Indeed, muscle-specific cat-4 overexpression reduced bacterial invasion of the intestine in aged worms at standard temperature (Fig. 3d,e). We then tested whether muscle-derived BH2 also acts in neurons. Neuronal-specific downregulation of either BH2 uptake (using ent-1 RNAi) or its conversion to BH4 through the salvage pathway (through dhfr-1 RNAi) significantly reduced the lifespan extension conferred by muscle-specific cat-4 overexpression at standard temperature, but had no effect in control worms (Extended Data Fig. 7n,o). These results further demonstrate that elevated BH2 production in muscle extends lifespan in a cell non-autonomous manner and requires both BH2 uptake and its conversion into bioactive BH4 in distal tissues, including the intestine and neurons.

We next examined whether increasing CAT-4 levels directly in the tissues that use BH2 is sufficient to promote longevity. While muscle-specific cat-4 overexpression had the strongest longevity effect, intestinal overexpression also markedly extended lifespan at both 15 °C and 20 °C (Fig. 3f,g). Although to a lesser extent, cat-4 overexpression in neurons also increased lifespan under both cold and standard temperatures (Fig. 3f,g). Together, these data suggest that, while elevated BH2 synthesis in muscle has a central role in longevity, increasing cat-4 expression in other tissues partially mimics its beneficial effects.

To determine how utilization of BH4 as an enzymatic cofactor contributes to cold-induced longevity, we performed a targeted RNAi screen against the four BH4-dependent enzymes expressed in C. elegans: phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH-1), tryptophan hydroxylase (TPH-1), tyrosine hydroxylase (CAT-2) and alkylglycerol monooxygenase (AGMO-1)18. Among them, knockdown of pah-1 after development shortened lifespan, whereas knockdown of the other BH4-dependent enzymes did not affect cold-induced longevity (Extended Data Fig. 8a). Consistently, loss-of-function tph-1, cat-2 and agmo-1 mutants were not short-lived, whereas pah-1 mutants exhibited reduced longevity specifically at cold temperature (Extended Data Fig. 8b–d).

Among the tissues involved in cold-induced longevity, we found that PAH-1 activity in the intestine is particularly important for lifespan extension (Fig. 4a and Extended Data Fig. 8e–g). Consistent with this, knockdown of pah-1 increased both the levels and aggregation of the intestinal intermediate filament IFB-2, thereby abolishing the protective effects of cold temperature on age-associated IFB-2 aggregation (Fig. 4b and Extended Data Fig. 8h). While muscle-specific overexpression of cat-4 was sufficient to prevent IFB-2 aggregation during ageing at standard temperature, this protective effect was lost after pah-1 knockdown (Fig. 4c and Extended Data Fig. 8i).

PAH-1 catalyses the synthesis of tyrosine, a metabolite that we found to be upregulated at cold temperature (Fig. 1a). Exogenous supplementation with tyrosine extended lifespan not only at cold temperature, but also at standard temperature (Fig. 4d,e). Importantly, tyrosine supplementation rescued the reduction in cold-induced longevity caused by cat-4 knockdown (Extended Data Fig. 8j), further supporting a functional role of regulated tyrosine metabolism in mediating the longevity effects of elevated BH2 synthesis.

To gain insights into the downstream mechanisms by which tyrosine promotes longevity, we performed over-representation analysis (ORA) of the differentially abundant proteins in worms at 15 °C relative to either 20 °C or 25 °C (Supplementary Table 5). Notably, we found strong enrichment for proteins involved in small-molecule and amino acid metabolic processes (Extended Data Fig. 9a,b). Within these pathways, we identified increased levels of 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate dioxygenase (HPD (human), HPD-1 (C. elegans); Extended Data Fig. 9c)—a key enzyme in the canonical tyrosine catabolic pathway that ultimately generates acetoacetate and fumarate, thereby linking tyrosine metabolism to central carbon metabolism25. Moreover, analysis of published transcriptomic datasets also revealed increased hpd-1 mRNA levels at cold temperature12 (Extended Data Fig. 9c). We found that loss of hpd-1 significantly reduced cold-induced longevity (Fig. 4f), supporting a functional contribution of tyrosine catabolism to lifespan extension at low temperature. Although hpd-1 knockdown also caused a slight decrease in lifespan at 20 °C and 25 °C, these effects did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 4f).

To identify molecular pathways that are responsive to elevated tyrosine levels, we performed quantitative proteomic analyses after tyrosine supplementation at both 15 °C and 20 °C (Supplementary Table 6). These analyses revealed 54 commonly upregulated proteins and 42 commonly downregulated proteins after tyrosine supplementation across both temperatures (Extended Data Fig. 9d,e). ORA of the differentially abundant proteins identified significant enrichment for lipid metabolic processes and enzyme regulator activity (Extended Data Fig. 9f,g). Together, these data suggest that tyrosine promotes longevity through specific downstream regulatory and metabolic pathways, including HPD-1-dependent tyrosine catabolism.

Loss of proteostasis during ageing is a major risk factor for diseases characterized by pathological protein aggregation26. Moderately cold temperatures suppress disease-related protein aggregation across tissues, including polyQ-expanded proteins in C. elegans models of Huntington’s disease2,9. Knockdown of cat-4 abolished this protective effect and increased aggregation without altering total polyQ levels (Extended Data Fig. 10a–c), indicating that CAT-4 activity modulates aggregation rather than overall protein amounts.

Consistent with the cell non-autonomous effects of muscle CAT-4 activity, loss of cat-4 increased aggregation at cold temperature not only in muscle, but also in worms expressing polyQ-expanded proteins exclusively in the intestine or neurons (Extended Data Fig. 10a–c). In neurons, polyQ67 aggregation causes behavioural deficits during ageing, including impaired chemotaxis and nose-touch avoidance compared with wild-type or polyQ19 controls27,28. Cold temperature partially rescued these neuronal deficits, whereas loss of cat-4 abolished this protection (Extended Data Fig. 10d,e). Conversely, muscle-specific overexpression of cat-4 reduced neuronal polyQ67 aggregation under both standard (20 °C) and warmer (25 °C) temperatures, preventing deficits in chemotaxis and nose-touch avoidance (Extended Data Fig. 10f–h). These results support a cell non-autonomous role for muscle CAT-4 activity in maintaining proteostasis in distal tissues.

We next investigated how elevated CAT-4 activity enhances proteostasis capacity. Cold temperature induces the proteasome activator PA28γ (human, encoded by PSME3)/PSME-3 (C. elegans), increasing trypsin-like proteasome activity2. Knockdown of psme-3 attenuated the ability of muscle-specific cat-4 overexpression to suppress neuronal polyQ67 aggregation, without altering total polyQ levels (Extended Data Fig. 11a), suggesting a broader defect in proteostasis rather than impaired degradation of polyQ proteins per se. Thus, CAT-4 activity appears to influence proteostasis through additional mechanisms. Besides proteasome activity, cold temperature also induces expression of different subunits of the TRiC/CCT complex10, a chaperonin that prevents polyQ-expanded aggregation29,30,31. Knockdown of cct-2 subunit completely abolished the protective effects of muscle-specific cat-4 overexpression at standard temperature (Extended Data Fig. 11b). By contrast, knockdown of fkb-6, a cold-induced co-chaperone of HSP9010, had no effect on polyQ67 aggregation (Extended Data Fig. 11c). We also tested hsf-1, a key transcription factor that protects against protein misfolding and aggregation32. Loss of hsf-1 partially attenuated the protective effects of muscle-specific cat-4 overexpression (Extended Data Fig. 11d), although less strongly than cct-2 or psme-3 knockdown.

Finally, we assessed whether PAH-1 activity contributes to these proteostasis mechanisms. Indeed, loss of pah-1 reduced the cold-induced upregulation of cct-2 and psme-3 expression, as well as the increase in trypsin-like proteasome activity (Extended Data Fig. 11e–g). Together, these results indicate that elevated CAT-4 activity and subsequent BH4-dependent tyrosine synthesis enhance proteostasis through different downstream mechanisms, including proteasome activity and the TRiC/CCT chaperonin complex.

We next examined whether BH2 supplementation prevents disease-related protein aggregation in human cells at standard temperature (37 °C). To this end, we generated HEK293 cells expressing mutant huntingtin exon 1 containing polyQ74 repeats, which is prone to aggregation33,34 (Fig. 5a). We found that supplementation with 10 µM BH2 markedly reduces polyQ aggregation without affecting overall protein levels (Fig. 5a). To determine whether BH2 also protects against aggregation of other disease-associated proteins, we assessed mutant variants of TDP-43 and FUS associated with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). In contrast to their wild-type counterparts, the ALS-causing mutant TDP-43(A382T) and FUS(P525L) variants aggregated in HEK293 human cells (Fig. 5b,c). Prompted by these findings, we examined whether BH2 attenuates disease-related neurodegeneration in human cells. Motor neurons differentiated from induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells expressing the severe ALS-linked FUS(P525L) mutation exhibit elevated apoptosis compared with isogenic control cells35. Notably, supplementation with either BH2 or tyrosine reduced apoptosis in ALS neurons (Fig. 5e,f). ALS motor neurons also showed elevated phosphorylation of receptor-interacting protein (RIP) kinase, a marker of necroptotic cell death, which was reduced by BH2 supplementation (Fig. 5g), further supporting a protective effect against disease-related neurodegeneration.

We then examined whether BH2 and tyrosine enhance proteostasis capacity through mechanisms similar to those activated by cold temperature in C. elegans. Indeed, both treatments caused a slight but significant increase in trypsin-like proteasome activity in HEK293 cells at standard temperature (Extended Data Fig. 12a,b). By contrast, neither treatment increased LC3 lipidation, a marker of autophagosomes (Extended Data Fig. 12c,d). As BH2 suppressed aggregation without significantly altering total levels of the pathological proteins (Fig. 5a–c), we examined additional proteostasis mechanisms. Consistent with our findings in worms, knockdown of the TRiC/CCT chaperonin subunit CCT2 reduced the ability of BH2 to suppress pathological protein aggregation, identifying TRiC/CCT-complex-dependent folding capacity as a key contributor to BH2-mediated proteostasis enhancement in human cells (Extended Data Fig. 12e).

In contrast to in C. elegans, mammalian cells express nitric oxide synthases (NOSs), which require BH4 as a cofactor16,18. Under conditions that decrease intracellular BH4 availability, such as oxidative stress—which promotes excessive oxidation of BH4 to BH2—or impaired DHFR activity, BH2 can compete with BH4 for binding to endothelial NOS (eNOS) in the vascular system36,37,38. This shifts eNOS activity from nitric oxide production toward superoxide generation, a process known as eNOS uncoupling38. Importantly, eNOS uncoupling is associated primarily with reduced BH4 availability and a consequent decrease in the BH4/BH2 ratio, rather than with elevated BH2 levels per se or BH2 supplementation. In fact, when cells retain an intact DHFR-dependent salvage pathway, BH2 supplementation increases intracellular BH4 levels and alleviates eNOS uncoupling under oxidative stress36,37. In contrast to paraquat-induced oxidative stress, neither 10 µM BH2 nor a 20-fold higher concentration increased the percentage of cells with detectable superoxide levels (Extended Data Fig. 12f). Moreover, even high doses of BH2 reduced pathological protein aggregation (Extended Data Fig. 12g), supporting the proteostasis benefits of BH2 supplementation without detectable superoxide production indicative of BH4 imbalance.

Given that oxidative stress promotes BH4 oxidation to BH2, we hypothesized that such conditions might limit the therapeutic potential of BH2 supplementation. Indeed, paraquat-induced oxidative stress abolished the protective effect of BH2 on protein aggregation (Extended Data Fig. 12h). By contrast, tyrosine supplementation retained its protective effect under oxidative stress (Extended Data Fig. 12i), suggesting that this downstream effector of the pathway can bypass conditions in which BH2 cannot be efficiently converted into BH4.

Here we identify BH2 as a cold-induced metabolite that links low temperature to systemic regulation of longevity and proteostasis (Extended Data Fig. 12j). Moderate cold increases CAT-4-dependent BH2 synthesis in muscle, from which BH2 is transported to distal tissues and converted into bioactive BH4 by DHFR-1. This establishes muscle as a metabolic hub that produces a pro-longevity signal of which the activity can be regulated locally in recipient tissues. BH4-dependent PAH-1 activity promotes tyrosine synthesis, and tyrosine supplementation recapitulates the longevity benefits of this pathway even at normal temperature, identifying the PAH-1–tyrosine axis as an important downstream mediator of the cold-induced BH2 pathway. The pathway also enhances organismal proteostasis: increasing BH2 synthesis in muscle suppresses pathological protein aggregation and preserves neuronal and intestinal function in distal tissues. Together, these findings expand the concept of cold-induced longevity beyond a predominantly cell-autonomous response to a systemic metabolic communication mechanism that coordinates longevity and proteostasis across tissues.

The ability of BH2 and tyrosine to suppress disease-associated protein aggregation and neurodegeneration-related phenotypes in human cells further supports functional conservation of this pathway. However, its translational potential may depend on the cellular context in which BH2 is converted to BH4. In mammals, BH4 is also required by NOS, and oxidative stress or impaired DHFR activity can disrupt the BH4/BH2 balance and promote eNOS uncoupling in endothelial cells36,37,38. BH2 supplementation did not increase detectable superoxide production in our cellular models, even at high concentrations, although these experiments do not directly address potential effects on NOS uncoupling in vivo. Consistent with the importance of BH2-to-BH4 conversion, we found that oxidative stress limits the protective effect of BH2, whereas tyrosine retains its protective effect. Thus, tyrosine may provide an alternative means of engaging this pathway when BH2-to-BH4 conversion is impaired. More broadly, our metabolomic analyses identified numerous additional metabolites induced by cold, raising the possibility that BH2 is one component of a larger network of systemic metabolic signals that coordinates the beneficial effects of low temperature. Defining these pathways may reveal additional strategies to reproduce the health-promoting effects of cold without altering body temperature.

The AcquireX Deep Scan workflow facilitated untargeted metabolite identification by iterative data-dependent acquisition. MS was performed at the CECAD Proteomics Facility. Temperature-comparison samples were analysed on the Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer equipped with a FAIMSpro differential ion mobility device coupled to an UltiMate 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Tyrosine-treatment samples were analysed on the Orbitrap Eclipse coupled to a Vanquish neo system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The samples were loaded onto a precolumn (Acclaim 5 µm PepMap 300 µ Cartridge or Ionopticks NanoShield, respectively) before being reverse-flushed onto an in-house-packed analytical column (30 cm length, 75 µm inner diameter, filled with 2.7 µm Poroshell EC120 C18, Agilent) or an Aurora Ultimate 25 cm column (Ionopticks), respectively. Peptides were chromatographically separated at a constant flow rate of 300 nl min−1 on a 90 min linear gradient running 0.1% formic acid against increasing amounts of 80% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. For measurements where FAIMS pro was used, it was operated at constant −50 V compensation voltage and electrode temperatures of 99.5 °C for the inner and 85 °C for the outer electrode.

For the gas-phase fractionated library57, a pool generated from all samples was analysed in six individual runs covering the range from 400 m/z to 1,000 m/z in 100 m/z increments. For each run, MS1 was acquired at 60,000 resolution with a maximum injection time of 98 ms and an AGC target of 100%. MS2 spectra were acquired at 30,000 resolution with a maximum injection time of 60 ms. Spectra were acquired in staggered 4 m/z windows, resulting in nominal 2 m/z windows after deconvolution using ProteoWizard58.

For each sample acquired on the Orbitrap Exploris 480, MS1 scans were obtained from 399 m/z to 1,001 m/z at 15,000 resolution. The maximum injection time was set to 22 ms and the AGC target was set to 100%. MS2 scans ranged from 400 m/z to 1,000 m/z and were acquired at 15,000 resolution with a maximum injection time of 22 ms and an AGC target of 100%. DIA scans covering the precursor range from 400 to 1,000 m/z and were acquired in 60 × 10 m/z windows with an overlap of 1 m/z. All of the scans were stored as a centroid. For samples acquired on Orbitrap Eclipse, the MS1 and MS2 resolution was set to 30,000 with a maximum injection time of 54 ms and identical AGC targets. The same range of 400 to 1,000 m/z was covered using 20 windows of 30 m/z each.

For samples acquired on the Orbitrap Exploris 480 (temperature-comparison datasets), the gas-phase fractionated library was build using DIA-NN (v.1.8.1)59 and the C. elegans proteomic database from WormBase (PRJNA13758.WBPS15, downloaded on 27 July 2021), with settings matching acquisition parameters. The samples were analysed in DIA-NN v.1.8.1 as well using the previously generated library and identical database. DIA-NN was run with the additional command line prompts ‘--report-lib-info’ and ‘--relaxed-prot-inf’. Further output settings were: filtered at 0.01 FDR, N-terminal methionine excision enabled, maximum number of missed cleavages set to 1, minimum peptide length set to 7, maximum peptide length set to 30, minimum precursor m/z set to 400, maximum precursor m/z set to 1,000 and cysteine carbamidomethylation enabled as a fixed modification. Next, the DIA-NN output was further filtered on a library q value and global q value of ≤ 0.01 and at least two unique peptides per protein using R (v.4.1.3). Then, label-free quantification values were calculated using the DIA-NN R package. The results were analysed using Perseus (v.1.6.15)60. Proteins were filtered for data completeness in at least one replicate group and missing values imputed using sigma downshift approach with standard settings. Next, FDR-controlled two-sided t-tests were performed between the experimental groups and WormBase IDs mapped to UniProt IDs.

For samples acquired on Orbitrap Eclipse (tyrosine-treatment datasets), a directDIA approach using Spectronaut 20.5 (Biognosys) was used with the GPF runs added as supporting runs and searched against the WormBase C. elegans proteomic database (PRJNA13758.WBPS19, downloaded 4 January 2025). The Spectronaut output was imported into R (v.4.5.2) for downstream analysis. Protein groups were expanded to individual UniProt identifiers, mapped to gene symbols using the C. elegans annotation database (org.Ce.eg.db, v.3.22.0), and protein abundances were summarized at the gene level using median values. Proteins with >50% missing values across samples were excluded. Protein intensities were log2-transformed after addition of a pseudocount of 1. Differential protein abundance analysis was conducted using the limma package (v.3.66.0) with two-sided empirical Bayes-moderated t-test. Proteins were considered differentially abundant when they exhibited an absolute log2-transformed fold change of >0.58 (corresponding to a 1.5-fold change) and a nominal P value < 0.05.

ORA was performed using the enrichGO function from the clusterProfiler package in R (v.4.5.2) to identify Gene Ontology (GO) terms enriched among differentially abundant proteins. Analyses were conducted using the org.Ce.eg.db annotation database (v.3.22.0). GO Biological Process and Molecular Function ontologies were analysed using a one-sided hypergeometric test. Multiple-testing correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, with significance thresholds of P < 0.05 and q < 0.2. For visualization, enrichment results were converted to gene ratios (GeneRatio), representing the proportion of input genes associated with each GO term. Dot plots were generated using ggplot2 (v.4.0.3), with the x axis representing the gene ratio, the y axis indicating GO terms, point size corresponding to the number of genes associated with each term (Count) and point colour representing statistical significance (−log10[P]).

The temperature-dependent gene expression data from day 3 adult C. elegans at 15 °C and 25 °C were obtained from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE62297 (ref. 12). Raw data were reanalysed using the database’s in-built GEO2R web tool with default parameters, including multiple-testing correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to calculate FDR-adjusted P values.

Bacterial invasion assays were performed as previously described21,22. After development at 20 °C, synchronized adult worms were either kept at 20 °C or transferred to 15 °C until the ages specified in the corresponding figures. Chemotaxis tests were performed on freshly poured agar plates (2% agar, 5 mM KPO4 at pH 6.0, 1 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgSO4). The plates were sectioned into four quadrants, along with an inner circle measuring approximately 1 cm across diagonally. A benzaldehyde attractant (0.5% benzaldehyde in ethanol supplemented with 0.25 M sodium azide) was applied to one pair of diagonal quadrants, while the opposite quadrants received a control mixture (ethanol + 0.25 M sodium azide). On the specified adult days (as indicated in figure legends), worms were collected in S-Basal medium, washed three times to remove residual bacteria and deposited at the centre of the plate. After sealing with Parafilm, the plates were incubated at the indicated temperatures for 90 min. Worms were then counted in each quadrant, excluding those remaining within the central circle. The chemotaxis index was calculated using the following formula: (number of animal in attractant quadrants − number of animals in control quadrants)/total number of animals (ref. 67).

Age-synchronized worms were assessed for nose-touch response according to established protocols28,50,68,69. In brief, the worms were placed onto a thin lawn of bacteria and allowed to move forward. For each experimental group, 40 worms were tested, and their responses to 10 gentle eyelash touches were recorded.
