#1079. AI models keep hacking real systems during tests. What does this mean?

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

AI models have repeatedly broken into real commercial and government systems during safety tests during 2026. Cybersecurity researcher Thorsten Holz explains what that means, and why he isn't expecting a robot uprising.

Let's start with people's real world fears about AI. Here's a sample of people we asked:

Sebastian, 22, says it'll be an AI-powered robot uprising that finally wipes out humanity: "I think that'll take some time," he says, admitting his dystopia owes a lot to the 2004 film "I, Robot."

Luisa, 19, has heard the rumors too. Her fear is what happens when these tools get into the wrong hands. "I'm more afraid of people," she says. "Powerful people."

Pilar, 25, is pessimistic about humanity's prospects. "The world is going down," she says. "Hopefully after my lifetime."

In 2026, AI systems have repeatedly slipped out of human control — and at some of the world's largest AI companies.

OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Meta have all disclosed in 2026 that their AI agents escaped the controlled test environments.

Agents are AI systems that don't just answer questions but act — running code, browsing the web, clicking through systems — on their own.

Thorsten Holz, scientific director at the Max Planck Institute for Security and Privacy in Germany, is among the researchers who test AI systems.

"It feels a bit crazy how powerful these models have become." Holz told DW. "I didn't anticipate they'd be so obsessed with solving tasks and that they'd start to do things we never [foresaw]."

Holz is one of 16 authors of ExploitGym. ExploitGym is an AI benchmark published in May 2026 by a team led by the University of California, Berkeley, with researchers from Anthropic, OpenAI and Google. It is a standardized test of cybersecurity capabilities and vulnerabilities.

Its 898 challenges test whether AI agents can turn known software bugs into working attacks. Each one runs inside a sandbox — a sealed-off digital space, cut off from the internet, so nothing the agent does can reach anything real. That's the theory, anyway.

In July, OpenAI disclosed that two of its AI models had escaped from their sandbox.

They were running an internal ExploitGym test with the models' safety refusals switched off, a setting that lets evaluators measure what a model is capable of rather than what it will decline to do.

Failing the task, the models went looking for a shortcut instead, and found a flaw in the software meant to keep them sealed in. It let them onto the open internet.

From there they worked out that Hugging Face, a site where AI developers store and share models and data, probably held the answer to the test.

So, they broke in and went looking for it, organizing the effort on message boards they set up themselves. The behavior was not instructed, according to OpenAI's account of the incident.

Hugging Face detected the intrusion and shut it down before OpenAI connected it to its own test. No customer data was reportedly taken.

"What happened is really a bit of science fiction," Holz said.

Then, Google confirmed that its Gemini model had guessed or found login credentials and accessed three real companies' websites during a May test run by the independent evaluator Irregular — an intrusion Google learned of in July 2026 and disclosed weeks later.

Anthropic's and Meta's escapes happened in sandboxes run by the same firm, which has said it notified the labs in late July 2026 and that it has since fixed the flaws.

In late September 2026, Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese said an OpenAI agent had broken into a statistics portal belonging to Medicare, Australia's public health system, reaching non-public files and writing data into a government server. No patient records were reportedly touched.

The incidents have revived older fears. If agents can program each other, where does that end? Could it lead to what philosopher Nick Bostrom has called a "paperclip maximizer"?

Bostrom's thought experiment imagines a machine given one simple goal: Make as many paperclips as possible.

The machine pursues the task so single-mindedly that it eventually treats humans as raw material standing in the way.

"For the intermediate future, I do not see any kind of scientific evidence that there could be this super intelligence that autonomously decides, 'Okay, let's kill,'" said Holz.

Rogue software needs vast datacenters to run, he said, which makes it visible. And the internet is built from parts that can keep working independently of one another.

But the AI escapes and hacks in 2026 were noticed late. Google learned of Gemini's intrusions two months after they happened, and OpenAI told Australia about the Medicare breach nearly three months after the event.

Holz said he expects a different kind of threat: Bad actors turning capable AI on critical infrastructure, mass compromise of ordinary machines, or chatbots used to manipulate information at scale and destabilize politics.

No, Europe cannot easily compete with China, according to experts.

France's Mistral and Germany's open-source Soofi project lag behind the frontier labs — a handful of US and Chinese firms building the most advanced models. Europe lacks the datacenter capacity to train systems at that scale, which leaves it dependent on non-European models, and largely subject to US or Chinese export controls.

For Holz, the more pressing gap is expertise. "What we definitely need to see is how we can build up more competence in the area of security, AI, and especially the intersection of both," he said.

Be skeptical of what the companies themselves claim, Holz said. They have a financial interest in the conversation, he said, particularly ahead of a stock market listing. "There's also fear mongering, or a bit of hype, about how advanced these models get."

Holz uses AI daily, as do his children. His nine-year-old generates coloring book pages. His 12-year-old uses it for homework, but has already learned the hard way that it lies and that "it's sometimes wrong."

#1075. How John Murtough will supercharge Birmingham City's Premier League mission: Cutting out expensive mistakes, elite contacts book and academy revolution

Published: 11:11 BST, 23 October 2026 | Updated: 14:53 BST, 23 October 2026

If anyone thought Birmingham were scaling back their ambitions after failing to reach the Premier League last season, they should think again today.

As exclusively revealed by Daily Mail Sport, Blues have appointed former Manchester United transfer chief John Murtough as their new director of football.

The remit is wide and yet narrow. He will oversee every area of the club but when the 55-year-old was approached for the job, the priority will have been clear – help us reach the Premier League.

Daily Mail Sport looks at why Blues went for Murtough and what he will be expected to deliver at St Andrew’s...

Murtough was director of football for three years at Old Trafford and left in April 2024, soon after Sir Jim Ratcliffe took control. He had also been United’s academy manager and director of development.

Knighthead’s plan for Blues is predicated on reaching the Premier League. They have invested heavily in transfers but not always made the right calls, as the summer 2025 recruitment drive proved. High wages were paid to players like Kyogo Furuhashi, who had little impact and left after a year.

John Murtough - Birmingham City's new director of football - is pictured with Erik ten Hag at his Manchester United unveiling

Blues have made some costly mistakes in the transfer window in recent years - including signing Kyogo Furuhashi (right)

During 11 years at Old Trafford, Murtough became known as ‘The Fixer’ and was instrumental in the appointment of Erik ten Hag. While the Dutchman divided opinion, he did deliver the FA Cup and the Carabao Cup – no small achievements in the post-Sir Alex Ferguson era.

Murtough was a driving force behind the women’s team at United. He also overhauled the approach to academy recruitment to target some of the best age-group players overseas. One of those who arrived on his watch was Alejandro Garnacho, who scored 26 goals and had 22 assists in 144 games and was sold to Chelsea for £40million.

Even though Murtough was criticised when players like Antony and Rasmus Hojlund struggled, there are those at United who feel his influence has been missed since his departure.

Birmingham hope to enjoy the fruits of that now. When Knighthead took control three years ago, they spoke of hiring ‘exceptional leaders’. The Americans are convinced Murtough fits the bill.

Nobody would doubt Craig Gardner’s commitment or work ethic, yet his approach to recruitment could be haphazard at times.

Blues are convinced that will change under Murtough. The summer window was overseen by the impressive Joe Carnall, whose reputation in the game is growing, and he would be a strong lieutenant to Murtough.

Carnall is an expert in coding and analytics tools. There are numerous metrics within Blues’ data model, most of which are guarded closely to ensure rivals do not gain a competitive edge. But no player will be signed unless he clears the data checks.

In recent times, Blues under Knighthead have insisted any transfer target should have up to three seasons’ senior football under his belt. They have also prioritised durability and have prioritised players in their mid-20s, although Colombian midfielder Jhon Solis is a notable exception. Solis has only just turned 22 but is already worth at least double the £6.5m Blues paid Girona for him last summer.

It will be interesting to see whether Murtough tries to alter this. Blues believe his vast experience and long list of contacts across the global game will open doors for them. Though Gardner did a fine job as technical director and was admired by Knighthead, Murtough is the kind of guy who can make football’s big players pick up the phone.

Jhon Solis is an exception to Birmingham's transfer rule of recent seasons - but already worth far more than Blues paid for him

Birmingham chiefs will hope there are more good times ahead with Murtough in the boardroom

Birmingham's youth set-up has been one of the top priorities for Knighthead since they took charge. After years of neglect by previous ownership groups, the Americans feel they have been playing catch-up for too long.

While Aston Villa have invested heavily in their academy and West Bromwich Albion’s is admired across the country, Blues have struggled. Blues’ academy was awarded category one status in May 2025 – a significant step forward – but attracting top talent across the region and beyond has still been a challenge.

Of course, Birmingham did produce Jude Bellingham, possibly the finest English footballer of his generation and one of the best on the planet. His younger brother, Jobe, and Jordan James, also made the first team before moving on but Blues want more.

If anyone can rectify that, it is Murtough. During his first spell with Everton’s academy, Wayne Rooney advanced to the first team. He also helped shape the system that delivered Jack Rodwell and Ross Barkley to the senior side. At United, Kobbie Mainoo progressed from a nine-year-old hopeful to a Premier League starter while Murtough was in place. Anthony Elanga and Garnacho also had useful United careers.

In the era of strict financial rules, developing homegrown players is essential – even if they do not establish themselves in the first team. As unromantic as it is, these players can be sold for pure profit with the money reinvested into recruitment.

The best place to implement such plans is the Premier League. Along with boss Chris Davies and his squad, Birmingham are counting on Murtough to take them there.

Angelman syndrome is a severe neurodevelopmental disorder caused by the loss of UBE3A, a gene that encodes an E3 ubiquitin ligase1,2. The mechanisms that underlie Angelman syndrome are not well understood, and in particular, the link between UBE3A substrates and disease phenotypes remains uncertain3. Here, using gain- and loss-of-function mutations in UBE3A4,5 along with unbiased proteomics, we show that asparagine synthetase (ASNS) is a target of UBE3A. We find that ASNS protein abundance is increased in the brains of Ube3a-null mice and in human neurons6. Genetic correction of ASNS overabundance rescues microcephaly and many behavioural deficits. Detailed molecular analyses show that ASNS overabundance activates stress signalling and causes a global reduction in neuronal protein synthesis. Our results suggest that loss of UBE3A traps neurons in a state of persistent stress and stunts neuronal growth during developmentally sensitive periods. Moreover, our results indicate that targeting ASNS and the stress response could hold therapeutic potential in Angelman syndrome.

Angelman syndrome (AS) occurs in 1:20,000 births and is characterized by intellectual disability, developmental delay, epilepsy, microcephaly, dysmorphic facial features and a unique behavioural profile1,2. It is caused by deletion or mutation of the maternally inherited copy of UBE3A, a gene also linked to mood disorders7,8, autism4,5,7 and cancer9,10. It is widely accepted that phenotypes in AS arise due to the dysregulation of UBE3A substrates during development. However, although numerous substrates have been proposed3, establishing their link to AS phenotypes has proved difficult, limiting researchers’ mechanistic understanding of this disorder.

UBE3A is the founding member of the HECT (homologous to E6-AP C terminus) domain family of E3 ubiquitin ligases, all of which have a conserved HECT domain that contains the necessary biochemical machinery to accept activated ubiquitin from E2 enzymes and to transfer it to target proteins9. UBE3A expression is imprinted specifically in neurons such that only the maternally inherited allele is expressed, with the paternal allele being epigenetically silenced11. This imprinting mechanism, along with many disease-related phenotypes, is conserved in AS model mice that lack a maternal copy of Ube3a (Ube3am−/p+)12. These phenotypes include motor and behavioural deficits, and microcephaly, the most prominent neuroanatomical deficit in AS13. Microcephaly is not present at birth in mice but is acquired postnatally, driven by a failure of neuronal growth that results in shorter and simplified dendritic arbors and smaller-diameter axons14,15.

The integrated stress response (ISR) is a conserved pathway that helps maintain cellular homeostasis in response to noxious stimuli, including proteotoxic stress, nutrient deprivation, viral infection and oxidative stress16. These stimuli are sensed by four specialized kinases (PERK, GCN2, PKR and HRI) that converge on phosphorylation of the α-subunit of eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 (p-eIF2α) to suppress global protein synthesis and initiate compensatory transcription programs. Asparagine synthetase (ASNS) is a conserved metabolic enzyme that is induced by the ATF4 transcription factor in response to amino acid deprivation17. ASNS catalyses the ATP-dependent synthesis of asparagine and glutamate from aspartate and glutamine, but its role in neuronal development remains poorly understood.

To identify substrates of UBE3A, we used cell lines expressing gain-of-function (GOF) and loss-of-function (LOF) mutants4,5 and subjected them to stable isotype labelling by amino acids in cell culture (SILAC), tryptic remnant immunoaffinity enrichment (di-Gly purification) and mass spectrometry18 (Fig. 1a). We performed this experiment in quadruplicate and identified proteins whose ubiquitination levels were enriched by at least 20% in cells expressing a hyperactivating mutation in UBE3A (Supplementary Table 1). Because of technical issues in this experiment (see Methods for details), the total numbers of detected proteins varied across replicates. However, comparing unique proteins across replicates allowed us to compile a list of 23 proteins that could serve as potential substrates (Fig. 1b). Within this list were novel substrates, as well as many proteins that are known to bind to or become ubiquitinated by UBE3A (Fig. 1c).

In both experiments, the abundance of the catalytic subunit of DNA-dependent protein kinase (DNA-PKc), ASNS and the ribosomal protein RPL3 was affected by UBE3A in a manner consistent with these proteins being substrates of UBE3A (Fig. 1d,e and Extended Data Fig. 1a–d). We next reconstituted an in vitro ubiquitin reaction consisting of recombinant human ASNS, UBE1 (E1), UBCH7 (E2), UBE3A, ubiquitin and ATP. We found that UBE3A was required to catalyse the time-dependent formation of high-molecular-weight ubiquitin chains on ASNS while simultaneously reducing the level of unmodified ASNS (Extended Data Fig. 2c–e). Finally, we examined ASNS ubiquitination in UBE3A-KD cells expressing HA-tagged ASNS and Flag-tagged ubiquitin and treated with MG132 (30 μM) for 1 h. A modest decrease in UBE3A produced a proportional 25% decrease in ASNS ubiquitination (Extended Data Fig. 2f,g), suggesting that UBE3A is the principal E3 enzyme targeting ASNS for proteasomal degradation in these cells.

Our proteomics experiment identified five candidate ubiquitination sites on ASNS: K83 and K168, which are poorly conserved and located in the glutamine hydrolysing domain; and K467, K478 and K504, which are more highly conserved and are found in the ASNS domain (Extended Data Fig. 2h). To confirm UBE3A-dependent ubiquitination at these sites, we generated ASNS lysine-to-arginine (KR) mutants and co-transfected them with Flag-tagged ubiquitin and UBE3A into HEK293T cells. Whereas ubiquitination of the K83R and K168R mutants resembled what was observed with WT ASNS, it was reduced or altered for the K467R, K478R and K504R mutants (Extended Data Fig. 2i). In steady-state experiments, WT UBE3A reduced the abundance of WT ASNS and the K83R and K168R mutants, but no changes were observed for the K467R, K478R and K504R mutants (Extended Data Fig. 2j,k). Because degradative ubiquitination is rarely directed by a single lysine, we cannot rule out changes in cooperativity, ubiquitination linkage or ubiquitination kinetics arising from K467R, K478R and K504R mutations. Nonetheless, these results strongly suggest that ubiquitination in the asparagine synthase domain is crucial for ASNS degradation.

We next assessed UBE3A-mediated ASNS regulation in vivo by analysing ASNS protein levels and ubiquitination in the brains of Ube3am−/p+ mice (referred to as Angelman mice). In hippocampal tissue, immunoprecipitated ASNS showed a reduction in ubiquitination of about 50% in Angelman mice, compared with WT controls (Extended Data Fig. 3a,b). Similarly, ASNS abundance was increased in the hippocampus (130.63 ± 3.92% above WT) and cortex (114.55 ± 3.60%) with a trend in the midbrain, but not in cerebellar samples (Extended Data Fig. 3c). These changes were not due to an increase in Asns transcription, because quantitative PCR (qPCR) of hippocampal RNA showed that there was no difference in Asns mRNA levels (Extended Data Fig. 3d). Finally, ASNS protein levels in hippocampal lysates from mice with an equivalent maternal GOF T485A mutation in UBE3A (mT503A in mice21) showed a subtle but consistent decrease (Extended Data Fig. 3e,f). Altogether, our biochemical experiments show that UBE3A ubiquitinates ASNS both in vitro and in vivo.

ASNS is expressed widely across tissues and cell types21; however, its expression in the brain is not well characterized. We visualized Asns transcripts in mouse glutamatergic neurons, GABAergic neurons and non-neuronal cells using whole-brain single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomic data22. Notably, Asns was expressed broadly in both excitatory and inhibitory neurons but was mostly absent in non-neuronal cells (Fig. 2a). When we extended this analysis to cell subclasses across the brain, we found that Asns was expressed in most neuronal subclasses but was undetectable in nearly all non-neuronal cells (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Table 3). We further confirmed this using a published scRNA-seq dataset from the mouse hippocampus23, which again showed that Asns expression was enriched in neurons (Fig. 2c), a pattern that matches the neuron-specific imprinting of Ube3a.

To characterize ASNS protein levels in situ, we validated an antibody against mouse ASNS protein using mouse Neuro2a cells transfected with a plasmid that expresses EGFP along with an shRNA that targets mouse Asns. We confirmed the specificity of the antibody through immunofluorescence experiments (Extended Data Fig. 4a–d). We first examined ASNS protein in the developing brain, focusing on the cortex and hippocampus. In mice at postnatal day 0 (P0), ASNS staining was most prominent in the cortical neuronal layers. Cell bodies and apical dendrites were prominently labelled, whereas axonal fibres showed subtle labelling, and staining was mostly absent from proliferative zones adjacent to the lateral ventricles (Fig. 2d). By P10, ASNS immunoreactivity was diffuse throughout the cortex and hippocampus, with more prominent somatic labelling in deeper-layer cortical neurons and the pyramidal layers of the developing hippocampus. By P21, ASNS staining was intermittent throughout the brain and enriched in deep layers of the cortex, the dentate gyrus, neuronal layers of the hippocampus, the amygdala, and thalamus (Fig. 2d,e). In other regions, ASNS strongly labelled most serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine; 5-HT)-positive serotonergic neurons of the dorsal raphe (88.20 ± 5.77% of cells) and a subset of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)-positive dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta (62.36 ± 3.83% of cells), suggesting that ASNS protein is heterogeneously maintained across the brain (Fig. 2f,g).

To test whether these ASNS-enriched cells were neurons, we co-stained for markers of neurons (NeuN), astrocytes (GFAP) and microglia (IBA1) in the cortex and hippocampus. Virtually all ASNS-positive cells were co-localized with NeuN, confirming that ASNS transcript and protein are predominantly neuronal (Fig. 2h–j). We next asked whether the heterogeneous labelling was stochastic or systematic by examining ASNS in inhibitory cells of the stratum oriens and stratum radiatum. If stochastic, ASNS-enriched cells should be represented equally across inhibitory neuron subclasses. If systematic, then ASNS enrichment should be biased towards specific subclasses. We co-stained brain slices for ASNS and several widely used markers of interneurons, including parvalbumin (PV), somatostatin (SST), calretinin (CR) and neuropeptide Y (NPY) (Extended Data Fig. 4e–g). Of note, all ASNS-positive cells were NPY-positive (PV, 56.94 ± 6.91%; SST, 57.5 ± 3.82%; CR, 22.62 ± 12.43%; NPY, 100%), suggesting that ASNS enrichment is not stochastic but maintained by functionally distinct classes of cells.

Finally, we asked whether loss of UBE3A increases ASNS protein abundance in selected neuronal populations. Using immunofluorescence, we examined ASNS abundance in NYP-positive interneurons and CaMKIIα-positive excitatory neurons in the hippocampus (Extended Data Fig. 4h–k). We saw a subtle increase in in both excitatory and inhibitory cells, which suggests that the loss of UBE3A affects ASNS abundance broadly rather than in specific cell populations.

We next wondered whether correcting ASNS overabundance could affect aberrant phenotypes in Angelman mice. So far, only one Asns hypomorphic mouse line has been published24. We therefore generated a new null line that contains a CRISPR–Cas9-targeted nonsense mutation in Asns. We crossed heterozygous Asns-null males (Asns+/−) with paternal-null Ube3a females (Ube3am+/p−) to generate four genotypes: WT (Ube3am+/p+, Asns+/+), Het (Ube3am+/p+, Asns+/−), Angelman (Ube3am−/p+, Asns+/+) and Rescue (Ube3am−/p+, Asns+/−) (Fig. 3a). As expected, western blotting and immunostaining analysis showed that Het mice had roughly 50% of WT ASNS protein levels, and ASNS abundance in Rescue mice returned to near WT levels (Fig. 3b and Extended Data Fig. 5a–c).

Behavioural phenotypes in Angelman mice are well characterized12 and we used a range of assays to examine these established phenotypes (Fig. 3c). Angelman mice are known to exhibit adult-onset obesity, which becomes apparent around 3 months of age14. We observed a similar obesity phenotype that was greatly improved in Rescue mice, independent of sex (Fig. 3d and Extended Data Fig. 5d). In the open field assay, Angelman mice ambulated less and spent more time in the centre of the arena than control mice did25. Notably, Rescue mice were indistinguishable from WT mice in terms of time spent in the centre of the arena (Fig. 3e), even though they showed deficits in the total distance travelled (Fig. 3f). We found additional phenotypic improvements in Rescue mice in the hindlimb clasping assay (Fig. 3g) and the marble bury assay (Fig. 3h,i). Notably, Het mice performed indistinguishably from WT mice in every assay.

Reducing the overabundance of ASNS did not rescue all phenotypes. For example, Rescue mice showed no improvement in the rotarod assay (Extended Data Fig. 5e). Angelman mice are known to skip a period of sleep, known as the siesta, that normally occurs during a mouse’s active phase26. We observed a similar phenotype in our Angelman mice, but sleep measures did not improve in Rescue mice (Extended Data Fig. 5f–h). These results indicate that many—but not all—AS phenotypes in mice result from the dysregulation of ASNS, suggesting that there are additional disease-relevant substrates.

Given that ASNS maintains amino acid homeostasis, we investigated the effects of UBE3A loss on the levels of amino acids in the brains of Angelman mice. We isolated hippocampi from all four genotypes at P21 for metabolomic profiling (Fig. 4a). As expected, levels of asparagine were decreased in Het and Rescue mice, both of which lack one copy of Asns (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Table 4). Conversely, asparagine levels were increased in Angelman mice, as compared with WT controls, functionally confirming the overabundance of ASNS in these mice. Of note, no amino acid was decreased, but rather, we observed significantly increased levels of phenylalanine, glycine and proline, as well as a trend towards increased levels of glutamine, histidine, leucine and tyrosine (Fig. 4b). All of these changes returned to baseline in Rescue mice, suggesting that they resulted from ASNS overabundance.

Loss of amino acid homeostasis is closely linked to the ISR, a conserved pathway that ultimately halts protein synthesis. Activation of the ISR is typically detected through increased phosphorylation of the eIF2α translation initiation factor at serine 51 (p-eIF2α)27. To determine whether this pathway was engaged in Angelman mice, we assessed the levels of p-eIF2α by western blot in brain hemisphere lysates. We observed a significant increase in p-eIF2α in Angelman mice (176.43 ± 7.13% of WT), indicating increased ISR signalling (Fig. 4c). Notably, this increase was improved by correcting ASNS overabundance in Rescue mice (p-eIF2α; 140.56 ± 10.94% of WT).

We next asked whether simple overexpression of ASNS is sufficient to activate the ISR in neurons. We used an adeno-associated virus (AAV) encoding EGFP or ASNS-T2A-EGFP (in which a 2A self-cleaving peptide (T2A) is inserted at the 5′ end of Asns) in cultured mouse cortical neurons, and assessed their effect on ISR signalling. Overexpression of ASNS was sufficient to increase the levels of p-eIF2α above EGFP-alone controls (129.27 ± 2.20% of control) (Extended Data Fig. 6a,b), indicating that ASNS overabundance alone is sufficient to activate the ISR.

Amino acid starvation activates a branch of the ISR known as the amino acid response (AAR). Because our metabolomic studies showed that free amino acid concentrations were altered in Angelman mice, we performed RNA-seq to determine whether the AAR is genuinely engaged in these mice. We sequenced RNA extracted from the cortex and hippocampus of P21 WT, Angelman and Rescue mice. We found few differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the hippocampus of Angelman mice relative to WT controls (51 DEGs; false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05) with only eight highly changed genes (log2-transformed fold expression < −0.75 or > 0.75; Extended Data Fig. 6c and Supplementary Table 5). By contrast, we found a much stronger signal in the cortex (1,717 DEGs, 777 up, 940 down; FDR < 0.05) with 189 highly changed genes (Fig. 4d,e and Supplementary Table 5). Given this more robust transcriptional response, we focused subsequent analyses on the cortex dataset.

We found only 22 DEGs (FDR < 0.05) in Het mice and 1,214 DEGs (623 up, 591 down; FDR < 0.05) in Rescue mice, 805 of which overlapped with Angelman mice (Fig. 5e). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis showed that downregulated genes implicated pathways related to mitochondrial function and protein synthesis, whereas upregulated genes were enriched for pathways related to neuronal growth and synaptic function (Fig. 4f,g). Notably, the significance of these changes was uniformly weakened in Rescue mice (Fig. 4f–i), strongly suggesting that they occurred as a direct result of ASNS overabundance. We specifically examined genes related to the ISR (GO: 0140467) and found only a weak enrichment, with just 7 of 51 genes in this set differentially expressed in Angelman mice (Extended Data Fig. 6d). We further compared our data with RNA-seq data from mice that lack Slc7a5, a gene encoding an essential amino acid transporter at the blood–brain barrier28. These mice show increased expression of genes encoding amino acid transporters (Slc7a1 and Slc7a3), transcription factors that are central to the ISR (Atf4 and Atf5) and transfer RNA (tRNA) synthases (Extended Data Fig. 6e). However, we did not detect changes in any of these genes in any of our genotypes, suggesting that these pathways are not activated in Angelman mice.

Of note, we found that Eif2ak1 is upregulated in Angelman mice and returned to baseline in Rescue mice (Extended Data Fig. 6d). Eif2ak1 encodes a kinase called HRI (haem-regulated inhibitor of translation), which is classically known to phosphorylate eIF2α in response to low levels of haem in the cell29. More recent work has found that HRI promotes mitophagy30, and that its expression is induced in neurons in response to proteasome inhibition31. Consistent with our RNA-seq results, HRI protein abundance was increased in the cortex of Angelman mice (Extended Data Fig. 6f,g), and in addition, we found that overexpression of ASNS in neurons was sufficient to increase HRI abundance (Extended Data Fig. 6j,k). By contrast, overexpression of ASNS did not activate GCN2 (general control nonderepressible 2), the kinase that phosphorylates eIF2α during the AAR (Extended Data Fig. 6h,i), nor did it induce changes in the two other upstream kinases PERK and PKR (Extended Data Fig. 6j,k). Together, these observations suggest that ASNS overabundance in Angelman mice drives a downregulation of mitochondrial and protein synthesis pathways, and that upregulation of the HRI kinase leads to increased phosphorylation of eIF2α.

We next examined protein synthesis directly, using two different strategies. First, we measured new protein synthesis in vivo by using intraperitoneal injections of l-azidohomoalanine (AHA). AHA is a cell- and blood–brain-barrier-permeable synthetic amino acid that is charged onto methionine tRNAs and incorporates into newly synthesized proteins32. Proteins containing AHA can be labelled and visualized using a process known as bioorthogonal noncanonical amino acid tagging (BONCAT)32. We extracted proteins from mouse brains 16 h after injection (Fig. 5a), and found that on average there was a four-to-five-fold reduction in the rates of new protein synthesis in Angelman mice, compared with WT mice (Fig. 5b,c). This deficit was greatly improved in our Rescue mice (65.949 ± 7.569 of WT). Second, we performed polysome profiling in all four genotypes by isolating actively translating ribosomes from the whole brain at P21 (Fig. 5d). Quantification of polysome/monosome ratios revealed a significant shift towards monosome fractions in Angelman mice, compared with WT mice (Angelman 72.94 ± 2.83% of WT; Het, 95.57 ± 4.32% of WT; Rescue, 95.08 ± 5.12% of WT), indicating decreased translational efficiency by polysomes (Fig. 5e). Again, this deficit was improved in our Rescue mice. We further examined whether ASNS overabundance alone is sufficient to slow new protein synthesis in neurons. Cultured neurons overexpressing ASNS were treated with AHA, and fluorescent noncanonical amino acid tagging (FUNCAT)33 was performed to visualize the incorporation of AHA. AHA fluorescence was reduced by around 50% in neurons overexpressing ASNS (Extended Data Fig. 7a,b), suggesting that ASNS overabundance causes a global slowing of protein synthesis.

Finally, we sought to understand how reduced protein synthesis contributes to disease-relevant phenotypes. Previous studies showed that microcephaly in Angelman model mice arises from impaired neuronal growth that stunts axon and dendrite development14,15. We hypothesized that a reduction in neuronal protein synthesis contributes to poor neuronal growth, underlying the microcephaly observed in these mice. We measured brain weights in all four genotypes at P21. Consistent with previous reports, we observed a significant reduction in brain weights in Angelman mice14. This deficit was greatly improved in Rescue mice (Fig. 5f), independent of sex (Extended Data Fig. 7c). A previous study reported that smaller neurons in Angelman model mice cause greater neuronal packing in the cortex, leading to increased neuronal densities14. We therefore analysed neuronal density, and found that this measure was also improved in our Rescue mice (Extended Data Fig. 7d,e). Altogether, these data suggest that ASNS overabundance triggers the ISR and reduces the rate of protein synthesis, which, in turn, negatively affects neuronal growth and development in AS.

Identifying disease-relevant substrates in AS remains difficult, given the promiscuity of UBE3A promiscuity and its modest effect on substrate abundance. Here, we addressed this challenge by using disease-linked UBE3A mutants4,5,34, combined with di-Gly purification and mass spectrometry. Although ASNS seems to be a major contributor to AS, not all phenotypes were rescued by ASNS correction, suggesting that other substrates contribute to overall pathology. Two limitations of our proteomics experiments are worth noting. First, although it is unlikely, we cannot rule out that endogenous UBE3A competed with overexpressed UBE3A for binding substrate proteins. Second, because our workflow relied on affinity purification of ubiquitinated proteins, low abundance or rapidly degraded proteins might be under-represented in our data. Future studies using deeper proteomic coverage or complementary approaches will be needed to fully define the UBE3A substrate landscape relevant to AS pathology.

Our study found that Asns expression is limited mainly to neurons (Fig. 2), matching the neuron-specific imprinting pattern of Ube3a. Asparagine is a non-essential amino acid that is readily attained through the diet, but at the blood–brain barrier, it must be transported using a bidirectional facilitated transporter system (System N)35. However, rather than driving the accumulation of asparagine into the brain, this system seems to remove it from the cerebrospinal fluid36. We hypothesize that neurons rely on ASNS to synthesize most, if not all, of the asparagine they need, and that perturbations to ASNS represent a point of susceptibility in development.

Homozygous loss of ASNS causes asparagine synthetase deficiency, a rare congenital condition characterized by microcephaly, global developmental delay, hypotonia, spastic quadriplegia and intractable seizures37. However, contrary to a loss of ASNS function, our study found increased levels of ASNS protein in Angelman model mice, suggesting that a gain in ASNS function causes aberrant phenotypes. In support of this idea, we found that overexpression of ASNS alone in neurons was sufficient to increase both p-eIF2α and HRI levels (Extended Data Fig. 6a,j), and that genetic reduction of ASNS expression rescued protein synthesis as well as many aberrant behavioural phenotypes (Figs. 3 and 5). GOF mutations in ASNS have yet to be established as a cause of neurodevelopmental disorders; however, our results suggest that such a mechanism is plausible.

Our finding that free amino acids in the brains of Angelman mice are elevated (Fig. 4b) is consistent with our observation that neuronal protein synthesis is reduced with the loss of UBE3A. However, contrary to our original hypothesis, we did not find an activation of the AAR in Angelman mice. The only ISR-associated gene that was upregulated in Angelman mice and rescued with Asns modulation was Eif2ak1, the gene that encodes HRI. It is notable that HRI is induced in neurons upon proteostatic stress31, and this observation, combined with UBE3A’s known role at the proteasome18,38,39,40, suggests that proteostatic stress has a crucial role in AS pathology. Moreover, HRI mediates both mitochondrial maintenance30 and protein synthesis31, two pathways that are highly implicated in the current study. Whether these changes result from increased asparagine production, or through an alternative unidentified activity of ASNS, remains to be elucidated.

Our study found a reduction in new protein synthesis and a reduction in polysome, but not monosome, fractions in neurons (Fig. 5d). Previous work has shown that monosomes are predominantly localized to neuronal processes and are capable of synthesizing proteins from at least a subset of transcripts41. Future studies will be required to address whether monosome-dependent forms of translation are preserved in Ube3am−/p+ neurons.

Finally, our results have therapeutic implications. Our finding that persistent stress signalling is central to AS pathology places it among a growing list of neurodevelopmental disorders in which disrupted proteostasis and heightened stress signalling are a common mechanism of pathology28,42,43. This suggests that targeting ASNS, HRI or the ISR could alleviate at least some symptoms in AS and could complement genetically targeted therapies to improve their safety and efficacy.

Differential expression analysis was performed using edgeR56 (v.4.0.16) in R after retaining genes with counts per million (CPM) > 1 in fewer than three samples and TMM normalization; generalized linear models included sex (~Sex + Genotype) for tissue-specific analyses with significance assessed by quasi-likelihood F-tests (FDR < 0.05). For exon-level analysis of Ube3a (NM_011668.3) and Asns (NM_012055.5), featureCounts was run in exon mode (-f) with reverse strand specificity (-s 2) and fractional assignment of multi-mapping reads (-M–fraction), and exon counts from RefSeq Select transcripts were normalized by exon length to obtain counts per kilobase.

Using the Allen Institute’s whole-mouse-brain single-cell RNA-seq dataset, we calculated the mean and variance of Asns log2 expression and its corresponding log2 expression z-score (computed using the scanpy.tl.score_genes function57) for each annotated cell type identified58. These gene-expression scores were then mapped onto the corresponding cell types in the Allen Institute’s annotated MERFISH spatial transcriptomics dataset58. Spatial maps were generated by colouring each cell type according to its Asns expression using the ggplot2 package59 in R (v3.4.4).

The single-cell genomics tool Seurat (v.4)60 was used for scRNA-seq data analysis. We followed the workflow described in the Seurat guided analysis on datasets normalized with SCTransform to normalize gene-expression levels. Principal component analysis was performed using the top 30 principal components. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analysis was performed with the top 30 dimensions. Clusters were identified with the functions FindNeighbors (dims = 1:30) and FindClusters (resolution = 0.8). A resolution of 0.8 was selected for the downstream analysis because clusters were clearly separated and matched visual inspection. Default parameters were used unless noted. For identified clusters, cluster marker analysis confirmed that they were transcriptionally distinct, with their unique marker genes. Major cell types and five different neuronal cell populations were annotated on the basis of the expression of unique markers as described in the original publication23, including astrocytes (Gfap, Aldh1l1 and Aqp4), endothelial cells (Ly6c1), oligodendrocytes (Mobp, Mog and Plp1), perivascular macrophages (Mrc1 and Lyve1), mural cells (Acta2), microglia (Tmem119, P2ry12 and Cx3cr1), ependymoma cells (Foxj1) and neuronal subpopulations N-Tbr1, N-Grp, N-Lhx6, N-Synpr and N-Spink8.

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v.10).

#1068. Nobel Prize in literature: How the winner is secretly chosen

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Can Xue is considered one of this year's favorites; Margaret Atwood and Salman Rushdie are other strong contenders. But what goes on behind the scenes in the selection of the Nobel Prize in literature?

On Thursday, October 8, at around 1:00 p.m. CEST, all eyes in the literary world will be set on Stockholm.

That's when the new permanent secretary of the Swedish Academy, Ingrid Carlberg, will hold a press conference to announce the winner of the 2026 Nobel Prize in literature, marking the end of a lengthy selection process that officially began a year earlier.

The Swedish Academy is the body responsible for awarding the prize. Its 18 members consist of writers, professors and intellectuals.

In September of the previous year, the Academy's Nobel Committee sends forms to hundreds of individuals and organizations eligible to submit nominations for the Nobel Prize in literature.

According to the Nobel Prize website, these include professors of literature and linguistics at universities and colleges, previous Nobel laureates in literature and the heads of writers' associations that represent the literary community in their respective countries. Anyone from one of these groups who has not received a form may also submit a nomination.

"The list can reach 200 names per year of nominated authors," explains Gisele Sapiro, research director at France's National Centre for Scientific Research. She studies how the Nobel Prize, as a global institution, influences the international circulation of literature, power dynamics in the literary world and translation markets.

"The committee can also add names itself, such as authors who were nominated in previous years," she says.

In the spring, the Nobel Committee then selects five names through a multistage process and presents them to the Academy. Over the summer, Academy members read the nominees' works and discuss them. The Academy always announces the decision in early October.

As for the criteria the Academy uses to make its decision, it prefers to keep them to itself.

The public doesn't learn which nominees missed out until 50 years later — that's how long the Academy waits before releasing the list.

Sometimes decades pass between an author's first nomination and actually receiving the prize. Danish writer Johannes V. Jensen, who won the Nobel Prize in literature in 1944, waited 40 years. Doris Lessing received the award in 2007 after 35 years, while Thomas Mann, who won in 1929, had to wait 27 years.

In his will, Alfred Nobel, the prize's founder, stipulated that it should be awarded to the person who had produced "the most outstanding work in an idealistic direction." That leaves plenty of room for interpretation.

According to Gisele Sapiro, the Academy aims to honor ambitious, influential literature that has, for example, given rise to a new literary movement. "At the same time, the work has to be universal, being able to transcend its own culture," she says.

Translations can be one indication of this. "I noted in my research, however, that all the laureates since 1990 had been translated into English, French and German — except for Abdulrazak Gurnah [the Tanzanian-British 2021 Nobel laureate], who had not been translated into German before winning — meaning that I think these three languages are the most important," Sapiro explains.

Sapiro says it is also a major advantage if nominees have already won other major literary awards, such as the International Man Booker Prize, the Cervantes Prize or the Büchner Prize.

Politics can also influence the Swedish Academy's decisions.

On several occasions, the prize has gone to writers who openly opposed the regimes in their home countries, such as Belarusian writer Svetlana Alexievich in 2015. She is an outspoken critic of authoritarian President Alexander Lukashenko.

Genre, on the other hand, does not matter. In the past, laureates have been honored not only for novels but also for poetry and plays. In 2016, singer-songwriter Bob Dylan received the prize for his song lyrics.

So far, only 33 of the 122 laureates have come from outside Europe. There is also a clear gender imbalance: only in the past eight years have men and women alternated as winners, significantly increasing women's share of the total number of laureates to 14.9%.

#1065. Why are some football shin pads so small?

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Liverpool striker Lewis Koumas (second from left) sported low socks and small shinpads on his full Premier League debut at Anfield

Liverpool's Lewis Koumas made his full Premier League debut against Manchester City on Sunday.

While doing so, he sported some very low socks over what appeared to be a very small shin pad.

Last season, images of Burnley's Marcus Edwards went viral after the 27-year-old was seen wearing barely visible shin pads during a Premier League match against West Ham.

Two years ago, a picture of teenager Harry Howell celebrating with James Milner while they were Brighton team-mates also gained attention after fans noticed the visible difference in the size of their shin pads.

Howell's 'micro' pair were an obvious contrast to Milner's far more traditional-sized guards.

So what is behind the trend of extra-small shin guards, and why are they used instead of regular-sized pairs?

The simple answer is that players can choose what size shin pads they want to wear.

The International Football Association Board (Ifab), responsible for the laws of the game, say that shin pads are a mandatory piece of equipment players must wear while playing.

Be of an appropriate size to provide reasonable protection and be covered by the socks

And players are responsible for the size and suitability of their shin pads

The law does not define what an "appropriate" shin pad size is, but leave that decision to each individual player.

Shin pads provide vital protection for players to prevent serious injuries.

There has been a rising trend in younger footballers, as well as some big stars, wearing small shin pads during matches.

The reason for this could be for more comfort and to allow players to move around with greater ease.

Referees are only required to check that some form of shin guards are being worn, Ifab told BBC Sport.

Ifab said players - or the parents or guardians of youth players - must take responsibility for their own safety on the pitch.

While shin pad size is ultimately an individuals choice, any decisions around player safety would also be made in consultation with their club and medical staff.

Marcus Edwards last year was seen wearing very small shin pads for Burnley in the Premier League

Grassroots football teams have started to ban small shin pads for safety reasons.

There are concerns that small shin pads do not cover the shins properly, which can lead to an increased risk of injury - especially at non-elite level.

In August 2024, a football club banned small shin pads after a youth player suffered a double leg break.

Penistone Church player Alfie Collins broke his tibia and fibula in a tackle during a game. The teenager's shin pads, which were no bigger than a credit card, were similar to a size recently worn by some stars of the professional game.

Shin pads "play an important role in injury prevention as they are intended to protect the shin from injury", the Football Association said.

"'Micro' or 'mini' shin pads may therefore increase the risk of injury due to the smaller amount of shin covered compared to larger sized shin pads," they added.
