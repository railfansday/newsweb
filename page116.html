#1356. What to watch in UEFA Champions League: PSG vs. Barcelona could be a classic, Serie A need to recover, more

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

The mad rush to the midpoint of the Champions League league phase continues apace. In the space of four weeks we will have gotten through three rounds of the competition, hitting the halfway mark and making up time after that elongated international break. It is a pivotal moment then in the Champions League's early exchanges -- it's up to you to decide just how important the race for the top eight is in the overall direction of the competition -- and this week certainly brings the big guns. Indeed, it is not hard to argue that Tuesday and Wednesday pit the four favorites for this competition against each other. On Wednesday it is the turn of the last two teams eliminated by champions Paris Saint-Germain to face off as Arsenal travel to Bayern Munich. Before that, however, it's the turn of the holders against the team that might be the best placed to steal their continental crown. PSG vs. Barcelona. This is going to be a barn burner, right?

First things first, we really need these two to face off when the stakes are high. In each of the last two seasons it has seemed European football has been locked on a collision course for a Barcelona-Paris Saint-Germain dust-up in the Champions League final. A meeting between two injury-addled versions of these teams was not going to satiate our desires.

What so many wanted was a chance for the Parisians to bury the ghost of La Remontada, an opportunity for the Spanish champions to avenge themselves on an opponent that keeps snatching up their star players. This would be a footballing feast for the ages because deep down, no one wants to see the best attack vs. the best defense. They want the best attack vs. the second-best defense. It wouldn't hurt if at least one of those elite frontlines came with a rearguard that verges on Keystone Cops-ian (here's looking at you, Hansi Flick's high line).

PSG have kept up their end of the bargain, Barcelona less so. Red cards cost them in last season's quarterfinal against Atletico Madrid; a historic goalkeeping display from Inter's Yan Sommer did it the year before. There wasn't much improvement needed to get them to the big games, but boy, have they improved. Shaving 10% off their expected goals allowed per game in LaLiga is not to be sniffed at, and Rodri ranking in the 95th percentile for interceptions per 90 across Europe's top five leagues (min. 500 minutes played) is all the more impressive given that Barcelona always have the ball.

Really, though, the growth has somehow been in the attack. Barcelona were never exactly shy about marching up the pitch beforehand, but they have gone from averaging 2.5 goals last season to over four this, chucking on three extra shots per game and averaging a ludicrous 3.3 non-penalty expected goals per 90. It's mid-October and Flick's side already has more xG for the season than Getafe (who got 51 points) managed in all of 2025-26.

If Lamine Yamal has a rival for the Ballon d'Or after this one, it's only because Raphinha, now repurposed as a center forward, has taken the leap for about the third time in the last five years. The supporting cast is staggeringly impressive. Anthony Gordon, Karim Adeyemi and Gabriel Jesus all look shrewd pickups, while Fermin Lopez keeps taking good shots. About the only thing you can say to diminish this team in its 11-game winning run is that every attacker would look better if Pedri was the one giving them the ball.

Their opponent isn't putting up anywhere the same kind of numbers, but the PSG team sheet that Barcelona see could be quite different to that which drops before Ligue 1 matches. Away to Strasbourg on Sunday (a top-half side in each of the last two seasons), Luis Enrique rolled out an XI that included only two starters from the Champions League final, one a somewhat out-of-form Khvicha Kvaratskhelia. When an equalizer was not forthcoming, on came Nuno Mendes, Vitinha, Desire Doue, Achraf Hakimi and Ousmane Dembele. A 3-1 win duly followed. Similar rotation was tried with less success against Monaco and Lille. Even so, it seems unlikely that Luis Enrique will ever need to adopt such extreme measures as playing his best XI to win Ligue 1. This extreme rotation has instilled some competition in the PSG squad; both Warren Zaire-Emery and Magnes Akliouche will hope to retain their starting spots from the side that blitzed Manchester City in the first half at the Etihad last week. There is something in that 4-2 win that overstates the excellence of the holders, whose three first-half goals came from shots worth a combined 0.34 xG, but it was clear that when a higher gear was needed, the champions could find it. They might well need to be there more consistently if beating Barcelona matters to them. Whether it does is not entirely clear given that this team has approached the league phase with as much vim as it has Ligue 1. Luis Enrique does not have quite as much to fear about two additional ultracompetitive games down the stretch as Arsenal boss Mikel Arteta or Liverpool's Andoni Iraola might. And so hopes that this is the game to light up autumn's gloom might need tempering somewhat. Still, maybe this won't be the last we see of these two in this season's Champions League.

Date: Tuesday, Oct. 20 | Time: 3 p.m. ETLocation: Parc des Princes, ParisLive stream: Paramount+Odds: PSG +138; Draw +333; Barcelona +138

Two rounds played and it is not looking good for Serie A's finest. Nine teams, including three each from England and Spain, are six points from six. The quartet from Italy as a whole have just four to their name, the bulk of them earned by Como in their impressive opening win over RB Leipzig. Last week was an unmitigated disaster, all four ending in defeat to prompt soul-searching in a nation that has had to do quite a lot of that in recent years.

And perhaps there was not that much to be worried about. The absolute pulverizing Napoli got at the hands of Arsenal in week one is the only game where there has been a clear gulf in class in either direction. Otherwise, Roma need to put in some shifts on their set-piece defense, but they were 3-1 up and the better team against Real Madrid until a disastrous last ten minutes. Como too had the better of their game against Feyenoord and Napoli had the better of the xG against Villarreal, albeit they were chasing for three-quarters of the game. Inter too can point to a better xG against Club Brugge. Certainly none of this is good -- a regular finalist should be beating anything Belgium throws at it -- but there is no reason why this early crisis for Serie A can't still end with all four of its teams in the top 24.

If that is going to happen, this week's games seem quite critical, aside from Como's meeting with Manchester United on Wednesday. Shakhtar Donetsk probably occupy a similar berth to Club Brugge in a Champions League power ranking, a punchy side that Inter should cruise past at home. Slovan Bratislava are even more likely to be on the losing end against Roma, but the pressure is still on Gian Piero Gasperini's impressive side.

Blow that game, though, and it's Manchester United, Paris Saint-Germain (both away) and Sporting at home before Christmas. Roma should be too good for this to happen to them, but you could envisage them getting into a hole like the one Athletic Club and Villarreal found themselves in last season, when decent performances did not bring decent results and it became too difficult for them to rouse themselves after the turn. Napoli could find themselves in a similar hole after the visit of Bodo/Glimt, owners of a 12-1-3 record in home Champions League matches and 3-3-9 on the road.

Just as last week was a conflagration of the worst possible outcomes for the Italians, there is no reason why they shouldn't come out from these games the toast of the week. After all, every team is favored by the bookmakers in its game. They do, however, need to play up to expectations or else this season could get away from them.

Though the injury is "not as bad as expected," Manchester City suffered a cruel blow in Saturday's 3-2 win over Ipswich Town, losing Antoine Semenyo to a knee injury that Enzo Maresca has confirmed should sideline him for eight weeks. City have rarely played without their No. 42. Indeed, no player has registered as many competitive minutes for the club as Semenyo since his arrival on Jan. 9. In the 2,727 minutes since, he has 13 goals, a tally bettered only by Erling Haaland, and six assists. Only Rayan Cherki and Jeremy Doku beat him on that measure. It is something of a case of one in, one out for City, who had Doku rejoin the squad after the international break. Semenyo will certainly be missed for what he can offer in front of goal, even if he had been struggling to find chances lately with the same frequency that he did in the games immediately after he left Bournemouth.

City have historically carried a smaller squad than most teams in their position, reflecting a preference of Pep Guardiola that his successor Maresca was hardly going to immediately break with. Two obvious options appear, but questions linger over both. Iliman Ndiaye has not particularly kicked on since his move from Everton, and while the sample size is only four games, a combined 0.37 non-penalty xG plus expected assists (xA) per 90 minutes as a starter is not really up to the level expected of a City forward. Phil Foden's underlying metrics as both a starter and off the bench are notably more impressive, aided by a goal against Ipswich at the weekend that was his first in 2026. The issue there, though, is why Foden was so resoundingly shunted down the pecking order when he was still averaging more than 0.5 npxG+xA per 90, starting just 15 games in all competitions since Semenyo's arrival. The most convincing explanation appears to be that while the 26-year-old was putting up pretty good numbers in terms of the last action -- 89th percentile in npxG compared to positional peers across Europe's top five leagues in the last 365 days and 85th in xA -- he wasn't providing that other quality that Doku (dribbling), Semenyo (an aerial outlet) or Cherki (elite creation) have in abundance. Nor does he know what his new coach wants quite like Enzo Fernandez, who occupies one of the ultra-attacking central midfield roles that might otherwise have been Foden's. Ultimately, he has failed to kick on since his player-of-the-year campaign in 2023-24. Now he has the chance to do so.

#1352. Backlash over data centers tests Europe's AI ambitions

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Massive data centers needed to run AI are triggering protests across Europe. Their operators counter that they boost digital sovereignty and energy independence.

Like coal-fired power plants and wind turbines, few people actually want a data center in their backyard. But they do want to use their services. As a global debate rages about the potential dangers of artificial intelligence, there are also many local discussions about where exactly the data centers needed to train and run AI should be built.

This is currently a major issue in Europe, where many governments and companies have decided that they would prefer to store data in Europe rather than in the United States, which is the global leader in computing. But more digital sovereignty in Europe will mean more consumption of the continent's resources.

According to the German Ministry for Economic Affairs, for every kilowatt-hour of electricity consumed, an average of between 0.4 and 4.4 liters (0.8 and 9.3 pints) of water is used to cool data centers, with the water either evaporating or being diverted back to tanks in a warmed state.

The summer that just ended was the hottest on record in Europe — in some areas, water use was restricted for residents, agriculture and even nuclear power plants. The summers are expected to get warmer, so many are wondering about the potential water consumption of data centers in the future.

They are also worried about the high electricity consumption of the centers. Fingrid, Finland's national grid operator, recently forecast that electricity consumption would rise by almost 40% if all projects with signed connection agreements were implemented in full.

This week, the European Commission announced a new scheme for data centers to increase transparency on their energy and water use. Those with a capacity of more than 500 kW will receive an efficiency grade that will also take into account their contribution to the grid, such as the reuse of waste heat. "Tripling our data center capacity cannot mean tripling the pressure on our grids, our water and our energy bills," said  Teresa Ribera, the Executive Vice-President of the European Commission for a Clean, Just and Competitive Transition.

Europe's most powerful data centers have primarily been built near the major cities of Frankfurt, London, Amsterdam, Paris and Dublin (FLAP-D) because of high connectivity, proximity to users and businesses, and well-established ecosystems with specialized engineers and secure supply chains.

However, according to a report by the investment group CBRE, the situation is changing as these markets face power and grid infrastructure constraints. New data centers are being built in the Dutch city of Groningen, Barcelona in Spain or the Belgian capital Brussels, as well as in more rural areas.

And as this happens, there are more and more protests taking place, both on and offline. Earlier this month, hundreds turned up for a protest in front of tech giant Google's offices in the Austrian city of Linz, demonstrating against its already advanced plans to build a data center in the small town of Kronstorf.

Earlier this month, bestselling Irish author Sally Rooney successfully led a campaign to overturn planning permission for a proposed data center in the village of Killala, arguing that it would breach national climate policy.

In central France, meanwhile, several elected officials in the village of Etrechet have resigned over Google's plans to build a massive data center, which, at full capacity, would require between 880 megawatts and 1.1 gigawatts of electricity or the equivalent of the total output of a nuclear power plant. Etrechet mayor Florence Laurent told the French daily Le Monde that she was resigning out of a "sense of powerlessness, since the village has no decision-making power" regarding the project.

Some operators of data centers argue that the facilities can be built in such a way that they save energy. A large data center currently being built in Marseille will reportedly use a cooling system that requires 30% less power and consumes no water.

Other data centers, in Scandinavia, for example, use waste heat from the servers and route it into district heating networks via heat exchangers and pumps.

This spring, the German energy company E.ON published the results of a survey that found nearly three-quarters of people would be more likely to welcome data centers near residential areas if they fed their waste heat into the heating network.

Data center operators also argue that the facilities can serve the public by connecting the batteries that usually provide backup power to local grids so that there is uninterrupted service in the event of power outages or at times of peak demand.

For proponents of building more data centers in Europe, the facilities not only contribute to the continent's digital sovereignty but also help to strengthen energy independence.

#1346. Cesc Fabregas eyes Champions League statement vs. Manchester United, with surprise support from Lionel Messi

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

If there is one match that perfectly describes the evolution of soccer, it is the one that will take place on Wednesday between Como and Manchester United at the Stadio Giuseppe Sinigaglia. On one side, one of the most legendary football clubs in the world, enduring a prolonged period of decline. On the other, a small Italian club building one of the most exciting projects in modern football thanks to financial investments, ideas and young players. On paper, you would expect Manchester United to win, but in reality, it's the opposite and Como are the favorites.

Como coach Cesc Fabregas described the match against Manchester United as "an historical night for the city," and he's right. When the Champions League draw took place in August, Como fans circled a few dates on their calendars. The first was the first-ever Champions League match at their home stadium, an incredible night of soccer that ended in a 4-1 win over RB Leipzig. Then they set their sights on the second home game, against Manchester United. A match that reflects Como's rise in modern soccer, driven by the work of the coach and the team, as well as the ambitious project led by club president Mirwan Suwarso and backed by one of the wealthiest ownership groups in the game.

Date: Wednesday, Oct. 21 | Time: 12:45 p.m. ETLocation: Stadio Sinigaglia -- ComoLive stream: Paramount+Odds: Como +120; Draw +271; Manchester United +230

Speaking ahead of the game, Fabregas joked about his approach to the match: "Tomorrow, we'll all line up on the edge of our own penalty area and park the bus. Joking aside, we'll try to play our own football. We always ask ourselves: how do you want to lose? By playing our own football and respecting our opponents. It's a historic day for our city, a special moment that we're experiencing during these months. We need to adapt well to the challenge, playing with composure and ambition. I don't think we need to dwell on United's history. Let's enjoy the challenge, try to win, and make the most of this historic day."

He added, "Manchester United are like Inter, Milan or Juventus when we play them. I focus on what the opposition do and how we can hurt them. If I keep telling the players to be careful of Manchester United, they'll go onto the pitch feeling afraid. A coach must always give his team confidence, and I feel strong with my players. We can win if we do things the way they should be done. If we think our opponents are superior to us, it will be much harder."

Fabregas will also have the support of his friend Lionel Messi, who recently posted a picture on social media of his son Ciro wearing a Como jersey. Fabregas revealed that he was behind the gift to the Argentine superstar, who also shared the national team dressing room with Nico Paz, Como's rising star.

Ciro Messi wearing Nico Paz's Como jersey 💙 pic.twitter.com/em3rhLEdVQ

There is also a mystery about Messi and the Italian club, as former Como owner Enrico Preziosi has claimed multiple times that he had the chance to sign Messi. He was just 13 at the time, but Preziosi was advised against signing him because of concerns about his height. The Argentine youngster was also dealing with growth-related issues and needed financial support to cover the cost of his treatment during adolescence. Preziosi told SportItalia in a TV interview, "I missed out on Messi back when I was at Como. They showed me a video of this young kid playing on field, with the ball glued to his feet. I met his father, who was asking for $50,000. I was convinced we should go ahead with the deal. Then I left on a trip to Hong Kong, and when I returned, I asked Carmine Gentile and Antonino Imborgia about the boy. Their response was that someone like him would never be able to play football because he was too short. The following year, he went to Barcelona at the age of 16, and they did an excellent job developing him. It was a real shame, especially because we could have signed him for very little." However, there are questions about the former Como owner's account. Messi was already at Barcelona by the age of 15, not 16 as the interview suggested, and both Gentile and Imborgia have denied the story, saying they never had the chance to sign him. Gentile told a local paper, "I never saw Messi at Como, nor did I ever say he was too small. This myth resurfaces every twenty years, but it's false. I was only in charge of the first team, not youth trials. Even if he had come here at the age of 12, no 12-year-old is 1.80 metres tall. So I can confirm that Messi never played for Como." We may never know the full truth about whether Como really had the chance to sign Messi, or whether the story is destined to remain a mystery. What is certain, however, is that Fabregas now has his sights firmly set on Manchester United and a historic night that could send a powerful message to the world, not just for this season, but for the years to come: Como are no longer just an ambitious project. #1341. What would Francis of Assisi make of his fame today?

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

The Catholic saint renounced wealth and fame to become a mendicant friar. Yet throughout history, his teachings and life have inspired popes, Dante and even Mussolini.

One could safely say that Francis of Assisi's life — and afterlife — is defined by paradox.

He embraced poverty, yet lies entombed today in a grand basilica in his native Assisi, Italy, with frescoes by renowned medieval artists.

He advocated meekness, yet hundreds of thousands venerated his remains when they went on public display earlier this year to commemorate 800 years since his death on October 3, 1226.

So, why has this Catholic saint inspired papal encyclicals, ecological activism, global pet‑blessing rituals and even the propaganda of former Italian fascist leader Benito Mussolini? And what might Francis make of this fame that he'd shunned while alive?

Born in Assisi, a hill town in central Italy, in 1181 or 1182, he was first named Giovanni di Pietro di Bernardone. His father, a cloth merchant with trade links to France, renamed him Francesco.

Early Franciscan hagiographers, that is medieval writers who recorded his life and shaped how later generations understood him, describe him as a lively, sociable young man who enjoyed singing, parties and the privileges of wealth.

However, he didn't want to join his father's trade; he dreamed of becoming a knight.

In 1202, Francis joined Assisi's forces in a battle against neighboring Perugia. He was captured, spent a year in prison and returned home shaken and ill. This, and a series of subsequent events, precipitated a slow shift in his outlook.

One occurred at the small, ruined Church of San Damiano outside Assisi, where Francis believed he heard Jesus Christ calling on him to "rebuild my church." Taking it literally, he began repairing local chapels. When he ran out of money, he sold cloth from his father's shop, which enraged the latter and led to a dramatic episode in Assisi's town square.

Before the bishop and gathered townspeople, Francis stripped off his fine clothes, renouncing his father, his inheritance and all material comforts. He committed himself to a life of poverty, humility, care for the poor and sick and a deep kinship with nature.

As a wandering preacher, he drew followers and eventually founded the Order of Friars Minor, known today as the Franciscans.

Widely considered the first saint to have miraculously received the stigmata — physical wounds resembling those on the crucified body of Jesus Christ — Francis was canonized by Pope Gregory IX on July 16, 1228. This unusually swift recognition amplified his fame beyond Assisi.

In the following centuries, Francis became one of the most frequently depicted saints in Western art, inspiring works from Dante's "Divine Comedy" to films, children's books and even a 1980 Marvel comic.

"Most saints are seen as unapproachable or unrelatable, but Francis' story of lifelong conversion and the desire to be in a positive relationship with others, God and the rest of creation speaks to people across time and cultures," theologian and former Franciscan friar Daniel P. Horan tells DW.

According to cultural historian Amanda Minervini, this broad appeal is also what made Francis so adaptable across eras.

"In Francis, there seems to be something for everyone: the pacifist, the ascetic, the mason, the environmentalist, the minimalist, the lover of animals also loved by animals, the diplomat, the mystic, the radical, the performer, and even the model of a different kind of masculinity," they tell DW.

Minervini points out that his image has been reconstructed throughout history. Their research highlights one particularly jarring example: Francis' political appropriation by Benito Mussolini's fascist regime.

In their recent book, "St. Francis and Mussolini: Fascist Appropriations of a Beloved Italian Saint" (2026), they explain how fascist rhetoric manipulated Francis' stigmata, reframing them as symbols of heroic suffering and sacrifice. "The wounds of the saint were thus invoked to glorify sacrifice and inspire Italian youth to go to war."

Noting how Mussolini chose to be symbolically baptized in 1927 to strengthen his alliance with the Vatican, they add, "St. Francis offered a particularly useful means of conferring religious legitimacy upon political power."

Minervini's research shows that Francis' pacifism, "likely because he had known what war meant," was deliberately played down. Their book reproduces a confidential note addressed to military chaplains in 1929, which expressed concern that they might preach ideals of peace to the troops.

"This document provides clear evidence that peace was an aspect of Francesco's legacy that fascist groups sought to suppress rather than promote."

Furthermore, after Italy — a former Axis power along with Nazi Germany — switched sides and joined the Allies during World War II, the latter were often willing to condone or overlook Italy's crimes rather than investigate its fascist past.

"In this climate, many fascists remained in place, and the politically compromised image of Francis was quietly set aside," explains Minervini.

Francis has, however, remained associated with peace-making.

In 1219, during the Fifth Crusade, he traveled with the crusading army to Damietta, a port city in northern Egypt and obtained permission to cross into the Muslim camp and meet personally with the sultan, Malik al-Kamil. He aimed to convert the sultan and end the bloody conflict.

Describing his action as "an extraordinarily audacious thing to do," Minervini says the encounter did not produce the conversion or political breakthrough Francis may have hoped for, but his unscathed return ensured early sources took notice of him.

Horan, the former Franciscan friar, adds that despite Christians viewing Muslims as the enemy back then, St. Francis "would not go along with that dehumanizing perspective" and instead celebrated "the shared humanity and brotherhood he recognized in the Sultan."

But he is most widely known as an animal lover and an early figure of environmental consciousness.

"He was ahead of his time both theologically and in terms of modern natural sciences, recognizing the complexity of the cosmos and the fact that human beings are also creatures in relationship with the world around us," says Horan, author of "Francis of Assisi and the Future of Faith" (2012).

In 1979, Pope John Paul II declared Francis the patron saint of ecology, while the Argentinian Jorge Mario Bergoglio chose the saint as his papal namesake when he became Pope Francis in 2013.

This pope would later publish "Laudato Si'" in 2015 — based on the saint's 1225 hymn "Canticle of the Creatures," in which he addresses natural elements as "Brother Sun," "Sister Moon" and "Sister Mother Earth."

The pope's encyclical connected environmental destruction directly to human suffering, besides critiquing consumerism and the "throwaway culture," and urging humanity to undergo an "ecological conversion."

"I also believe that in a time of deep polarization and economic inequality that St Francis models another way of being in the world, one that prioritizes relationship and community over individual gain and power," says Horan.

"It's always tricky to try and guess what St. Francis, who died 800 years ago this October, would think about anything today. Our world is so unlike his own. But we do know that in his own time he was very uncomfortable with the positive reception he and his brother friars were receiving across Europe," says Horan.

Minervini suggests that he might have been more comfortable with representations of himself as a pacifist saint.

"I suspect he would be profoundly troubled, perhaps even furious, as it is known he could get upset, by the condition of our late-capitalist, technocratic world and by the ways in which we continue to understand ourselves as separate from, and superior to, the other creatures."
