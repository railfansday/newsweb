#1036. Emerging economies are embracing solar power and skipping fossil fuels much faster than Europe or the US

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Renewable electricity is cheaper than ever. While the West got rich on coal and oil, some of the world's poorest countries are banking on solar, wind and water instead.

As emerging economies and global living standards rise, is the world in for another fossil fuel boom? The data says not. Countries from Southeast Asia to Sub-Saharan Africa aren't investing in fossil fuels as the West did; they're investing in electricity powered by renewables.

When the US and Europe industrialized in the 19th and 20th centuries, they did so using fossil energy. From coal ovens, gas heating and combustion-engine cars to electricity produced in coal and gas power plants, entire societies relied on polluting fuels.

Yet while burning fossil fuels allowed industrialized countries to boom, it also caused the climate crisis that is linked to extreme weather events around the world today.

Many of the same countries that became economically powerful off the back of fossil fuels are now trying to transition to renewables, but centuries of legacy infrastructure hold them back.

Because a gas boiler won't run on wind power and a diesel truck cannot be solar-powered, societies looking to shift to green energy need to use more electricity and increase their share of renewables.

That is where many middle- and lower-income countries have an advantage. With less existing energy infrastructure built around fossil fuels, they are better placed for a faster rollout of renewable electricity sources.

In many instances, solar power in particular is now cheaper to produce than fossil electricity. And unlike large coal or gas plants, which take years to construct and millions in investments, solar and wind farms can be built quickly and with much less upfront cost.

At the same time, according to an analysis by renewables think tank Ember, electronic devices like refrigerators, electric stoves or air conditioners have all become much cheaper in the last decades, making electricity a more affordable option as an energy source for cooking, heating and more.

While electric cars are still a luxury item in most places, their prices are coming down too. And a UN study found that in India, Indonesia and Vietnam electric scooters and motorbikes already cost the same as petrol models. Yet a lot less to fuel.

All this means that renewable electricity is quickly becoming the best choice for the wallet — not just for the climate. And countries are taking note.

"We're seeing a leapfrog take place right across the emerging markets,” said Kingsmill Bond, energy strategist at Ember. "The whole of Asia is electrifying quickly, and the whole of Europe and the United States is stagnant."

In 2023, only around a quarter of global electricity was generated by renewables. But that figure is rising fast, and almost all new capacity added these days is based on solar or wind power.

Lower-income countries already use less fossil fuels and more renewables than higher-income ones.

And large middle-income economies are outpacing richer ones as well. In 2023, solar power alone made up at least six percent and ten percent of electricity in India and Vietnam, respectively. The global average was five percent.

This boom has meant middle- and lower-income countries are electrifying fast, consuming less energy directly from fossil fuels than richer countries. Vietnam, for instance, is already using more electricity than the US, Europe and even China, and India is following the same path.

But the potential for energy leapfrogging might be particularly high in low-income countries, especially across Sub-Saharan Africa.

Countries such as Kenya, Ethiopia and Namibia are rapidly expanding their energy infrastructure to meet the demands of a growing, young population. But rural areas in many countries are not connected to electricity grids.

Joel Nana, project manager at South African NGO Sustainable Energy Africa says that "even when there is access to power, it's often not reliable."

Until a few years ago, he says, people defaulted to diesel generators to meet their energy needs. That came at a cost because importing fuel is particularly expensive in many African countries, meaning energy crises and oil price fluctuations hit much harder.

These factors make solar power a solution for both energy access and independence. And it has become so cheap that Nana says it's a "no-brainer."

"Some businesses can make back their investment in just two years, so why would you want to deploy anything else?” he said. "Solar has rapidly become the de facto default technology that everyone falls back on.”

And once it is set up, it is very effective. Sub-Saharan Africa is extremely well-suited to solar power. It gets more sun than almost anywhere in the world, and has enough space for solar farms to cover electricity demand hundreds of times over.

"If you add solar plus batteries, you can get cheap electricity about 90 percent of the time from the sun alone”, said Kingsmill Bond.

Countries like Namibia, South Africa, Kenya and Senegal have expanded their solar capacity massively in the past few years.

In a 2026 report for African Tech Futures Lab and Ember, Nana analyzed import data from China and estimated that around 100,000 solar panels were installed across Africa every day in 2026, up 45 percent from just the previous year.

Similar trends are emerging all across the world. In Pakistan, for example, people have been buying solar panels from China en masse in the past few years to avoid steep electricity grid prices and frequent power outages.

Aleksi Lumijärvi, director for knowledge, policy and finance at the International Renewable Energy Association says these decisions can have a major impact on the global economy in the coming decades.

"Industrial companies will find that it will be cheaper to move production to, for example, somewhere in Sub-Saharan Africa, because they have this cheap, clean solar power plus batteries available.”

Kingsmill Bond says where electricity is cheap and abundant, wealth will follow.

"You're going to have faster growth in the emerging markets, you will have fewer financial crises. It will make a material difference to people's quality of lives.”

Nana says the next step for African countries is to establish policies that ensure everyone benefits from the solar boom.

"We are heading towards the most decentralized grid in the whole world within the next five years,” he told DW. His mantra for governments, he says, is: "This is happening, whether you like it or not.” He expects a wave of new energy regulations, many of which are now in the final stages of negotiation, to come into effect across Africa in the coming months and years.

"Countries are realizing it's in their interest to integrate this technology into national strategies, to share the benefits and to make use of this competitive advantage."

“Harry Kane is now Bayern’s seventh-highest scorer of all time, as well as being Tottenham’s highest scorer. The man he overtook at the top of that list, Jimmy Greaves, is also Chelsea’s seventh-highest scorer of all time. Do any other players feature on two (or more) clubs’ all-time top-10 scoring charts?” asks Owen Powell.

A trawl through the Knowledge archive reminded us that we have previously looked at players who are the record goalscorer for more than one club. This question is slightly different, as we’re focusing on goalscorers who are in the all-time top 10 for the most different clubs. There are dozens of players who appear for two clubs, including Sergio Agüero (Atlético Madrid and Manchester City), Kenny Dalglish (Celtic and Liverpool), Jimmy Greaves (Chelsea and Tottenham), Lionel Messi (Barcelona and Inter Miami), Cristiano Ronaldo (Real Madrid and Al-Nassr) and Alan Shearer (Blackburn and Newcastle).

There’s one more worthy of mention. “Harry Johnson is all-time top scorer for both Sheffield United and Mansfield,” writes Darren White. “He played for the Blades between 1919 and 1931, scoring 201 league goals. At the age of 32, he left for Mansfield, scoring 104 goals for them over three seasons. He didn’t play for anyone else, so he has the possibly unique record (for someone with more than one club) of being top scorer for every team he represented.”

And so to those who are among the top 10 goalscorers for more than two clubs: “Romário (Vasco da Gama, Flamengo and PSV) and Delio Onnis (Monaco, Tours FC and Gimnasia) feature on at least three clubs’ all time top-10 scoring charts,” writes Dirk Maas. “Onnis also played for Toulon, but I could not find an official source to confirm that Onnis is in their top 10 as well.”

Elsewhere, Rhys Shakeshaft suggests Robbie Keane, who is among the top 10 goalscorers for three of his clubs (Tottenham, LA Galaxy, ATK), and André-Pierre Gignac (Marseille, Toulouse, Tigres UANL). And Tom Stuart was among those who nominated John Aldridge (Newport County, Oxford United, Tranmere Rovers). The striker is also the Republic of Ireland’s fourth-highest goalscorer.

As well as being Cameroon’s all-time leading goalscorer, Samuel Eto’o is potentially in the top 10 for four clubs: Mallorca, Barcelona, Anzhi Makhachkala and Antalyaspor. “I’m not 100% sure if I can trust the Antalyaspor record,” writes Rhys Shakeshaft, who sent in the answer, “but I’m happy with the other three.”

Historical goalscoring data isn’t always 100% reliable, but we think Sam Kerr is among the top 10 leading scorers for five teams: Perth Glory FC, Western New York Flash, Gotham FC, Chicago Red Stars and Chelsea.

Even five clubs wouldn’t be a record. “I’ve managed to find one male footballer who, amazingly, is in the top 10 for six different clubs!” writes Ben. “Morocco’s Abderrazak Hamdallah is the all-time top scorer for OC Safi (Morocco), second for El-Jaish (Qatar), fourth for Guangzhou R&F/Guangzhou City (China), fourth for Al-Nassr, seventh for Al-Ittihad and 10 for Al-Shabab (all Saudi Arabia). “The German national kit is not going to be made by Adidas any more, going to Nike after 70 years. Is this the longest kit supply partnership in history, and what’s the longest current run in club football?” asks Paul Savage.

Dirk Maas has unearthed a couple of club sponsorships that have lasted more than 60 years. “Adidas has been Bayern Munich’s kit sponsor since 1965,” writes Dirk, “but the first time the Adidas trefoil was visible on the shirt was 17 October 1973. We can’t find anything to beat the 70-year relationship between Germany and Adidas. That goes back to 1977 and includes some memorable kits – most of them for the right reasons.

In the Knowledge last week we looked at players who scored more own-goals than goals during their career. A number of you pointed out the omission of Des Walker, a magnificent defender for Nottingham Forest and England in particular, but one who wasn’t renowned for his goalscoring exploits.

Plant development is highly plastic, with growth and architecture continuously shaped by environmental conditions. Roots, for instance, adjust their development to local water, nutrient and soil conditions. Despite this plasticity, individual root cell types are generally viewed as largely homogeneous populations that progress along a single developmental trajectory1. Here we show that rather than progressing along a single developmental trajectory, most Arabidopsis root cell lineage trajectories exhibit a stereotyped bifid topology, as revealed by single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcript mapping. One branch is characterized by a transcriptional program that is shared across multiple cell types and enriched for genes associated with environmental responses, including abscisic acid (ABA) signalling. This state occurs reproducibly in a subset of cells across multiple cell types, independent of developmental lineage, thereby revealing an additional layer of root cell identity. It is conserved across Arabidopsis ecotypes and persists despite major perturbations in developmental identity. Moreover, it is reduced in ABA-deficient and ABA-insensitive mutants and becomes more prevalent under water-deficit conditions. Together, these findings reveal an additional layer of root cell identity that is superimposed on developmental lineage and its prevalence is modulated by environmental conditions. These results uncover a previously unrecognized source of environment-associated transcriptional heterogeneity shared across root cell types.

The root of Arabidopsis thaliana is a valuable model for studying cell differentiation in plants. Cell types are arranged in concentric layers around a central vascular bundle and arise from distinct stem cell populations maintained near the root tip in the meristem1. As stem cells divide, their descendants are displaced shootward and progressively differentiate, which creates developmental trajectories in which the spatial arrangement of cells recapitulates the temporal dynamics of differentiation (Fig. 1a).

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has refined the transcriptional signatures of Arabidopsis root cell types and enabled high-resolution reconstruction of these developmental trajectories2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9. Although these studies have identified transient cell states in individual lineages, they have largely analysed each lineage independently, which may overlook transcriptional states shared across multiple cell types irrespective of developmental identity. Whether such shared transcriptional states exist and how they contribute to root cell identity remain largely unexplored.

To characterize cell states during root development, here we generated a high-resolution scRNA-seq (10x Genomics) atlas of the A. thaliana Col-0 root tip (Fig. 1b). After filtering low-quality cells, doublets and genes significantly affected by protoplast isolation, batch integration generated a reference atlas comprising 87,056 cells from 9 replicates, with a total of 25,784 detected genes (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1).

We identified 27 transcriptionally distinct clusters and assigned their identities by combining differential expression with optimized whole-mount hybridization chain reaction (HCR)10 and tissue clearing11 to spatially map the expression of the 15 most specific marker genes (Fig. 1b–d). This process resolved all known cell types, including distinct root cap subpopulations (early, tip and lateral) (Fig. 1b–d). One cluster located between the root cap and epidermis could not be assigned directly by marker expression. However, on the basis of its position and developmental origin of these tissues, it was annotated as ‘epidermis–meristem’ (Fig. 1b).

Cells were grouped according to their developmental lineages (epidermis, root cap, cortex, endodermis, xylem pole pericycle (XPP), phloem pole pericycle (PPP), procambium, phloem and xylem), which confirmed that ontogenic programs remain the dominant determinant of root cell identity (Fig. 1b,e). Cells from each lineage were analysed independently to reconstruct developmental trajectories. A meristematic score, derived from genes enriched in the meristematic epidermis cluster, was calculated for each lineage, and the cell with the highest score was defined as the trajectory starting point (t = 0). Trajectory topology was inferred using partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA), and developmental progression was ordered by diffusion pseudotime from t = 0 (Fig. 1e–g).

The root cap lineage exhibited the most complex topology and recapitulated known developmental events. That is, the split of lateral and tip populations and their convergence with columella cells to form the sloughing border-like cells12 (Fig. 1g). These trajectories were supported by stage-specific marker genes13,14,15 (Extended Data Fig. 1a), which further validated the inferred developmental trajectories.

Although some lineages are anticipated to be composed of distinct cell subtypes, such as phloem and xylem, which are made of metaphloem and protophloem and metaxylem and protoxylem, respectively, and the epidermis, which subdivides into root hairs and non-root hairs, each is expected to follow a single developmental trajectory from undifferentiated to differentiated states. Notably, PAGA revealed that all cell types and subcell types instead exhibit two branches (branch 1 and branch 2) that diverge at an early pseudotime point (Fig. 1g). Only the epidermis contained cells preceding this bifurcation and a clearly identifiable meristematic population. In most other lineages, branching occurred directly from t = 0, which probably reflects the difficulty in capturing the rarer inner-layer meristematic cells during protoplasting.

To independently validate the inferred topology, whole-mount HCR was used to detect early and late markers in epidermis, endodermis and XPP (Extended Data Fig. 1b–e). In all three lineages, genes predicted to be expressed near t = 0 were detected closer to the meristem than predicted late markers. Consistent with this finding, lineage-specific meristem, elongation and maturation gene signatures obtained from a previous study8 progressed as expected along pseudotime. This result provides support for the robustness of our t = 0 assignment and inferred developmental ordering (Extended Data Fig. 2a).

Independent trajectory inference using Monocle also recovered the bifid topology (Extended Data Fig. 2b–d). Cell ordering in both branches was highly correlated with diffusion pseudotime and supported by RNA velocity analyses (Extended Data Fig. 2e–g).

Together, these data show that in addition to developmental progression, cells in most cell types exhibit two alternative transcriptional states. This result therefore reveals the existence of an additional, previously unrecognized, level of complexity in cell identity.

To identify transcriptional programs associated with cell type, developmental progression and branch identity, we performed high-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (hdWGCNA). Of the 6,649 genes assigned to 25 identified co-expression modules (Fig. 2a,b and Supplementary Table 3), most were enriched in specific cell types, groups of cell types or developmental stages. By contrast, modules 20 and 21 were specifically enriched in branch 1 across all lineages. This result demonstrates that there is a transcriptional program shared across multiple cell types independently of developmental identity.

Across all cell types, branch 1 cells consistently contained more detected genes and higher unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts than branch 2 cells (Fig. 2c), which indicated greater overall transcriptional activity. Modules 20 and 21 were also among the largest co-expression modules identified (320 and 615 genes, respectively; Fig. 2d), which showed that this transcriptional asymmetry involves a broad coordinated gene expression program. Although the PAGA analysis did not resolve two branches in non-hair cells, enrichment of the branch-specific modules and increased transcript abundance suggest that both populations are also present in this lineage.

Given the unexpected transcriptional differences between the two branches of the same cell type, we next sought to exclude possible experimental or analytical artefacts that might have created spurious trajectory bifurcations. Branch separation was not explained by cell cycle phase, experimental batches or genes affected by protoplast isolation (Extended Data Fig. 3a–e). Because roots grown on the surface of agar experience distinct agar-facing and air-facing microenvironments, we also profiled root tips from plants grown fully embedded in agar. The bifid trajectories remained clearly detectable across all cell types, which therefore ruled out these growth microenvironments as a contributing factor (Extended Data Fig. 3f–h).

Another possibility was that the lower UMI branch (branch 2) reflected damaged cells from which fewer transcripts were captured, despite these cells not exhibiting elevated mitochondrial or chloroplastic transcript content (Extended Data Fig. 3i–j). If so, lowly expressed genes would be preferentially detected in the higher UMI branch 1, whereas highly expressed genes would be shared between both branches. Using the XPP, we found that depletion of branch-1-specific genes in branch 2 substantially exceeded that expected from expression level alone (Extended Data Fig. 4a–c). Likewise, branch separation persisted after randomly downsampling branch 1 cells to match the UMI distribution of their branch 2 counterparts and reprocessing the dataset in three independent replicates (Extended Data Fig. 4d–g). Together, these analyses indicate that the lack of activation of the branch 1 transcriptional program in branch 2 cells cannot be explained solely by their lower transcript abundance. Finally, both branches and the branch-1-specific modules were also detected in a previously published Arabidopsis root tip single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) dataset16 (Extended Data Fig. 5). This result rules out technical differences between cells and nuclei, protoplast isolation or cell damage as explanations for the observed bifurcation.

To further demonstrate the robustness of the observed branches, we re-analysed two independent previously published Arabidopsis root tip scRNA-seq datasets7,8 (Extended Data Figs. 6 and 7). In all three cases (one snRNA-seq dataset and two scRNA-seq datasets), we recovered both branches in all cell types, together with the higher UMI and gene content of branch 1 cells and enrichment of modules 20 and 21. Indeed, branch assignment remained robust across a range of label-transfer parameters (Extended Data Figs. 5–7). The first scRNA-seq study7 did not initially identify the branches because many branch 2 cells were excluded owing to their lower UMI content. Conversely, the second study8, which integrates two additional published root scRNA-seq datasets3,4, clearly showed two branches in their uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) plots and noted their presence in the epidermis, cortex and endodermis. However, these findings were not explored further.

Some of our libraries were generated from an equimolar mix of cells from Col-0 and a second ecotype, which enabled experimental determination of cell multiplet rates while simultaneously generating root tip atlases for four additional Arabidopsis ecotypes (C24, Cvi-0, Ler and Ws-2; Methods, Extended Data Fig. 8a and Supplementary Table 2). Both branches were detected across all cell lineages in all ecotypes, with the same co-expression modules enriched in branch 1 (Extended Data Fig. 8b,c). Moreover, fewer than 4% of branch-1-specific module genes were differentially expressed in any ecotype, which demonstrated that the transcriptional program underlying the branching is highly conserved (Extended Data Fig. 8e,f). Among the ecotypes analysed, only Cvi-0 showed a significantly lower proportion of branch 1 cells (relative to branch 2) compared with Col-0 and was the ecotype with the greatest overall transcriptional divergence (Extended Data Fig. 8d–f)

A previous snRNA-seq study16 also generated a snATAC–seq atlas of the root tip. Using this chromatin accessibility data alone, we identified cells corresponding to each branch, with branch 1 cells displaying elevated fractions of reads in peaks and increased transcription start site enrichment, consistent with greater transcriptional activity (Extended Data Fig. 9a–c). Notably, this increase was specific to genes belonging to the branch-1-specific modules. Through this orthogonal method, this finding confirms the transcriptional asymmetry underlying the dichotomy identified by the scRNA-seq trajectories (Extended Data Fig. 9d,e).

To independently validate this transcriptional state in vivo, we measured the transcripts of five branch-1-specific genes by whole-mount HCR in intact root tips: AT2G41660 (also known as MIZ1), AT3G04010, AT4G26080 (also known as ABI1), AT5G56550 (also known as OXS3) and AT4G24060 (also known as VDOF1) (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 10a,b). All five genes showed heterogeneous expression in the cell types in which they are expressed, with some cells exhibiting strong transcript enrichment (positive) and others showing depletion (negative). Volumetric fluorescence quantification of segmented epidermal, cortex and endodermal cells confirmed up to 20-fold differences in fluorescence intensity between cells in the same tissue layer (Fig. 3a). Canonical cell-type markers remained detectable in negative cells, which indicated that reduced branch 1 marker detection was unlikely to reflect a general failure of HCR detection (Extended Data Fig. 10c). Although branch 2 lacks a distinct transcriptional program, analyses of epidermal gene expression identified AT3G01720 as largely complementary to the branch 1 marker AT5G56550, a pattern confirmed by HCR in vivo (Fig. 3b–h). Volumetric fluorescence quantification of segmented cells across three independent roots, together with Gaussian mixture modelling of both scRNA-seq scores and HCR fluorescence ratios, identified two discrete epidermal populations corresponding to branch 1 and branch 2 cells. This result provides direct in vivo evidence for the two transcriptionally distinct cell populations in the same tissue layer (Fig. 3e–h).

Together, these independent lines of evidence demonstrate that the bifid topology reflects a reproducible biological state characterized by the activation of a coordinated transcriptional program in a subset of cells across multiple root cell types.

To investigate the biological characteristics and potential regulators of this branch-specific transcriptional program, we analysed gene ontology (GO) term enrichment in the two branch-1-specific modules 20 and 21 (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Table 4). These modules were enriched for responses to environmental stimuli, including nutrients, water and oxygen. Terms linked to positive regulation of RNA synthesis were also enriched, consistent with the observed increase in transcriptional activity of branch 1 cells. Among the signalling pathways analysed, only ABA, a central regulator of environmental responses and the circadian clock (an integrator of multiple environmental signals), were enriched (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Table 4). Consequently, this cellular state will henceforth be referred to as the environment-regulated state (ERS), with branch 1 and branch 2 corresponding to ERS-positive (ERS+) and ERS-negative (ERS–) cells, respectively. These terms reflect the presence or absence of this transcriptional state at the single-cell level rather than any intrinsic difference in the capacity of individual cells to respond to environmental signals.

To complement this enrichment analysis, we investigated transcription factors (TFs) that could potentially regulate the ERS. Using an Arabidopsis DAP-seq dataset17 and predicted binding similarities from Cis-BP2 (ref. 18), we predicted putative targets for 800 TFs, filtered using hdWGCNA with the XGBoost algorithm. This process produced putative regulons for 186 TFs expressed in our atlas (average expression scores per cell identity are shown in Fig. 4b). Notably, 84 of these TFs (>45%) showed regulon enrichment in ERS+ cells, a result consistent with the elevated activity of this state (Fig. 4b). These TFs included all the profiled ones linked to ABA signalling (ABF1, ABF2, ATHB5, ATHB6, ATHB7 and HAT22)19,20,21,22,23,24,25 or known to be circadian-regulated, such as members of the REVEILLE family (RVE1, RVE2, RVE6, EPR1 and RVE8)26,27,28 as well as cycling DOF factors (CDF2–CDF5). Consistently, two ERS markers validated by HCR (Fig. 3a), AT4G26080 and AT2G41660, are known direct ABA targets29,30,31. This result provides further support that ABA signalling has a role in ERS regulation.

Because ABA was the only signalling pathway identified by GO enrichment, we functionally tested its role in ERS formation by profiling an ABA biosynthesis mutant (aba2-3)32 and a quintuple ABA receptor mutant (pyl11458)33 (Fig. 4c–e). Both mutants showed a reduced proportion of ERS+ cells across the whole root (Fig. 4d) and in almost all cell types independently, albeit not always significantly (Extended Data Fig. 11a). Consistently, the ERS-specific modules 20 and 21 were the most strongly downregulated co-expression modules, which confirmed that ABA signalling is involved in the establishment of the ERS (Fig. 4e).

The ERS is present in only a subset of cells regardless of lineage, which suggests that it is superimposed on, and independent from, developmental programs. To test this idea, we performed scRNA-seq to profile the root tips of the scr-3 mutant, which exhibits major defects in root cell identity through loss of SCARECROW (SCR) function34 (Extended Data Fig. 11b–d). Using our Col-0 reference atlas for cell-type assignment, we observed the expected depletion of endodermis and protoxylem, together with additional alterations affecting the columella and pericycle. This result is consistent with shifts towards root cap and procambium identities, respectively, and uncovers broader cell identity changes than previously described8,35,36,37 (Extended Data Fig. 11d). Despite these major developmental perturbations, ERS+ cells remained detectable across all cell populations, including those with altered identities (Extended Data Fig. 11b,c). These observations support the conclusion that the ERS represents a transcriptional state superimposed on multiple cell lineage identities regardless of cell type or developmental stage.

Our atlas incorporated a set of experiments designed to test the effect of the composition of the growth medium on cell identities. Given the well-established role of ABA in water-related stresses and the enrichment of GO terms linked to responses to water and nutrients, we speculated that larger reductions in water potential would further increase the proportion of ERS+ cells. To that end, we used a hard-agar model of drought to explore this idea. In this model, reduced water content induces a drought response that more closely resembles soil drying than conventional osmotic treatments38.

Seedlings were grown on half MS containing 100%, 50% or 20% water (two replicates each) and profiled by scRNA-seq (Fig. 5d). Cells were assigned an identity on the basis of our reference atlas. Fewer cells were recovered from the 20% water condition (Supplementary Table 2), together with an enrichment of undifferentiated cells and an underrepresentation of larger differentiated cells (Extended Data Fig. 12c). This result is consistent with their increased fragility under severe water deficit.

We then statistically assessed changes in the proportion of ERS+ cells between the conditions, taking into account cells that can either be in ERS+ or ERS– state, using water concentration as a continuous covariate (Fig. 5e). The proportion of ERS+ cells increased substantially when the water potential was reduced, from 0.38 in 100% water to 0.66 in 20% water. This result demonstrates that water deficit increases the prevalence of ERS+ cells (Fig. 5e). This change was significant both across the whole-root level and in individual cell types (Extended Data Fig. 12d), and was unrelated to differences in cell filtering (here and across the study) (Extended Data Fig. 12h). We observed that cells grown under water-deficit conditions contained more detected transcripts than those grown under control conditions (Extended Data Fig. 12e), which suggests that the increased prevalence of ERS+ cells might reflect higher transcriptional output. However, ERS-specific module scores remained elevated in water-deficit cells with comparable transcript abundance, and this difference remained significant after adjusting for log-transformed UMI counts (Extended Data Fig. 12f). Likewise, logistic regression showed that the increased odds of a cell being classified as ERS+ remained significant after accounting for transcript abundance (Extended Data Fig. 12g), which indicated that the increased prevalence of ERS+ cells cannot be fully explained by higher transcriptional output alone. Whether this result reflects changes in the probability of cells transitioning between ERS+ and ERS− states, or more persistent occupancy of the ERS+ state, cannot be determined from the current data.

To further validate this finding, we examined the expression of two ERS-specific markers that we had already profiled (Fig. 3a)—AT3G04010 and AT4G26080 (which are ABA-responsive)29,30—in vivo by whole-mount HCR in intact root tips under both the control (100% water) and water-deficit (20% water) conditions (Fig. 5f). We observed a marked increase in the number of cells expressing both these genes under the water-deficit condition (Fig. 5f). Quantifications revealed an approximate 1.6-fold increase in overall signal intensity and a 2.2–2.5-fold increase in areas showing a positive signal, independent of differences in root length (Fig. 5g and Extended Data Fig. 12i). Consistent with these measurements, empirical cumulative distribution functions showed a global shift of per-cell fluorescence towards higher values under water-deficit conditions for both gene markers (Fig. 5h). Together, these HCR analyses provide independent in planta evidence that more cells express ERS markers under water-deficit conditions, consistent with the increased prevalence of ERS+ cells detected by scRNA-seq.

Overall, these findings demonstrate that environmental conditions modulate the prevalence of the ERS, thereby establishing this transcriptional state as a dynamic layer of root cell identity that may contribute to adaptation to water-deficit conditions.

Through deep characterization of root cell transcriptional states coupled with HCR spatial mapping, we identified an alternative trajectory for all cell types above the root meristematic region. This trajectory, which we term the ERS, corresponds to a strong and specific transcriptional signature superimposed on a subset of cells across multiple lineages simultaneously. Cell identity in the root tip can therefore be defined by three factors: (1) the lineage that defines the cell type, which remains stable during development; (2) the developmental stage; and (3) the presence or absence of this additional transcriptional state, the prevalence of which is modulated by environmental conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2). Although detected in parallel with developmental trajectories, the ERS seems to be independent of developmental origin, as it was detected across all cell types throughout developmental progression. Instead, it represents an additional layer of identity strongly associated with ABA signalling. Our analyses also suggest that the circadian clock may influence ERS formation. This aspect will be interesting to explore further, particularly given its known feedback with ABA signalling and stress responses39. We note that although an ERS can be detected across all lineages, it is not entirely equivalent in all cell types. Some ERS-specific genes were enriched in all ERS+ cells, whereas others were restricted to specific lineages, which suggests that there is a degree of cell-type specificity in the occurrence of this transcriptional state, which may be shaped by the underlying regulatory networks of each cell type. Differences in ABA sensitivity or signalling capacity among cells within and between cell types could equally contribute to this variability and represent an important avenue for future investigation.

A notable feature of this discovery is the scale of transcriptional heterogeneity observed in cell types, which was largely undetectable using bulk methods before single-cell technologies. Recent imaging approaches have revealed transcriptional heterogeneity in plant tissues40,41,42,43,44. However, to our knowledge, none has reported the clear and consistent dichotomy observed here. The robust presence of two populations across independent datasets and detection methods, together with consistent detection of the same ERS-specific co-expressed gene modules, strongly supports the interpretation that the ERS reflects a regulated transcriptional program. Nonetheless, important questions remain regarding the mechanisms that establish and maintain this state. Exploring the temporal dynamics of the ERS will be essential, including how rapidly it is induced and how stable it remains under constant or changing environments. Moreover, it will be important to determine whether the proportions of ERS+ and ERS– cells detected by scRNA-seq reflect stochastic transitions between states, the probabilities of which are modulated by environmental conditions. Live imaging of ERS marker genes will be an important avenue to address these questions. Equally important will be determining the signals that govern entry into and exit from the ERS, including whether ABA directly influences the probability that cells adopt this state or whether it permissively sets the proportion of cells competent to do so.

Another open question is the spatial arrangement and coordination of ERS+ cells. We showed that the ERS can occur in all root layers, independent of cell type, but the signal determining which cells enter this state is unclear. The increase in ERS prevalence under water-deficit conditions suggests that this state is part of a physiological response to the environment. If this response is linked to water and nutrient availability, it would make sense that it spans from the epidermis, which senses and absorbs these resources, to the vasculature, which distributes them throughout the plant. As such, ERS formation may contribute to, or result from, a coordinated response across the tissue. The persistence of the ERS across environmental and genetic perturbations suggests that it represents an important physiological state. Correlating ERS occurrence with known pathways or functional markers will be crucial to clarify its role.

The marked increase in ERS prevalence under water-deficit conditions demonstrates that this transcriptional state is dynamically regulated by environmental conditions. Determining which environmental cues regulate ERS prevalence, how these signals are integrated and how the ERS influences cellular physiology will be important next steps. More broadly, our findings establish the ERS as a previously unrecognized layer of root cell identity. We anticipate that the identification of the ERS will facilitate the discovery and investigation of additional environmentally regulated transcriptional states across plant tissues and species. As such, it provides a framework for understanding how environmental regulation shapes cell identity across plant tissues and how plants dynamically remodel cellular states in response to their environment, which will ultimately advance our understanding of plant physiology.

The method was applied independently to each library and manually adjusted, which enabled the proportion of filtered cells to vary according to the intrinsic quality of each preparation. The procedure (illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1) is described below.

A Seurat (v.5.0.0)47 object was created from the N barcodes identified as cells by CellRanger, excluding genes expressed in fewer than three cells. Data were log-normalized and scaled. Highly variable genes (HVGs) were identified using FindVariableFeatures (nfeatures=15,000). Genes affected by protoplast isolation (see the section ‘Identification of genes affected by protoplast isolation’), as well as mitochondrial and chloroplastic genes, were excluded from the HVG set. The first 40 principal components were used for UMAP dimensionality reduction and clustering. Major root cell types (root cap, epidermis, cortex, endodermis, pericycle, phloem, xylem and procambium) were annotated.

The following steps were applied to each cell type individually:

A new Seurat object was created containing the ‘cell type’ cells and the first top N barcodes (ranked by UMI content) that were classified as background by CellRanger. The object was processed as above, with the exception that FindVariableFeatures was run with the parameter nfeatures=5000 and that the first 20 principal components were used for UMAP and cell clustering.

The object was subsetted to retain only the cluster (or clusters) containing the ‘cell type’ cells, which typically also includes some background barcodes. This subsetted object was then re-processed as in the previous step.

In this subset, background barcodes typically form distinct clusters, often together with low-quality cells of the given type. All cells clustering with background were considered more similar to background than to high-quality cells and were reclassified as background and discarded.

Cells retained after filtering across all types were merged into a new Seurat object.

Using this approach, between 8 and 30% of CellRanger-identified cells were typically removed. Preparations processed using 10x Genomics NextGem chemistry tended to generate a higher proportion of discarded cells.

Doublets were identified using scDblFinder48,49 following the recommendations of the Single-cell Best Practices guide (www.sc-best-practices.org). The analysis was performed on the Seurat object obtained after cell filtering, with the parameters artificialDoublets=25000, dims=30, dbr.sd=1 and propRandom=1. All cells identified as doublets were subsequently removed from the dataset.

After doublet removal, preparations composed of a mix of two Arabidopsis ecotypes were subjected to cell genotyping based on SNPs. SNP UMIs were counted per cell using the countsnps command in the sctools package (https://github.com/timoast/sctools). To separate the two ecotype populations, density-based clustering was performed on the SNP UMI counts for each ecotype using the dbscan50 package in R (Supplementary Fig. 1), with the eps and minPts parameters adjusted for each dataset. The few cells that did not cluster with either population were discarded.

Each condition included at least three replicates, except for the water-deficit experiments, which had two replicates each. Replicates were integrated using Seurat (v.5.0.0) to generate batch-corrected atlases. In brief, replicates were merged into a single Seurat object. HVGs were detected using the FindVariableFeatures function with nfeatures=25000. Genes affected by protoplasting, as well as mitochondrial and chloroplastic genes, were excluded from the HVG dataset. Data were scaled, and the first 50 principal components were computed using RunPCA. Replicates were then integrated using the anchor-based RPCA method in Seurat (v.5.0.0) with IntegrateLayers, using method=RPCAIntegration and dims=1:50.

For the Col-0 reference atlas, the replicates RL650, RL970 and RL971, which exhibited the highest data complexity, were used as reference during integration with reference=1:3. The resulting 50-dimensional integrated RPCA reduction was used for UMAP and clustering.

In some cases, after the integration step, a few diffuse groups of low-UMI cells could be identified associated with the epidermis and cortex lineages, which probably represent low-quality cells that had passed the initial filtering step. To remove these, separate ‘epidermis–root cap’ and ‘cortex’ objects were created, re-integrated and processed as described above. After removing the low-quality cells, the atlas was re-processed with RPCA integration, and the first 45 dimensions were used for UMAP and clustering.

In other cases beyond the Col-0 atlas, small clusters of low-quality cells were also removed; see the integration scripts for further details.

Plants were prepared as for scRNA-seq, as described above. Four replicates each of dissociated and non-dissociated root tips were compared. For dissociated samples, root tips were placed in the protoplasting solution for 1 h at room temperature, as for scRNA-seq. After this incubation step, samples were centrifuged for 5 min at 500g, the supernatant was removed and the tissue samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground into a powder. For non-dissociated samples, root tips were collected and directly snap-frozen and ground into powder in liquid nitrogen. Bulk RNA-seq libraries were generated using a TruSeq stranded mRNA kit (Illumina). Bulk RNA-seq libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 1500 (single end, 101 cycles). RNA-seq read quality was assessed using FastQC and MultiQC51,52. RNA-seq reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic (v.0.36) with the following parameters: ILLUMINACLIP:TruSeq3-SE.fa:2:30:10 LEADING:3 TRAILING:3 SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15 MINLEN:36 (ref. 53). Reads were aligned to the TAIR10 genome using STAR54 with the following parameters: --alignIntronMax 5000, --alignIntronMin 10, --readFilesCommand zcat, --quantMode GeneCounts, --outSAMtype BAM SortedByCoordinate. Differentially expressed genes resulting from the cell dissociation step were identified using the DESeq2 R package55, with a Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted P < 0.001 and a log2 fold change in expression of >2. This experiment was repeated twice, and genes that were identified as differentially expressed in both experiments were taken as the set of genes affected by protoplast isolation.

Candidate cluster markers for our reference atlas were generated using the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat, with the only.pos = TRUE parameter. To select the best target genes, cluster markers were filtered on the basis of the following criteria: the gene should be detected almost only in the cluster (or clusters) it is enriched in; and the gene should have a large portion of its coding sequence that does not share homology with other Arabidopsis genes, which will enable the design of highly specific probes for these sequences. Split probes for our target genes were designed and synthesized by Molecular Instruments (www.molecularinstruments.com).

Experiments were performed as previously described56. Roots were collected at 6 d.a.g., as for scRNA-seq, and immediately placed in FAA (7.4% formaldehyde, 5% acetic acid and 50% ethanol in ultrapure water) for 1 h at room temperature. Samples were then dehydrated in successive 10 min washes at room temperature: once in 70% ethanol, once in 90% ethanol and twice in 100% ethanol. Samples were washed twice in 100% methanol and left in the second wash at −20 °C overnight (or up to a few weeks).

Roots were rehydrated in a series of 5 min washes at room temperature in 75%, 50%, 25% methanol and in DPBS-T (0.1% Tween 20 in DPBS) and incubated in 1× cell wall digestion solution (10× stock: 250 mg macerozyme, 250 mg cellulase, 125 mg pectolyase and 375 mg pectinase in 50 ml ultrapure water) in DPBS-T for 5 min. After a wash in DPBS-T, samples were fixed in 10% (v/v) formaldehyde (37% solution, Sigma-Aldrich) in DPBS-T for 15 min at room temperature and washed twice in DPBS-T. Roots were incubated in 1 ml proteinase K buffer (0.1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8) and 50 mM EDTA (pH 8) in ultrapure water) with 8 μl proteinase K (10 mg ml–1) for 15 min at 37 °C. After two washes in DPBS-T, samples were fixed in 10% (v/v) formaldehyde (37% solution, Sigma-Aldrich) in DPBS-T for 15 min at room temperature and washed twice in DPBS-T.

We followed the third-generation in situ HCR procedure developed by Molecular Instruments, and as previously described10, but the dextran sulfate used in the amplification buffer had to be replaced by a low-molecular-weight dextran sulfate to preserve the root structure. Roots were pre-hybridized in the 30% probe hybridization buffer for 30 min at 37 °C, then placed in the probe solution (2–3 μl of probe mixture in 500 μl of 30% probe hybridization buffer) and incubated at 37 °C overnight.

Samples were washed twice in the 30% probe wash buffer for 30 min at 37 °C, then washed twice in 5× SSCT (5× SSC and 0.1% Tween 20 in ultrapure water) for 10 min at room temperature. Samples were placed in the amplification buffer for 30 min at room temperature. In the meantime, fluorescently labelled hairpins were prepared by snap-cooling 10 μl of 3 μM stock in hairpin storage buffer (heat at 95 °C for 90 s in a thermocycler and cool to room temperature in the dark for 30 min). We predominantly used HCR amplifiers coupled to Alexa 647 fluorophores in this study, with Alexa 488-coupled amplifiers used for experiments targeting two genes simultaneously. The hairpin solution was prepared by adding the snap-cooled hairpins to 500 μl amplification buffer. Samples were placed in the hairpin solution and incubated overnight at room temperature in the dark. They were subsequently washed 3 times in 5× SSCT for 20 min at room temperature.

Samples were incubated in ClearSee11 in the dark for 3 days.

Cell walls were stained by incubating samples in 1 ml ClearSee and 2 μl calcofluor solution (50 mg ml–1 in DMSO) for 2 h in the dark. Samples were washed in ClearSee for 1 h, in the dark, and mounted in ClearSee in a chamber made with a square coverslip and double-sided tape. The chamber was sealed using top coat nail polish, and samples were imaged on the same day.

Mounted roots were imaged using a Nikon Ti-E inverted motorized microscope with a Nikon A1Si spectral detector confocal system or a Nikon A1RMP confocal microscope, controlled by NIS-Elements software, and a ×60 water-immersion objective. Calcofluor white and the Alex 488- and the Alexa 647-coupled amplifiers were excited at 405 nm, 488 nm and 640 nm, respectively. Seven to eight images were taken per root along the differentiation axis. Images were processed using Fiji57. Images of the same roots were stitched using the Grid/Collection stitching plugin58, and the brightness and contrast were adjusted for each image based on the signal detected. Optical sections were generated using the Orthogonal Views method in Fiji.

All datasets other than the Col-0 atlas were annotated using the Col-0 atlas as the reference. Anchors between the reference atlas and the other datasets were identified using FindTransferAnchors in Seurat (v.5), specifying the Col-0 RPCA-integrated data as the reference assay. TransferData in Seurat (v.5) was then used to calculate, for each cell, a prediction score for every identity, based on the identified anchors and the atlas annotations. Each cell was assigned the identity with the highest prediction score.

We used the CellCycleScoring function in Seurat to assign a cell cycle score to each cell of our reference atlas based on the expression of S phase marker genes (E2Fc (also known as AT1G47870), E2Fb (AT5G22220), DPa (also known as AT5G02470), CDKG;2 (also known as AT1G67580), E2Fa (also known as AT2G36010), DPb (also known as AT5G03415), CYCA3;1 (also known as AT5G43080), CYCA3;2 (also known as AT1G47210), KRP5 (also known as AT3G24810), KRP2 (also known as AT3G50630), KRP6 (also known as AT3G19150), KRP3 (also known as AT5G48820), WEE (also known as AT1G02970), KRP4 (also known as AT2G32710), CYCD4;2 (also known as AT5G10440)) and of G2/M phase marker genes (CYCB1;1 (also known as AT4G37490), CYCB2;1 (also known as AT2G17620), CDKB2;2 (also known as AT1G20930), CYCB3;1 (also known as AT1G16330), CYCA2;2 (also known as AT5G11300), CYCA2;4 (also known as AT1G80370), CYCA1;1 (also known as AT1G44110), CYCA2;1 (also known as AT5G25380), CDKD;1 (also known as AT1G73690), CDKB2;1 (also known as AT1G76540), CKS2 (also known as AT2G27970), CYCB1;2 (also known as AT5G06150), CYCD3;1 (also known as AT4G34160), CCS52B (also known as AT5G13840), CYCA2;3 (also known as AT1G15570), CCS52A2 (also known as AT4G11920), CYCB2;3 (also known as AT1G20610) and CYCB1;3 (also known as AT3G11520)) obtained from a previous study59. This score was used to provide a predicted classification of the cell cycle phase and to regress out cell cycle effects using vars.to.regress = c(“S.Score”, “G2M.Score”) in the ScaleData function. After regression, the top 50 principal components were calculated and the replicates were integrated again. UMAP dimensionality reduction was then performed, and cell identities were visualized in the embedding to confirm that both branches remained present.

The Col-0 atlas was subsetted into nine lineage-specific objects: epidermis, root cap, cortex, endodermis, PPP, XPP, procambium, phloem and xylem. Each lineage dataset was then processed independently.

Subclusters in each lineage were identified using FindNeighbors and FindClusters in Seurat (v.5), with the values of dims, k.param and resolution adapted to each lineage.

For each lineage, a ‘root cell’ corresponding to t = 0 in the trajectory was defined, as required by the diffusion pseudotime analysis underlying the PAGA trajectory inference. In the case of the epidermis and root cap lineages, developmental progression is known to originate at their junction. Therefore, for the epidermal lineage, the root cell was defined as the cell with the lowest coordinate along the UMAP y axis. For the root cap lineage, the root cell was defined as the cell with the highest coordinate along the UMAP y axis. For all other lineages, for which such anatomical landmarks are less obvious, the root cell was defined as the cell with the highest meristematic score (see below).

To perform trajectory inference, we used the SCP package60, which implements PAGA61,62,63 analysis directly in R, thereby avoiding conversion of Seurat objects to AnnData format to run scanpy63. Seurat v.5 assays were first converted to v.3 assays to enable SCP. Trajectories were inferred with the RunPAGA function, grouping cells by the subclusters defined above, with the following parameters: inear_reduction = “integrated.rpca”, nonlinear_reduction = “umap.rpca”, infer_pseudotime=TRUE, n_pcs and n_neighbours matching the values used for subclustering, and root_cell containing the barcode of the designated root cell. The trajectory topology was visualized with the PAGAPlot function, using reduction = “umap.rpca” and an edge_threshold adapted to each lineage. The pseudotime value assigned to each cell was then transferred to the metadata of the original Seurat (v.5) object.

The diffusion pseudotime analysis required the identification of a cell that would serve as t = 0; that is, the starting point of each trajectory. To do so, we developed an approach for identifying the ‘most meristematic’ cell in each lineage.

The epidermis is the only lineage for which the meristematic region can be unambiguously located based on both the topology of its clusters and the known biology of the tissue. It lies at the junction with the root cap, before differentiation into the two epidermal subtypes: hair cells and non-hair cells. This cluster was therefore designated as epidermis–meristem. To identify marker genes for this region, we ran the FindMarkers function on the subsetted Seurat object containing only epidermal clusters, with ident.1 = 5 (corresponding to the ‘epidermis–meristem’ cluster), only.pos=TRUE, and test.use = ‘t’. The resulting gene list was then used with the AddModuleScore function in Seurat (v.5) to assign a meristematic score to every cell in the atlas.

We reasoned that at least a subset of the genes enriched in the epidermal meristem would also be enriched in meristematic regions of other lineages. Thus, the cell with the highest meristematic score in each lineage was taken to represent the most likely earliest developmental state and used as the root cell (t = 0) for pseudotime analysis.

Monocle 3 (v.1.3.7)64,65,66 was used as an alternative method to confirm the developmental trajectories obtained using PAGA. Our Col-0 atlas was converted to a cds object using SeuratWrappers (v.0.4.0) (https://github.com/satijalab/seurat-wrappers). Cells were clustered and partitioned with cluster_cells using resolution=0.0002, and trajectories were computed using learn_graph with learn_graph_control=list(minimal_branch_len=20)). As for the PAGA trajectories, root cells were defined as those with the highest meristematic score per lineage, and these were used in order_cells to assign a pseudotime to each cell in the trajectory. All plots were generated with plot_cells. Concordance between (scaled) Monocle and diffusion pseudotime assignments was assessed using Spearman’s correlation coefficients computed independently for each lineage.

RNA velocity analysis was performed using the dynamical model implemented in the R package velociraptor67 (v.1.10.0) (wrapping scVelo68) on a per-lineage basis. Spliced and unspliced count matrices were generated independently for each library from velocyto69 (v.0.17.17) loom files and merged after restricting to genes detected across all samples. Because the intronic signal in Arabidopsis scRNA-seq datasets is limited, only genes with sufficient spliced and unspliced coverage were retained for velocity inference (minimum 50 cells with unspliced counts and 300 total unspliced counts; minimum 50 cells with spliced counts and 1,000 total spliced counts globally across the dataset). The top 4,000 variable genes were identified independently for each lineage, and protoplasting-sensitive genes, mitochondrial genes and chloroplast genes were excluded before analysis. The final gene set used for velocity inference corresponded to the intersection between lineage variable features and genes passing global spliced or unspliced filtering criteria. For each lineage, a SingleCellExperiment object containing spliced, unspliced and total RNA counts was generated and analysed using the scVelo dynamical model with the RPCA integrated embedding used as the neighbourhood graph for kinetic inference. Root cells for latent time estimation were defined independently for each lineage as the top 1% of cells with the highest meristematic score. Given the relatively low proportion of unspliced reads in our dataset (about 3% median unspliced fraction), RNA velocity was used primarily as an orthogonal validation of trajectory structure rather than as the primary trajectory inference approach. Concordance between scVelo latent time and diffusion pseudotime was assessed using Spearman’s correlation coefficients computed independently for each lineage.

To test whether the apparent absence of ERS-associated genes in ERS− cells could be explained by reduced transcript capture, we compared the expression behaviour of genes from ERS+-specific modules (modules 20 and 21) and modules expressed in both branches (modules 13 and 14) in the XPP lineage, the largest lineage exhibiting the ERS dichotomy. Average log-normalized expression was calculated independently in ERS+ and ERS− cells for each gene. To compare genes across a shared expression range, overlapping expression intervals between ERS+-specific and shared-module genes were defined using the 10–90th percentile range of average ERS+ expression for each group. A linear model describing the relationship between ERS+ and ERS− expression was then fitted using genes from modules expressed in both branches. Residual ERS− expression values (observed minus expected expression) were subsequently calculated for all genes relative to this model. Residual distributions were compared between ERS+-specific genes and genes expressed in both branches in the overlapping expression interval using a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test. This analysis tested whether ERS+-specific genes exhibited systematically lower ERS− expression than expected based solely on their expression level in ERS+ cells.

Because the number of transcripts detected in branch 1 was systematically higher than in branch 2, we tested whether the observed branching events could result from coverage artefacts. To control for this factor, UMI counts were selectively downsampled in ERS+ cells to match the UMI distribution of ERS− cells in each cell type. Specifically, for each ERS+ cell, a target UMI count was assigned based on quantile matching to the corresponding ERS− cell-type distribution, and counts were downsampled using the downsampleMatrix function from DropletUtils70 (v1.20.0). ERS− cells were left unchanged. Three independent downsampled count matrices were generated using different random seeds. Each downsampled matrix was used to generate a new Seurat object, which was processed independently using the same workflow as the reference atlas, including log-normalization, variable feature selection, scaling, PCA and UMAP embedding. Protoplasting-sensitive genes, mitochondrial genes and chloroplast genes were excluded from the variable feature set before dimensionality reduction. A meristematic score was then calculated for each cell using the same gene set as in the reference atlas.

We used hdWGCNA (v0.4.1)71,72,73 to identify modules of co-expressed genes across our Col-0 reference atlas. To determine the list of genes that would be used in the analysis, we first applied the SetupForWGCNA function to obtain all genes expressed in at least 10% of cells in at least one lineage. This list was intersected with the VariableFeatures of the atlas, which contains all genes detected in every replicate, excluding those affected by protoplast isolation as well as mitochondrial and chloroplastic genes. The resulting set of 12,923 genes was supplied to SetupForWGCNA with the parameter gene_select = “custom”.

To reduce data sparsity before WGCNA, we generated metacells (aggregates of small groups of similar cells per biological sample) and their associated expression matrices, which served as inputs for the network analysis. Metacells were generated using the MetacellsByGroups function using group.by=c(“lineage”,“orig.ident”), reduction = “integrated.rpca”, ident.group = “lineage”, k = 20 and max_shared=5, followed by normalization with NormalizeMetacells.

We then set up the expression dataset for consensus network analysis across all replicates using SetMultiExpr with multi.group.by = “orig.ident”. After determining the soft power threshold for each batch with the TestSoftPowersConsensus function, the consensus co-expression network was constructed using ConstructNetworkusing minModuleSize=30, consensus=TRUE and deepSplit=2.

Module scores were computed using ModuleExprScore using n_genes = “all” and method = “Seurat”, and projected onto the atlas UMAP with ModuleFeaturePlot using features = “scores”.

GO enrichment analysis was performed on the gene sets from modules 20 and 21 using ShinyGO (v.0.82; https://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/). The analysis was run with the GO Biological Process pathway database, an FDR cut-off value of 0.01 and the number of pathways to display set to 50. Pathways were ranked by fold enrichment, and the results were visualized as a hierarchical clustering tree.

Putative TF target genes were inferred from the DAP-seq dataset17. The ‘Motifs in peaks (P ≤ 1 × 10–4)’ BED file (http://neomorph.salk.edu/dap_web/pages/browse_table_aj.php) was used as a DNA-binding database for the profiled TFs of the DAP-seq dataset.

The 500 bp promoter coordinates for Arabidopsis genes were extracted using the promoters function in GenomicRanges74, genes(TxDb.Athaliana.BioMart.plantsmart28) as an annotation for Arabidopsis genes and the arguments upstream = 500, downstream = 50. For each TF, the binding coordinates were extracted from the Motif in Peaks.bed file and intersected with the promoter coordinates using the subsetByOverlaps function in GenomicRanges. Every gene that had a binding motif for the TF in the 500 bp region upstream of its transcription start site (TSS) was considered a putative target gene. For TFs that were not profiled in the DAP-seq studies but, according to the cisBP2 study18, have a strong similarity in their DNA-binding domains with another TF that was studied by DAP-seq, their target genes were considered to be the same as the TF profiled by DAP-seq.

We then converted the TF target predictions into data to be processed with hdWGCNA. A sparse matrix was generated whereby rows are all genes in the dataset, columns are TFs with putative targets and values are set to 1 if the gene was a target of the TF and 0 otherwise. This was input into the Seurat object using the SetMotifMatrix function of hdWGCNA (v.0.4.1). The list of TFs and their target genes was set with SetMotifTargets, and a dataframe containing the target numbers was input with SetMotifs. Because we were interested in what drives the expression of co-expression modules in the dataset, we restricted the analysis to all genes that were assigned to a co-expression module as well as all TFs with target information. A network of TFs and target genes was built using ConstructTFNetwork, in which the XGBoost regression was used to evaluate how important each TF is for predicting each gene based on single-cell data72,75. The network was then pruned to keep only the strongest TF–gene connections. Specifically, we selected the top ten TFs above a regulatory score threshold as regulators for each gene, using the AssignTFRegulons function with strategy = “A”, reg_thresh=0.01 and n_tfs=10. A positive regulon score was then computed for each cell using RegulonScores, and the scaled average regulon score per cell identity was plotted using pheatmap76.

Three published Arabidopsis root single-cell datasets were used to verify that the ERS and associated ERS-specific genes described in this study are also present in other datasets7,8,16. For ref. 8, we directly used the processed Seurat object provided by the authors. For ref. 7, we reprocessed the raw fastq files using the same pre-processing steps as described for our Col-0 atlas. For ref. 16, we used the count matrices provided by the authors and performed batch integration.

Once the final Seurat objects were generated, cells were annotated using our reference atlas following the same procedure described in the section ‘Cell annotation’ above. Cells were also projected onto the atlas UMAP embedding using the MapQuery function in Seurat (v.5.0.0). Co-expression gene modules were projected onto these datasets using the ProjectModules function in hdWGCNA (v.0.4.1), and module scores were calculated with ModuleExprScore using n_genes = “all” and method = “Seurat”.

To assess the robustness of ERS assignments in external datasets, label transfer was repeated across a range of Seurat parameter settings using the integrated Col-0 atlas as reference. Specifically, projections were performed using 30, 40 or 50 principal components and k.anchor values of 5, 10 or 20, which generated nine parameter combinations in total. For each parameter set, transfer anchors were identified using FindTransferAnchors with the RPCA integrated embedding, and cell identities were assigned using TransferData. To focus analyses on cells robustly assigned to the ERS bifurcation, only cells classified as ERS+ or ERS− in the baseline parameter set (50 principal components, k.anchor = 5) were retained for downstream comparisons. Predicted identities were separated into cell-type and ERS-state components, and stability was assessed independently for both. Cell-type stability was quantified as the fraction of parameter sets in which each cell retained its baseline cell-type assignment, whereas ERS stability was calculated as the consistency of ERS+ and ERS− assignment in the baseline cell type across parameter sets. Global agreement between parameter sets was further quantified using pairwise adjusted rand index (ARI) comparisons across all transferred labels. Furthermore, the proportion of ERS+ cells in each cell type, the fraction of cells retaining their baseline cell type assignment and the frequency of ERS state switching across parameter sets were calculated independently for each external dataset.

The fragment files generated with Cell Ranger ATAC (v.1.2) and mapped to TAIR10/Araport11 reference were obtained from a previous study16, imported to snapatac2 (v.2.6.4) and paired-insertions calculated77 in 500 bp bins78. The cells were initially defined as barcodes associated with more than 3,500 and fewer than 50,000 fragments per barcode and with a TSS enrichment score higher than 2.5. Low-quality cells and outliers were also identified per-sample basis using the 3× median absolute deviation threshold and further removed from analysis79. Following peak calling using macs3 (ref. 80), peak and gene activity matrices were calculated using the paired-insertion counting strategy. For ATAC–seq data integration into our scRNA-seq dataset, the peak matrices were imported to the Signac package81 and the dimensions of ATAC–seq were reduced using latent semantic indexing. ATAC–seq nuclei were assigned identities using our reference atlas and the FindTransferAnchors function with the parameters reduction = ‘cca’ and dims = 1:30, followed by TransferData with weight.reduction = obj[[“lsi”]] and dims = 2:30. The TransferData function and previously computed anchors were also used to generate an ‘imputed’ scRNA-seq matrix for the ATAC nuclei. Using this imputed matrix, the snATAC–seq and scRNA-seq reference atlases were merged into a single object, processed with ScaleData and RunPCA, and the first 30 principal components were used to produce a UMAP co-embedding of the two datasets.

To perform differential expression analysis of our co-expression modules, a Seurat object was created with all conditions to compare, and the data were scaled using ScaleData (Seurat v.5.0.0). The modules were projected into the new dataset using ProjectModules (hdWGCNA v.0.4.1). A module score was then calculated using ModuleExprScore. The differential module scores were determined using the FindDMEs function with features = “ModuleScores” and test.use = “wilcox”. The results were plotted using PlotDMEsLollipop.

All changes in cell composition were tested with sccomp (v.1.99.18) in R82. Specifically, when evaluating changes in ERS+ (and consequently ERS−) proportions, the Seurat object was subsetted to retain only cells that had reached the developmental stage at which they could be assigned to either state to avoid taking into account sampling effects that might affect the total number of mature cells captured. This process excluded the root cap, ‘early’ identities and the non-hair cells, for which the trajectories could determine the point of branching. The test was then run on the subsetted data using sccomp_estimate and sccomp_test, and results were plotted with sccomp_boxplot or with plot in the case of the water-deficit experiment for which the covariate is continuous.

Seedlings were grown vertically on full (1×) or half (0.5×) MS plates, as for scRNA-seq experiments. Pictures of the roots were taken at 6 d.a.g., using a stereomicroscope (n = 16 for full MS, n = 23 for half MS, from two different plates each). A portion corresponding to approximately 2.5 mm from the tip was selected for each image of the root, and root hairs were manually counted using the Cell Counter plugin in Fiji. A significant difference in root hair density between the two conditions was determined using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test (wilcox.test function) in R.

Note that the seedlings used for this quantification were in the top row of the plate (seeds were sown in two rows for scRNA-seq experiments). Roots of seedlings grown in the bottom row tended to have fewer root hairs (in both conditions).

A new Seurat object was created using the Col-0 cells that were grown on full MS (which is all replicates except the three half MS replicates), as well as the cells of the C24, Cvi-0, Ler and Ws-2 atlases. The expression data were then pseudobulked per genotype, cell identity and batch using the AggregateExpression in Seurat (v.5.0.0). For each possible inter-accession replicate pair, a differential expression test was performed on the pseudobulk data using FindMarkers with DESeq2. Genes were considered differentially expressed if the adjusted P < 0.05 and the absolute log2 fold-change was >0.5. For a gene to be called an accession-specific DEG, it had to be systematically differentially expressed in a given accession compared with the other four, but not be differentially expressed in any pairwise comparison between the remaining four accessions. The proportion of ecotype-specific DEGs per identity or per module were plotted using pheatmap.

Module scores for hdWGCNA modules 20 and 21 were calculated using AddModuleScore in Seurat (v.5.0.0). Analyses were restricted to cells classified as ERS+ or ERS− from the 100% and 20% water conditions. Total UMI counts were log10-transformed after addition of a pseudocount. Module scores were analysed using linear mixed-effects models implemented in the lme4 (v.1.1.36) and lmerTest (v.3.2.1) R packages, with water condition as a fixed effect, log10 UMI count modelled using a natural spline with four degrees of freedom (splines v.4.3.1 package) and biological replicate included as a random intercept. For visualization, cells were divided into ten equal-frequency bins according to log10 UMI count, and mean module scores ± s.e.m. were calculated in each bin. Binning was used for visualization only; all statistical inference was performed on the unbinned cell-level data. The association between water condition and ERS identity was assessed using binomial generalized linear mixed-effects models (glmer; lme4) with biological replicate included as a random intercept. An unadjusted model included water condition alone, whereas the adjusted model also included log10 UMI count as a fixed-effect covariate. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals for the effect of water condition were obtained using broom.mixed (v.0.2.9.6).

To compare roots grown under different conditions using whole-mount HCR, all roots were grown, processed and imaged simultaneously using identical confocal settings.

First, a mask defining the cytoplasmic area of the root was created in Fiji. A threshold-based mask of the whole root was generated from the calcofluor channel using Image > Adjust > Threshold, followed by Process > Binary > Close and Process > Binary > Fill Holes. A second threshold-based mask was created from the calcofluor channel to define cell walls. Subtracting the cell wall mask from the whole-root mask (Process > Math > Subtract) produced a mask containing only the cytoplasmic regions. This mask was then multiplied by a mask selecting the region from the elongation zone upwards, corresponding to the area where ERS expression begins in single-cell data.

The resulting cytoplasmic mask was multiplied by the channel containing the HCR signal to retain only signal in cells from the elongation zone upwards. A separate threshold-based mask was created from the HCR signal channel to identify areas above a defined signal threshold. Multiplying this mask by the cytoplasmic mask ensured that only signal inside cells from the elongation zone upwards was considered.

Custom Fiji macros were used to automate measurements across z-stacks. One macro measured the surface area covered by a mask in each slice, which enabled calculation of the total cytoplasmic area for normalization and the area of positive signal. Another macro measured the sum of all pixel intensities per slice, which was applied to the cytoplasmic signal image.

Differences in signal between conditions were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Single-cell segmentation was performed in 3D using the cell-wall staining channel. Individual cells were first manually seeded by placing markers in each cell lumen and storing these positions as regions of interest. A binary marker image was then generated from the seed positions. The cell wall channel was processed using the MorphoLibJ83 Morphological Filters (3D) function with the Gradient filter to enhance cell boundaries. The processed cell-wall image and binary marker image were subsequently used for marker-controlled watershed segmentation using MorphoLibJ. Segmentation outputs were manually curated to correct improper segmentations caused by discontinuities or holes in cell-wall staining, either by removing incorrectly segmented cells or merging oversegmented regions. Before fluorescence quantification, signal channels were background-corrected to minimize the contribution of imaging noise and diffuse background fluorescence. In brief, pixel intensities were measured in a region outside the root tissue, and the 99th percentile background intensity value was subtracted from all pixels in the image using the Subtract function in Fiji. The resulting filtered fluorescence images were combined with the curated segmentation masks to quantify volumetric single-cell mean fluorescence intensity using the Intensity Measurements 2D/3D function in MorphoLibJ. Measured fluorescence values were then mapped back onto segmented cells using the Assign Measure to Labels function in MorphoLibJ to generate colour-coded single-cell fluorescence representations.

For the water-deficit HCR experiments, segmentation was restricted to epidermal and cortical cells. Fluorescence quantification was performed on the original, non-background-subtracted images so that identical image processing was applied across control and water-deficit samples, which enabled direct comparison of fluorescence intensities between conditions. Mean fluorescence intensity was measured for each segmented cell. To account for batch-to-batch differences in HCR signal intensity, the mean fluorescence intensity for each segmented cell was normalized to the mean per-cell fluorescence of the corresponding 100% water control sample processed in parallel. Empirical cumulative distribution functions were then generated to compare the distributions of normalized single-cell fluorescence intensities between conditions.

To test whether mutually exclusive marker expression defined two separable cell populations, Gaussian mixture modelling was applied independently to fluorescence quantification data and scRNA-seq expression data using the R package mclust84 (v.6.0.0). For fluorescence imaging data, mean single-cell fluorescence intensities for AT3G01720 and AT5G56550 were extracted from segmented cells following background correction and volumetric quantification. A marker ratio was calculated for each cell as AT3G01720/(AT3G01720 + AT5G56550), and Gaussian mixture modelling was performed on the resulting distribution using Mclust. Cells were assigned to the inferred populations based on maximum posterior probability. The fitted Gaussian components and their relative proportions were visualized over the observed ratio distribution. For scRNA-seq data, normalized expression values for AT3G01720 and AT5G56550 were extracted from hair cells in the reference atlas. Cells with zero combined expression for both markers were excluded from the analysis. A marker score was then calculated as the difference between AT3G01720 and AT5G56550 expression values, and Gaussian mixture modelling was applied to the score distribution using Mclust. Fitted Gaussian distributions were visualized together with the observed score distribution to assess the presence of two transcriptionally distinct populations.

#1026. Nobelitis — How winning a Nobel Prize can give you delusions of grandeur

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

After being chosen for one of the world's most prestigious awards, some Nobel laureates lose touch with reality and suddenly start spouting unscientific nonsense. They're afflicted by what some have called "Nobelitis."

Linus Pauling won not one, but two Nobel Prizes — the first, for chemistry, in 1954 for his research into the nature of the chemical bond, and the Peace Prize in 1962 for his work in the anti-nuclear weapons movement.

The American chemist not only won two Nobel Prizes, but won them on his own, a rare feat for any scientist. Such an honor often turns laureates into global superstars overnight, with millions hanging on their every word.

That instant fame can be dangerous, said Nils Hansson, a professor in the Department of History, Theory and Ethics of Medicine at Heinrich Heine University in Düsseldorf. Especially, he said, when these world-renowned experts "start spreading crazy, unproven scientific theories" — ideas that, when disseminated by Nobel laureates, risk being taken seriously.

That's what happened with Pauling. Beginning in the mid 1960s he began claiming that vitamin C was a universal remedy, a cure for everything from cancer to cardiovascular conditions and degenerative diseases like Alzheimer's disease, multiple sclerosis or osteoarthritis.

Pauling developed an alternative treatment he called orthomolecular medicine, based on specific doses of vitamins and minerals for each patient. To this day, some 60 years later, evidence for his methods remains inconclusive.

Pauling's case is a classic example of "Nobelitis," or "Nobel disease" — not an official diagnosis, but an informal term used to describe how some laureates begin to suffer delusions of grandeur later in life. It was first coined by Paul Nurse, president of The Royal Society, the UK's national academy of sciences, and himself a winner of the Nobel Prize.

"Be skeptical of everything, including yourself and your own ideas," Nurse said in a 2019 interview with KU Leuven University in Belgium, when asked what made someone a good scientist. In cases of Nobelitis, that self-skepticism is the first to go.

That was also the case with virologist Luc Montagnier, who was jointly honored in 2008 for his discovery of the human immunodeficiency virus, HIV, with colleague Francoise Barre-Sinoussi in the early 1980s.

Montagnier would later go on to support homeopathic medicine, another alternative treatment of disease and claim that HIV transmission in Africa could be diminished through better hygiene and nutrition.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, Montagnier said the SARS-CoV-2 virus had been created in a lab with parts of the HIV genetic code. "It's difficult for a practicing virologist to say that a Nobel laureate in virology is spreading nonsense," said German virologist Christian Drosten on NDR's Coronavirus Update podcast in May 2020. "But that is utter nonsense."

James Watson, who was never known for his modesty, also let fame go to his head. In 1962, he was awarded the Nobel Prize along with Maurice Wilkins and Francis Crick for their discovery of the structure of the DNA molecule. But in the early 2000s, Watson repeatedly spread racist theories that Black people were less intelligent than white people.

For medical historian Hansson, Watson is a prime example that the Nobel Prize is not automatically awarded to a person who should be considered as a role model.

"You may have made a fantastic discovery, but that doesn't make you a universal genius," he said.

But the great expectations of an admiring public may also contribute to the development of Nobelitis. Paul Nurse, who won the Nobel Prize in physiology or medicine in 2001, soon found himself getting daily requests for interviews on all sorts of topics.

"As if suddenly I was acknowledgeable about all workings of the world and of human beings," he told DW.

Nurse had this advice for researchers: "Be careful quite what you say. Talk about things that you do know something about that would be fine. And if you don't know so much or you're not sure about something, be really clear that you're not sure."

Hansson has also noticed that the media eagerly report whatever Nobel laureates say — even when prize winners stray far from their area of expertise.

Nobelitis sufferers are leaders in their fields and rightly honored for their work with the Nobel. But they're also regular individuals, not immune to megalomania and other behavioral problems — meaning that homeopathy, orthomolecular medicine and other pseudosciences don't automatically become credible just because they're backed by a Nobel Prize winner.

#1021. Michael Carrick responds to Man United pressure with impassioned speech

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Carrick spoke for almost six minutes before answering a question as he insisted he is right person to turn Manchester United around

Michael Carrick used a six-minute opening speech to admit that Manchester United have “lost our way” but insisted he still believes he is the right person to turn the club’s fortunes around.

The manager has come under fierce pressure amid a seven-game winless run in all competitions and criticism of his tactics from captain Bruno Fernandes following the 4-1 defeat in Como.

Carrick said that his squad is “not divided” following the criticism from Fernandes, but reiterated the “hurt” he has felt since the “unacceptable” performance on Wednesday.

Carrick will take charge of United against Bournemouth on Sunday and the former midfielder wanted to speak first before being asked a question in his pre-match press conference at Carrington.

He used his address to highlight the importance of “sacrifice”, “humility” and “hard work” in an impassioned speech which implored the “values” of the club and “respecting” the responsibility of representing United.

“I think recently we haven't had a good time, I think that's pretty obvious,” Carrick began. “I totally accept that, what we're going through at the moment. We had five months of relative success in a spell where I was happy with a lot of things that I was seeing and that we were doing as a team.

“We haven't done well enough lately, I think that's pretty obvious to say, and we've had a spell that we're really not happy with. Wednesday was a bad night, it was a bad night for a lot of reasons and it was a bad night that hurt a lot. It hurt me to see us play that way, to see how the game panned out.

“As I said after the game, it was really unacceptable for us to play like that and to be like that. The supporters are a huge part of this football club, for me to see the supporters have to make the effort and the time and spend the money to come and support us on Wednesday and have the feeling after the game, like we all felt, was a tough moment. I think that's pretty obvious.

“My responsibility is to get a team on a pitch that represents this football club, represents the supporters and they can be proud of it with the identity that we show. We have gone away from that just recently in certain games, which I'm totally fully aware of and definitely doing something about. I think it's important that we all recognise that, I certainly recognise it.

“Our supporters are the utmost of importance to me and this football club and for them to feel like they felt for five months at the start of the season, at the start of the year, for us to have that about us and that connection and that real vigour and intensity and enjoyment and passion that we all felt through that period was something that we need to get back to and we will get back to it. I fully believe that but I think it's important that we recognise that we haven't been doing that.

“The foundation of this football club and the values of this football club have been put down and etched for many, many years and I totally understand and I get every single one of them.

"It comes down to humility, it comes down to being humble, it comes down to being hard-working and being really proud to work hard and being proud to live up to their values.

“There's a resilience about this football club that's been shown for a long, long time. Whether that's bouncing back from adversity, whether that's comebacks, whether that's late goals, whether that's success, whether that's sustaining success, there's a resilience in this football club that runs very, very deep.

“I've seen it with my own eyes, I've felt it, I'm aware of some of the individuals and know them closely who have been a big part of that history. I said it after the game, Manchester United is always bigger and always has been, always will be bigger than any single individual. Bigger than me, bigger than any of the players and it always has been.

“I'm fortunate enough to know some players myself and play with some - Ryan Giggs, arguably one of the greatest to play for this football club. It was never about Ryan Giggs, it was about Manchester United. Wayne Rooney was the top goalscorer of this football club.

“His biggest thing was playing for the team, sacrificing for the team and what was good for Manchester United. Sir Alex Ferguson, the best manager of all time, the greatest for me and working with him and his main priority was Manchester United. Going back to Sir Matt [Busby], Sir Bobby [Charlton], the resilience was beyond belief of what they showed and the way they rebuilt this football club.

“Again, it was based on being humble, having humility and hard work and we can never lose that and I won't let us lose that. We've had it for a spell, I feel very strongly about that. I think it inspires me a lot to live up to that and keep driving that with the players and the group that we've shown for a good period of time and that we're capable of it.

“We're in a moment where it's not gone well so we need to show exactly what it means. Respect this football club, respect what it takes to be here, respect what it takes to win, what you have to do to win. I'm not entitled to be here, entitlement cannot be at this football club, we're not entitled to win.

“We've won in the past, we've got an incredible history but that doesn't mean that you're entitled to just win. I'm fully aware of that, it takes hard work, it takes a lot of sacrifice. You've got to invest yourself in the football club and you've got to do a lot of things that maybe aren't so nice but you've got to do it for each other and you've got to win.

“I think it's important we live up to that and it's important for me to push those values and it's important for our supporters to feel that. Moving forward, we've had it, we've kind of lost our way a little bit if I'm totally honest and we'll get it back. I'm totally confident we'll get it back.

“Respect this football club, respect what it takes because it's very, very important to me.”

After then taking questions ahead of Sunday’s game against Bournemouth, Carrick maintained he will “do what is best for this football club” after being pushed over Fernandes’ future as captain.

“The most important thing is the team and the dynamics of the squad and the football club and I always do what is best for the club,” Carrick said. “Whatever the situation is I will do what is best for the football club.

“If I have spoken to him or I haven’t spoken to him, I don’t think this is the time to go into too much detail.”

Carrick also revealed he had held conversations with the United hierarchy following the Como defeat and said it was his responsibility to “hold the umbrella for me to get wet and everyone else to stay dry”.

"It hurts an awful lot but it doesn't stop me,” he added. “That hurt is used as pure motivation and inspiration to want more and work for more and to get us going in that different direction.

“It feels like it can be a long way away but it doesn't take too much to flip the other way and we are more capable of doing that. We will. We will win football matches."
