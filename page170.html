Samsung says its foldable phones are selling like hotcakes, and if you read between the lines, Apple might be one big reason why.

It is possible that's happening with foldables as we speak.

If Apple can convince people to happily spend $2,000 on a smartphone, that would be a really impressive magic trick.

#279. India slashes cost of cancer drugs after court rules mark-ups are ‘extortion’

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Case filed in 2022 by patient unable to afford medicine leads to cap on drug prices for millions who face ruin if diagnosed

The Indian government has moved to reduce the price of cancer drugs after a retired bank clerk took her case to the supreme court.

From next month, 137 cancer drugs in India will be cheaper as the government tries to tackle the tragedy of poorer patients either forgoing treatment, selling land or falling into debt to meet the high cost of some medicines, which are making large profits for hospitals and pharmacists.

Sarojam Radhakrishnan, a former bank employee in Kerala, was diagnosed with breast cancer in 2021. Doctors prescribed life-saving targeted treatments but the monthly cost, of more than 63,000 rupees (£500), was far beyond her family’s means.

Realising that thousands of families were being ruined by the cost of such drugs, Radhakrishnan filed a petition to the Kerala high court in 2022 demanding government intervention to lower the costs.

When the case reached the supreme court, the judges looked at hospitals’ and retailers’ mark-ups on cancer drugs and called the situation “carnage” and “extortion”.

During a hearing on 29 September, justices Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta noted how a life-saving cancer drug supplied to hospitals at 2,700 rupees carried a vastly marked-up price of 27,000 rupees.

The judges found that hospitals, distributors and chemists enjoyed large margins on medicines. Analysis by the National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority revealed a trade mark-up on some cancer drugs averaging 170%, with some extreme examples stretching to more than 700%.

A 30% trade margin cap on the price of 42 drugs has been in place for some years but many others remained off this list. Now, prodded by the supreme court’s findings, the government has imposed the cap on 137 other drugs.

“The measure is expected to reduce medicine prices by up to 70% and help cancer patients save 2,500 crore rupees [£199m] annually, significantly reducing the amount they pay from their own pockets,” a statement from the Department of Pharmaceuticals said.

People on low incomes in India dread illness, as the cost of treatment can mean financial ruin, particularly for cancer patients.

Whenever the Indian Cancer Society holds a free diagnosis camp in Gurgaon where he lives, Krishan Kalra urges local drivers, maids, cooks and tailors to turn up because tests they cannot afford are offered free.

As a former member of the council of the Rajiv Gandhi Cancer Institute and Research Centre, Kalra knows the importance of early diagnosis, but he said very few people attended. “When I ask why, they say they’re too scared – if something serious like cancer is detected, they haven’t got the money to pay for the treatment so they prefer to remain in the dark,” he said.

With the cost of cancer drugs ranging in price from 38,000 to 380,000 rupees, many families quietly walk out of the doctor’s consulting room upon hearing the estimated cost of treatment.

Studies by the Public Health Foundation of India show that spending on healthcare pushes about 55 million Indians a year below the poverty line.

Few Indians can afford the premiums for comprehensive health insurance. The government’s nationwide insurance scheme, Ayushman Bharat, covers treatment for some cancers but it provides only 500,000 rupees’ worth of annual cover for a family – the cost of just one dose of Kadcyla (ado-trastuzumab emtansine), a drug used to treat breast cancer. Patients may need up to 14 doses.

Dr Ravi Kannan is a surgical oncologist and director of Cachar cancer hospital and research centre in Silchar, Assam. He has worked in hospitals elsewhere in India and is familiar with doctors’ trauma of seeing patients walk out to go home and die.

“Some families start the treatment, hoping against hope to manage the cost. They start but they don’t come back for chemotherapy or radiation. They can’t sustain the cost,” he said. “The decision is a welcome step if the outcome is that more patients can access the drugs they need.”

India records about 1.56m new cancer cases annually – up from 1.39m cases in 2020 – making it a significant public health challenge.

As the supreme court delves deeper into the subject, what was clear, said Jyotsna Singh, co-convener of the Working Group on Access to Medicines and Treatments, a civil society network, was that a “total overhaul” of the healthcare system was needed.

“Right from the cost of manufacturing a drug to the point of sale, we need to make the system transparent and regulate the entire supply chain. Only then will these drugs become available to those who need them,” she said.

Singh said companies and hospitals were likely to try to find legal loopholes to wriggle out of the 30% cap, suggesting that hospitals may offset the lower price charged for a drug by raising charges on other items.

The supreme court judges have also asked the government’s lawyers why the cap should apply only to cancer drugs and not the entire range of non-patented drugs. The court has given the government until 27 October to respond.

Unfortunately, the court’s ruling came too late for Radhakrishnan, who died in September 2022 while her case was still pending in Kerala.

#275. Fewer people are flying as airfare soars. Here's why airlines aren't worried

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Fewer people are flying this year, but with airfare this high and climbing, airline executives say that's just fine.

The number of people screened at U.S. checkpoints is on track for its first annual decline since 2020. Major U.S. carriers have said capturing higher fares is more important than fully filling their planes or flying record numbers of travelers.

"We're actively prioritizing yield versus load factor and really happy as we see it. It fits right in with the premium investments that we're making," American Airlines Chief Commercial Officer Nat Pieper said on the company's earnings call Thursday.

American's load factor, a gauge of how full planes are flying, fell to 83.4% in the third quarter from 86% in the same quarter of 2025.

The price of plane tickets has skyrocketed as airlines grapple with a spike in jet fuel prices, their biggest expense after labor, and pass more of those costs on to customers.

Fares in September were up more than 22% from a year earlier, according to the latest inflation read. Fare tracker Hopper said it expects this holiday season to come with the most expensive ticket prices in years.

The average Southwest Airlines fare in the third quarter, for example, was $231.69, up close to $50 from a year earlier. That doesn't include Southwest's new fees, like its extra charges for checked bags or a seat closer to the front of the plane.

The airline's count of revenue passengers, however, fell 3.2% from a year ago, to about 33.5 million people.

Still, both fares and the passenger counts exceeded Southwest's expectations.

"Passenger fares came in about $3 above what our forecast was and the more important thing is that passenger volumes also exceeded our expectations," Southwest CEO Bob Jordan said on an earnings call Thursday.

Since April — when airlines began warning in earnest about profits taking a hit from the spike in jet fuel — through Thursday, about 9.3 million fewer people were screened at airport checkpoints compared with the same period of 2025, according to the Transportation Security Administration. While that's down less than 2% from last year, it follows years of growth.

With jet fuel prices more than double year-ago levels — as seen in U.S. Gulf Coast spot prices going for $4.63 a gallon, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration — airlines have been focused on recovering as much of the fuel spike as possible.

Even despite some drops, airline executives said during their earnings calls that travel demand overall is robust, especially for profitable premium cabins like business class.

And not every carrier is seeing a decline. United Airlines flew 2.4% more passengers in the third quarter than it did a year ago, and said its unit revenue rose by 16%, the biggest increase since the pandemic.

"I've been pleasantly surprised at the industry's ability this year to raise yields and pass through much of the increase in fuel prices," CEO Scott Kirby said on the company's earnings call Wednesday. "So it's possible that airlines will continue to focus on yields at the expense of load factor and that alone could get us to double-digit margins next year."

Most airlines are anticipating that fuel costs will continue to bite, but executives say they're confident in their pricing power.

United, for example, said it expects to pay an average of $4.80 a gallon for fuel in the fourth quarter, up from an average price of $3.90 a gallon in the third.

American, United, Delta Air Lines and Southwest all cut their profit forecasts for 2026 this month when they reported earnings. American is the only one of the four that expects to post a loss this year.

American and others have also reduced growth plans or warned the industry could dial back on its goals for expanding capacity, which could keep fares elevated. Reduced capacity can make bookable seats scarcer, driving up prices even more if demand holds.

The carriers said demand has been particularly strong for premium seats, which command hundreds, if not thousands of dollars, more than coach tickets.

Smaller airlines set to report results in the coming weeks, including Frontier Airlines and Allegiant Air, have traditionally focused on more price-sensitive customers, which could show if those travelers are pulling back at all.

One factor that could also affect air travel is the demise of Spirit Airlines, which went out of business in early May.

"Some of its customers have likely stopped flying," Bank of America said in a research note Thursday.

#273. Can Germany revive its economy before the AfD gains ground?

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Conflicts, rising costs and Chinese competition are testing Germany's economic model like never before. Can new growth sectors create jobs and ease voters' economic fears, thereby helping contain the far-right AfD?

Germany's export-led economic model faces unprecedented strain just as support for the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) party reaches a new peak.

Chinese rivals are steadily eroding the market share of major German industrial players, while persistently high energy prices, elevated labor costs and excessive red tape are adding to the structural crisis.

After two years of contraction, real GDP edged up only 0.2% last year, according to the Federal Statistical Office, Destatis. Exports to China fell 9.7% in 2025, withGerman vehicle and part shipments plunging by a third.

While there are signs the German economy is turning the corner, the government's latest forecast of 1.3% growth in 2026 is still modest compared with the pre-COVID boom years.

The AfD is currently polling ahead of all other political parties in Germany. The country's many economic challenges have been a major factor in the right-wing populists' rise.

Germany's domestic intelligence agency sees reliable indications that the AfD is right-wing extremist in its entirety.

The party has vowed to put Germany back on a growth path by ending the transition to renewable energies, lowering taxes and renegotiating European Union treaties, while also reforming welfare and sharply restricting immigration.

As Chancellor Friedrich Merz's coalition searches for a fresh growth strategy to fend off the rise in populist politics, the government has set out its high-tech agenda focusing on six priority sectors.

These are artificial intelligence (AI), quantum computing, microelectronics, biotechnology, clean energy (including nuclear fusion) and climate-neutral mobility.

Irene Bertschek, head of the Digital Economy research unit at the Leibniz Center for European Economic Research (ZEW), argues that Germany's research in these "key enabling technologies is very strong, particularly in the area of climate-neutral technologies."

Yet, the results are often commercialized abroad, Bertschek told DW, with the United States leading in AI and China closing the gap quickly.

A report published Monday by the Made for Germany initiative — a group of 147 companies and investors — builds on the government's plan, estimating that these and other new technologies could attract up to €4.1 trillion ($4.6 trillion) in investment by 2035.

Realizing that potential, however, will depend less on today's industrial giants, like Volkswagen, Siemens and BASF.

"It will not primarily be the established companies that conquer these new fields, but innovative newcomers — startups," Oliver Falck, director of the ifo Center for the Economics of Innovation and Digital Transformation, told DW.

The task, Falck said, is to create the right conditions for startups to emerge, including proximity to top universities, access to private capital and enough freedom to experiment.

He argues for state financing during the early-stage "valley of death" — the risky phase between research and commercial viability when private financing is often scarce.

There are clear signs this shift is already happening. Data from Deutsche Bank and startupdetector, a German startup database and market intelligence platform, show more than 3,000 startups were founded in Germany in the first half of 2026, up 88% from the same period last year. One-third of the new ventures have an AI focus.

Venture-capital investment soared 44% to €5.8 billion in the first half of 2026, with firms such as Neura Robotics and drone manufacturers Stark and Quantum Systems benefiting.

US investors, meanwhile, are increasingly buying up Germany's famed Mittelstand — its traditional small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) — with an eye on their specialized technology.

"The German Mittelstand is highly innovative. Now it is essential to invest in key technologies such as artificial intelligence and to tap into the potential that arises at the intersection with established technologies," Bertschek stressed.

The expert, who is also chair of the German government's Commission of Experts for Research and Innovation (EFI), thinks SMEs should make better use of their huge amounts of production data and collaborate with AI startups to help those young firms grow.

Another idea for accelerating the shift to new high-tech sectors comes from Sven Gabor Janszky, chairman of the Leipzig-based futures-research institute 2b AHEAD. He argues that Germany needs special economic zones to help catch the next wave of economic growth.

"For a few specific technologies, you lower the regulations, you recruit ambitious workers and pump money into these zones," Janszky told DW.

This, he added, would follow the examples of Silicon Valley and Chinese mega-cities Shenzhen and Shanghai's Pudong district, and echoes the conditions that helped rebuild the German economy after World War II.

In Janszky's view, these zones would help Germany position itself as a first mover in emerging fields rather than remaining tied to older industrial strengths.

"EU regulations may try to prohibit this move, but Germany will have to overcome them so we can grow the economy again," he added.

The sharp rise in Germany's defense spending will also help several of the high-tech sectors now being prioritized.

According to the International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS), the defense budget is expected to reach around €120 billion in 2026 — roughly 2.6% of GDP. The government wants this figure to hit 3.5% by 2029.

The resulting procurement will provide a major boost to the production of drones, sensors, communications systems and other dual-use technologies that have both civilian and military applications.

Yet, with major rival China benefiting from much lower energy and worker costs, along with huge government subsidies, Germany will continue to face similar price disadvantages when exporting its new high-end tech. But that doesn't have to spell failure, said Falck, the ifo expert.

"Take the semiconductor industry as an example. It is probably unrealistic to aim for leadership along the entire value chain. But it is enough to lead at individual choke points," he pointed out.

"And that is exactly where Germany is strong: as a supplier of equipment for state-of-the-art chip fabs and as a supplier to the world's AI gigafactories, with power semiconductors."

In other words, with a first-mover advantage, decades of high-quality production data and a focus on specialized niches, Germany can remain a major force in advanced industrial and engineering technologies.

#272. Turtle Beach Vulcan II XP Review: Clicky Compromise

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Turtle Beach is no stranger to competitive-grade gaming keyboards. In recent years, it has focused on its mid- to high-tier Vulcan range, with the Vulcan II XP as its newest entry.

It has a lot going for it—Titan HS mechanical switches, easy modification and customization options, and a suite of pro-tier gaming features available through its support software. However, while it arrives as a full-size keyboard (NumPad included!), a series of trade-offs and compromises ultimately make it feel very middle-of-the-pack.

Measuring 462 x 153 x 32 millimeters, it looks undeniably sharp thanks to its low profile and floating-key approach, all sitting atop a premium brushed-aluminum top plate. Unfortunately, the first compromise is the disappointing underside: a cheap-feeling basic plastic shell basin. Thankfully, the legs feel sturdy when popped up, adding around 10 mm in height and a comfortable angle to the board.

I also like the included soft foam wrist rest, which snaps to the front underside and adds nice support when gaming or typing. It's not memory foam, though, so I fear it might lose some of that cushion over time. The Vulcan also has integrated media controls on the top right with back, play/pause, and forward buttons, plus a media wheel with a push-to-mute feature.

It’s a $150 wired-only device (Turtle Beach is launching a wireless version, the Vulcan II XP Air for an extra $50) that connects via a detachable 1.8-meter USB-C to USB-A cord. A physical connection means zero input latency, which competitive gamers will benefit from.

The corded approach does make the Vulcan II XP a breeze to set up. Plug it into a Windows PC, and that's it. But you’ll need Turtle Beach’s Swarm II app to get the most out of it, especially given that the default RGB lighting is a blinding rainbow cascade.

Swarm II brings out the best in this keyboard. Visually, it offers 10 built-in customizable light effects—you can adjust brightness, pattern speed, and colors for some templates. They're pretty gimmicky—especially the Snake one—but it makes for a fun showcase. What's cooler is that you can adjust every LED, perfect for the 11th “custom” slot where you can choose colors, speed, and one of six patterns. You can turn the LEDs off entirely, but only through the app.

Beyond the eye candy, Turtle Beach packs in an impressive slate of features. You can remap inputs down to the individual key and store those configurations as one of five onboard profiles—perfect if you want layouts tailored to specific games, or a more work-oriented profile.

Critical to the keyboard’s gaming credentials, it includes a macro manager that lets you create custom inputs and command combos, down to how many milliseconds of input it should take before activating a keypress. It supports dozens of popular games by default, from competitive favorites like Counter-Strike and Overwatch to single-player epics like Skyrim, which have popular modding scenes and can benefit from bespoke controls. It even has presets for office apps and Photoshop, adding to the Vulcan’s overall versatility. You can easily import new layouts, too.

I’ve been testing the version with linear switches—Turtle Beach also offers a model with tactile switches, both available in black or white. If you’re new to mechanical keyboards, the main difference is that linear keys press all the way down smoothly, while tactile ones have a little “bump” of resistance as the internal switch activates, which you can feel as the keystroke registers. It’s ultimately personal preference.

For the most part, this is a joy to type on, with precise spacing between keys, and the keys themselves having a satisfying “clackiness” when fully pressed. The only small issue is that the factory-lubed linear keys are so smooth and silent until you get that clack as they hit the base, that it’s easy to accidentally long-press a key. Until you’re used to that, it can catch you off-guard—not an issue when typing, where the worst is an errant stretch of letters whennnnnnnn pausing to think mid-sentence, but it tripped me up a couple of times in games until I was used to it.

Unfortunately, unlike Turtle Beach’s earlier Vulcan II TKL Pro, the keys here aren’t Hall effect, but they are hot-swappable—another trade-off, exchanging long-term reliability and accuracy for the ability to replace or upgrade keys easily. The board supports three-pin or five-pin mechanical switch keys and makes swapping them easy. The flat keycaps also pop off without trouble, which makes cleaning simple. Turtle Beach claims the switches last 50 million keystrokes, which should be plenty, but only a third of the TKL Pro's 150 million.

In gaming, the Vulcan II XP offers a 1,000-Hz polling rate for a 1-millisecond response time, which is competition-grade but falls short of the higher-end 8,000-Hz keyboards on the market. Hopping back to Swarm II, you can activate its more advanced features, like Easy-Shift, allowing for key duplication—basically putting commonly used features within easier reach—and ReacTap, assigning two commands to the same key, useful for games that require very swift inputs. (This might be banned in some competitive scenes under automation rules, though.)

These features can be a little fiddly to set up and map, but with a little experimentation, you can build layouts and input fields that work exactly as you want or need. I tailored a bunch of controls for Borderlands 3 and was dropping Psychos like never before. A minor annoyance, though: Reverting a specific input while trying to create a custom setup would sometimes revert the entire profile, which meant I had to redo everything.

While the Vulcan II XP offers great performance for the price, I do have concerns about the overall build quality. On the unit I received, the media wheel was inverted by default—turning it counter-clockwise toward the minus symbol turned the volume up. This was even after I updated the board’s firmware through the Swarm II app, and I had to remap the inputs for it to work as intended. It’s good that the Vulcan II XP’s customization is specific and nuanced enough to remap even the dial direction, but I shouldn’t have had to.

I also found that the LEDs below the “0” and “.” keys on the NumPad don’t seem to work properly, glowing a stubborn, glaring white no matter what color or pattern is assigned. I even tried turning off every other key’s light and manually assigning colors to just those—to no avail.

It’s another reason the board feels middle-of-the-road. Like the shiny metallic top plate hiding the cheaper plastic base, the Vulcan II XP dazzles at a glance, but the closer you look, the more flaws and small annoyances are revealed.

For the price, some of those annoyances are forgivable. For anyone who hasn't upgraded to a mechanical keyboard, this is a decent entry without breaking the bank, delivering a lovely typing experience backed up by enough pro-tier gaming features to feel like a real upgrade—but more demanding or professional players will want to aim higher.

#271. 8 books to cure your inevitable ACOTAR hangover

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

With Sarah J. Maas's next installment, A Court of Splintered Harmony, arriving Oct. 27, another ACOTAR book hangover is inevitable. You know the symptoms: an inability to stop thinking about fictional people, an overwhelming urge to reread their best scenes, and the creeping suspicion that no other book will ever make you feel this way again.

Luckily, your next obsession might be waiting on another shelf. From the deadly vampire tournament of The Serpent and the Wings of Night to the dragon-riding chaos of Fourth Wing to other Maas classics, these eight books deliver dangerous worlds, complicated loyalties, and romances worth losing sleep over. Consider them your remedy for impending Prythian withdrawal.

For readers who loved watching a human survive among dangerously powerful immortals.

If Feyre trying to stay alive Under the Mountain had you stressed, meet Oraya. She’s a human raised by a vampire king, which means she’s spent her life surrounded by people who could eat her. Hoping to improve those odds, she enters a deadly tournament and teams up with Raihn, a vampire who's both her competition and an enemy of her father. An inconvenient person to find attractive, really.

Oraya needs help surviving, but trusting the wrong person could kill her — a predicament that should feel familiar to ACOTAR readers. Welcome to The Serpent and the Wings of Night.

This New York Times bestseller began as a self-published novel in 2022 before Bramble picked it up and rereleased it in December 2023, bringing Oraya’s romantic and survival problems to even more readers. And there’s plenty to keep you busy: Five of the six planned main books are out, along with two companion books, though Oraya and Raihn’s central story wraps up in the first two.

For readers who want forbidden attraction and a heroine questioning everything.

Poppy is supposed to save her kingdom, but she's barely allowed to participate in it. Chosen as the Maiden, a sacred figure in her society, she lives under strict religious rules: no friendships, no romantic relationships, and very little freedom while she waits for a ceremony called her Ascension. She secretly trains to fight anyway. Then she meets Hawke, the guard assigned to protect her, and starts wanting a life she’s been told she can’t have.

Their forbidden attraction helped earn From Blood and Ash the romance award in the 2020 Goodreads Choice Awards, where it received roughly 79,000 votes. But Poppy has more to contend with than feelings for her guard: As violent threats to the kingdom grow, she begins questioning the religious authorities who control her future.

If you liked ACOTAR's mix of romance, secrets, and a heroine discovering that the people protecting her might also be holding her back, this belongs on your list.

For readers who want deadly training, simmering hostility, and wings.

Violet Sorrengail wanted to spend her life working with books. Instead, her mother sends her to a military college, where she must convince a dragon to bond with her and avoid being killed by her classmates — a substantial departure from her original career plan. Making matters worse is Xaden Riorson, a powerful wingleader whose family history gives him every reason to hate hers.

Violet also has chronic conditions, a characteristic inspired by Fourth Wing author Rebecca Yarros's own experiences with Ehlers-Danlos syndrome and POTS. "I went to my publisher and said, 'I want her to have what I have,'" Yarros told Elle in 2025. This shapes how Violet approaches the college's deadly challenges, relying on her intelligence to survive situations she can’t simply overpower.

And if you get attached, you’ll have plenty of company. Onyx Storm, the third book in Yarros's series, sold 2.7 million copies in its first week in 2025, according to publisher figures reported by the New York Times. A Prime Video adaptation is also in the works, backed by Michael B. Jordan’s Outlier Society, with Esther McGregor and Emilio Sakraya reportedly cast as Violet and Xaden. (There’s no premiere date yet, so you have time to form your own opinions before the screen versions arrive.)

If you enjoyed watching Feyre work out how to beat opponents much stronger than her in ACOTAR — while developing feelings for someone she has reasons to avoid — there will be plenty here to keep you occupied.

For readers who take the "enemies" part of enemies-to-lovers seriously.

Lara isn't marrying King Aren with hopes of a happy life together. No, she's been trained to infiltrate his kingdom, discover its weaknesses, and help her homeland bring it down. Unfortunately for the mission, spending time with her husband and his people makes her question whether she’s been given the full story.

Important note: There's no actual magic in this world, but an undercover mission disguised as a marriage supplies more than enough trouble. And if you prefer listening to your fictional relationship problems, this one actually started as an audio series. The Bridge Kingdom debuted as an Audible Original in 2018, before Jensen self-published the print and digital editions in 2019.

For readers ready to get attached to another Maas heroine.

If you can't seem to get yourself away from Maas, go back to where her publishing career began. Throne of Glass arrived in 2012, three years before ACOTAR introduced readers to Feyre. It follows Celaena Sardothien, an imprisoned assassin offered a fairly unappealing job opportunity. If she beats a group of thieves, assassins, and other competitors, she'll become the king's champion, serving the ruler she despises in exchange for her eventual freedom. Then competitors start turning up dead, and figuring out what’s happening becomes just as urgent as winning.

Believe it or not, the original inspiration was Cinderella, according to Maas. The story eventually moved away from a direct retelling and grew into a completed eight-book series, including the prequel collection The Assassin's Blade. So if you're looking for another Maas series to occupy your thoughts for several thousand pages, clear some space.

For readers who want more of ACOTAR's sinister fairy-tale atmosphere.

If you miss the creepy woods and unsettling bargains of early ACOTAR, try spending some time with Elspeth Spindle. She has an ancient spirit called the Nightmare living in her head, which is useful when she needs protection and considerably less reassuring when she considers what he wants in return. Joining a quest to cure her kingdom of dark magic puts her alongside a mysterious highwayman, and getting closer to him makes the whole secret-monster-in-her-head situation harder to hide.

Gillig had some appropriately eerie references in mind when she created the Nightmare: the yew tree from Patrick Ness's A Monster Calls and the creature in Henry Fuseli’s painting The Nightmare. But she wanted Elspeth to have some darkness of her own. As she wrote in a 2022 essay for Fantasy Cafe, she envisioned "a maiden who does not deliver the monster, but becomes one herself."

If that sounds like your kind of fairy tale, there's a manageable commitment ahead: One Dark Window and its sequel, Two Twisted Crowns, make up the completed duology.

For readers who want more faerie drama and a prince they can hold a grudge against.

Growing up in Faerie sounds lovely until you're the human everyone looks down on. Jude ended up in this exact situation after a faerie murdered her parents and took her and her sisters home with him. Now she wants to become a knight and secure a place at court, but Prince Cardan and his friends seem determined to make her regret trying. Unfortunately for them, she has no intention of backing off.

Soon, Jude is caught up in spying and a fight over the throne, while her hatred of Cardan develops some...inconvenient complications. If ACOTAR left you wanting more dangerous faeries getting tangled up in each other’s lives, there's plenty of that in The Cruel Prince. Just keep in mind that this is YA: You'll get romantic tension, but don’t expect Nesta and Cassian's approach to resolving it.

Plus, if you like the first book, Jude and Cardan’s main story spans a completed trilogy, so you can go straight into The Wicked King and The Queen of Nothing without waiting for the next installment.

For readers who want more Maas with a change of scenery.

OK, just one more Maas recommendation. House of Earth and Blood is the first book in her Crescent City series, where powerful Fae and ancient grudges coexist with cellphones. Bryce Quinlan is a half-human, half-Fae woman whose life falls apart when a demon murders her closest friends. When similar killings happen again, she teams up with Hunt Athalar, a fallen angel, to find out who's responsible. Working together also gives them time to develop feelings that...complicate the arrangement.

The novel marked her adult debut, though she’d been working on it privately for years alongside ACOTAR and Throne of Glass. There's a whole city of supernatural creatures, power structures, and rules to get acquainted with, but for anyone still attached to Feyre's circle of friends, Bryce's friendships offer a familiar reason to get invested.

#265. American Express is cutting 5% of its workforce to 'prepare for AI'

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

American Express is cutting thousands of employees as the leading credit card company prepares for artificial intelligence to take over more of its internal processes.

Amex is eliminating 5% of its global workforce and taking a $336 million restructuring charge, CEO Stephen Squeri told analysts Friday on the company's third-quarter earnings call. The firm had almost 77,000 employees at the end of 2025.

Chief Financial Officer Christophe Le Caillec told CNBC in an interview that the restructuring was to make American Express "leaner, more agile, and also to prepare for AI."

"You don't want to automate processes that are not optimally structured," Le Caillec said. "So what we're doing here is to reduce layers and increase spans of controls, so that we can automate and take advantage of AI in the coming quarters and the coming years."

The Amex announcement reflects a broader trend across financial services, where banks are working to replace repetitive roles with AI automation. On Thursday, Barclays confirmed plans to trim 5,000 jobs — also roughly 5% of its staff — in another tech-driven cost initiative. The moves come even as the sector is enjoying robust profits from growing consumer spending and resilient credit.

During the analyst call, Squeri said the charge was to improve the company's efficiency.

"We've got good momentum, but to accelerate innovation and win over the next few years, we needed to become [a] more agile and efficient organization," Squeri said.

Squeri devoted a 10-minute section of his prepared remarks to his views on AI, focusing mainly on how it will be used to personalize experiences and offers for Amex cardholders.

While the CEO emphasized the need to meet customers' desires to use AI, including agents, he added that Amex is "not going to let assistants run rogue through our system."

"We need to protect our customers [and] merchants, and we're only going to allow agents to access our systems that are either approved through API or MCP channels," he said, citing two ways to securely connect outside platforms with Amex systems.

Amex posted quarterly results that topped Wall Street profit expectations. Adjusted earnings per share were $4.68, beating the $4.48 average estimate of analysts, according to LSEG.

The company raised its full-year earnings guidance to a range of $18.30 to $18.60 per share, on an unadjusted basis, up from its previous target of $17.30 to $17.90.

That metric was helped by proceeds from Amex's sale of its stake in Global Business Travel Group, part of which went into the restructuring plan, Le Caillec said.

Total revenue rose 9% to $20.06 billion, coming in slightly below Wall Street's $20.12 billion LSEG forecast. Executives attributed part of the spending drag to recent exits from small business co-brand partnerships.

Shares of the card company fell roughly 4% in afternoon trading.

#264. AI-designed virus infects person for the first time

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Would you let an AI-designed virus be injected in your body? Well, at least one individual just did, according to the company behind it.

AI bioscience startup Radical Numerics released a report this week saying a patient received a treatment containing an AI-designed virus. Radical Numerics says this is the first treatment of its kind.

The type of virus that Radical Numerics' AI model, called Omnii-1, created is called a phage, which isn't a new type of virus. Phage therapy is an existing experimental treatment where doctors administer viruses that attack bacteria to a person with a serious bacterial infection. The idea is to kill the bacteria causing the infection without infecting human cells.

Radical Numerics' AI model did design this specific phage to combat the patient's particular bacterial infection, and that AI-created phage was just administered to a person — which is a first.

However, as the New York Times reports, there are some questionable aspects of this trial.

For example, we don't yet know how the trial went. While the trial is ongoing, Radical Numerics won't provide information about the patient or the doctors involved. What's more, Radical Numerics' report wasn't peer-reviewed or published in a scientific journal. Instead, the company simply posted the paper on its website — the same way any company might publish a news update or blog post.

The company's CEO, Eric Nguyen, said the team plans to share more details once they receive consent from the patient. He cited confidentiality agreements as the reason Radical Numerics can't disclose more information at this time.

We might be waiting a while to find out whether the treatment worked. While phage therapy can last anywhere from a couple of days to a few weeks, doctors also have to monitor the patient for months afterward to make sure the infection was actually cured and won't come back.

#262. Ukraine: Life near the front line in Sloviansk

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

As Russian continues to attack Sloviansk in eastern Ukraine, thousands of residents refuse to leave or struggle to find a way out. For many, poverty, family ties and beloved pets make evacuation painfully difficult.

A few days ago, three Russian aerial bombs struck a residential neighborhood on the outskirts of Sloviansk. One hit a five-story apartment building, injuring seven people. Along with neighboring Kramatorsk, Sloviansk is one of the last major towns in the eastern Ukrainian region of Donetsk still under Ukrainian control. Since summer, the city has been a combat zone.

In a courtyard surrounded by damaged apartment blocks, a dusty carpet hangs over the metal frame of a swing set. Tetjana, a pensioner who lives in the building across the street, has brought it out of her rubble-strewn apartment to clean it. She was in her kitchen when the bombs struck.

"The bomb hit the second floor, and a woman who lived on the fourth floor was screaming," she tells DW. "They managed to rescue her before that entire section of the building collapsed," she adds.

Despite being offered the opportunity to evacuate, Tetjana has yet to leave Sloviansk. "I don't know where I would go," she says. Rent can cost up to €400 ($449) a month. Tetjana's pension is the equivalent of just €67 ($75) a month. Even with monthly relocation assistance of about €40 ($45), she could not make ends meet. "I couldn't live on that. Nowhere!"

Meanwhile, electricity and water are still available in her neighborhood, but the gas supply has been cut off. "We'll get through this," Tetjana says hopefully. "Everything will be OK. That's what I tell everyone. I want that so much."

Days after the attack, the few residents remaining in the damaged buildings were still clearing out their apartments. But another attack hit as they worked. This time, the bombs struck a building housing a state-run emergency assistance center and the Red Cross. While firefighters put out the blaze, rescue workers and volunteers retrieved belongings left inside and cleared away broken glass. Eight people were injured in the attack.

"I was on the balcony when there was a deafening bang. It felt as if someone had hit me over the head," says Natalja, describing one of the recent attacks. The pensioner comes to this part of the city to care for her twin sister. "She can no longer get around. Today, I couldn't bring her bread, only crispbread," she tells DW. Although shops and a few stalls remain at the burned-out market, bread is not available every day.

"I tried to persuade my sister to leave. I would go to a nursing home and she would go to a hospital. But her dog is sick, and I have one, too," Natalja says, noting that pets are not allowed during evacuations. "I can't leave my sister behind. She is worried and angry with me, as if everything were my fault. I have no strength left."

Natalja slowly sets off for home, "so I can get there before dark." The city center is nearly 4 kilometers (2.5 miles) away. Public transportation has been suspended since 30 people were killed in an attack on two buses in neighboring Kramatorsk.

The center of Sloviansk is heavily damaged. Few people are out on the streets, though a handful of shops remain open. Of the city's 100,000 residents, about 20,000 remain, most of them elderly people who are unable to leave.

Vasyl, a pensioner, is from the village of Mayaky, north of Sloviansk. A neighbor drove him into the city center, where he sat down for lunch in a cafe. "We're used to living under fire by now," he says. "But it's a bad habit. We should have broken it long ago."

Vasyl wants peace for his homeland. But he rejects Russian President Vladimir Putin's demand that the part of the Donetsk region under Ukrainian control be handed over to Moscow.

Two elderly women talk on a street covered with nets to protect against drones. "I can't remember the last time I slept through the night," says Iryna, a pensioner. "It's terrible, but I have nowhere to go. I'm supposed to leave my two cats behind, but I love them so much. They're all I have."

"We can only hide, trembling, and pray to God that everything will be all right," Iryna continues. "When I have to go to the market, I don't know if I'll make it there alive."

Russian drones attacked the central market several weeks ago, injuring at least three people. Since then, fewer vendors and customers have been coming to the market.

Valeriya sells women's underwear. With kindergartens closed, she has to bring her 5-year-old daughter Sofia to work. Their neighborhood in Sloviansk is not yet subject to the mandatory evacuation of families with children.

"We're used to this life by now. We were evacuated in 2022, but later returned. Now we're waiting to be evacuated again," Valeriya says.

Sofia races through the market aisles with her friend Anya, who is a year older. Anya's mother is a soldier defending her hometown. Her grandmother, Tetjana, sits nearby selling men's clothing. She, too, has to look after her granddaughter while working.

Tetjana has started shipping her belongings to the Dnipropetrovsk region, where she hopes to start a new life. She is sending them while the post office is still open. "It hurts to see our Ukraine being destroyed," she says, fighting back tears. "Who will rebuild our cities?"

She hopes to return one day. But the fate of Bakhmut weighs on her mind. "Look at Bakhmut. There are no houses left. I'm counting on our boys to hold our cities, even though it's very difficult." Bakhmut, about 50 kilometers (31 miles) from Sloviansk, fell to Russian forces in 2023 after months of heavy fighting.

The women see little hope for a ceasefire. "Putin won't want one. And our younger generation will ask us what we fought for in the first place," Tetjana says. She believes Russia must withdraw from Ukrainian territory.

Drones buzz overhead as gunfire echoes across the market. "It's hard for our boys to shoot them down," Tetjana says. "But they're doing their best."
