#20. Controversial US-backed vaccine trial in African newborns halted

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Hepatitis B study in Guinea-Bissau that was to be funded by CDC and that drew global backlash ‘will not move forward’

The US-backed hepatitis B vaccine trial in African newborns that drew global backlash was halted, the lead researcher said on Wednesday.

It was “now clear” that the study “will not move forward in Guinea-Bissau”, Frederik Schaltz-Buchholzer, a Danish researcher, wrote on LinkedIn, adding, “we were not given the necessary governmental approval.”

The minister of health in Guinea-Bissau, Quinhin Nantote, previously denied that the project would move forward. “This is not even remotely true,” Nantote told the Guardian in Portuguese last week.

The study was to be funded with $1.6m from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), along with another $1.6m from private partners like the Bill Ackman-backed Pershing Square Foundation and the Bluebell Foundation.

“The solution is clear here: take the money and vaccinate as many kids as you can,” said Paul Offit, a vaccines expert at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia, who sharply criticized the proposed study as unethical.

The government of Guinea-Bissau now has control over the researchers’ vaccines, which they apprehended when they searched the offices of the Bandim Health Project on Monday.

It was vital to support Guinea-Bissau in rolling the immunizations out to all newborns as soon as possible, Offit said. Guinea-Bissau currently plans to offer the universal birth dose in 2028.

The Danish researchers, led by Schaltz-Buchholzer, Christine Stabell Benn and Peter Aaby, hypothesized giving live vaccines, like the Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG), may have an overall mortality benefit beyond simply preventing illnesses like tuberculosis – but giving another vaccine like hepatitis B alongside it could dampen that effect.

Such a hypothesis was “fanciful” because the hepatitis B vaccine is a single protein among billions encountered by the immune system each day, Offit said.

“There’s no evidence for that, and it doesn’t make biological sense,” he continued. He also added that there was a very clear benefit to giving the vaccine to babies as soon as possible, because of high rates of hepatitis B in the country and the possibility of lifelong illness and death from infection.

The Danish researchers did not respond by press time to questions about the study or to this criticism. The CDC did not respond by press time to questions about whether the funding, which was appropriated through its global health work, would now be used to roll out the vaccine to all newborns in Guinea-Bissau, as recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO).

“WHO recommends that all infants receive a first dose of the hepatitis B vaccine as soon as possible after birth – preferably within 24 hours – followed by at least two additional doses,” said Tarik Jašarević, a WHO spokesperson. “WHO remains committed to working with national authorities, researchers, and partners to ensure that all newborns – in Guinea‑Bissau and worldwide – receive timely, evidence‑based protection against hepatitis B.”

Those who declined participation in the study would not have received the vaccines – making true informed consent impossible, experts say.

“Imagine you’re a mother. You want to do the best for your child,” said Henrik Støvring, a Danish researcher who has co-published research raising questions about previous research from the Bandim project.

“If they decide to participate in the trial, they have a 50% chance of getting the vaccine for their children. But if they stay out, they have a 0% chance of getting the vaccine. So how can you say that’s a non-coercive informed consent?” Støvring asked.

The proposed trial led to distrust among Bissau-Guineans, who wondered if the vaccine – which has a decades-long record of being safe and effective – was being tested on Africans because it was dangerous, Schaltz-Buchholzer said in his post.

It “should be clear” that both birth vaccination schedules – with or without hepatitis B – are not “dangerous to anyone”, he wrote. There is a clear benefit to giving hepatitis B vaccines at birth, and not doing so can lead to lifelong danger, experts say.

Conducting a study like this “makes the case that there’s something that’s worthy of study, for which they have no evidence”, Offit said.

“We have millions and millions, arguably hundreds of millions, of children in this world who have gotten that vaccine as a birth dose or haven’t. If there was any evidence that this was problematic, you would know this by now,” Offit added. “We eliminated hepatitis B in the United States in children virtually within years of starting that program, so the benefits are clear.”

Schaltz-Buchholzer also cast doubt on the effectiveness and safety of the hepatitis B vaccine at birth, noting that he believes the study was canceled because it challenges “prevailing medical assumptions, such as that HBV0 does not have non-specific effects”. The study, in addition to looking at mortality, would have also examined side-effects such as skin conditions and neurodevelopmental conditions. Dozens of studies have shown no link between vaccination and autism.

“All newborns should be given a birth dose of hepatitis B vaccine,” Offit said. “Guinea-Bissau is a country that suffers a high incidence of chronic hepatitis B. That country, more than any other country, arguably, needs to launch this program.”

#17. Carta and NewView partner, hoping to entice more startups to let employees sell shares early

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Startups are staying private longer, leaving employees, who typically take a portion of their compensation as stock they can’t sell, with little hope of early liquidity.

An increasing number of late-stage startups are recognizing this and offering tender offers to employees. These are transactions that allow employees (and, often, early investors) to sell a portion of their shares for a predetermined price to an approved investor.

To simplify the tender process, NewView recently partnered with Carta. The collaboration combines NewView’s secondary deal-structuring experience with Carta’s equity platform. Ravi Viswanathan, founder and managing partner of NewView, pitches this as a way to help startups launch and manage liquidity programs more seamlessly.

“The hope is that this partnership will make it easier for CEOs and companies to think about tenders,” he said. He wants founders to think about giving employees early liquidity as a “win-win-win” for workers, companies, and investors alike.

A growing number of startups seem to agree. Over the past three years, tender offers are projected to increase threefold for Series C and later-stage startups, according to a recent study from NewView Capital and Carta. Plus, the startups that do choose tender offers are no longer just the most valuable private companies, like OpenAI, Databricks, or Anduril, according to Viswanathan.

For instance, last year, his firm, which specializes in secondary deals along with typical venture investments, led a tender for Hightouch at a $1.3 billion valuation, when the marketing customization startup was seven years old.

However, Carta and NewView also found that the overall share of startups launching these offerings remains relatively small, with just 44 executed in the first half of 2026. So Viswanathan wants to encourage other startups to consider tender offers, calling them excellent retention tools and morale boosters.

A decade ago, being a unicorn was enough to attract talent, Viswanathan said, but with so many billion-dollar companies around today, that status may not be as much of a draw now. Offering a tender, he said, “is an opportunity to say you will also get liquidity in your journey with us.”

Some of the companies that recently held tender offers include AI customer support startup Decagon, self-driving tech developer Wayve, and AI voice generator ElevenLabs, which executed two such offerings in less than a year.

“The companies are staying private for 12 to 15 years. That’s not changing,” Viswanathan said. “In that interim, so that we don’t have this talent drain from high-quality venture-backed companies into their public counterparts, we need to make sure that there is liquidity for the employees.”

#16. Seven shot dead by gunmen at church funeral in Jordan, officials say

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Between 150 and 200 people were in the church at the time of the shooting, according to one witness

Seven people have been killed in an attack on a funeral in central Jordan, officials have said.

At least 34 others were injured as gunmen opened fire on a church in Hmoud, in the Karak governorate on Friday.

The unidentified gunmen are reported to have arrived in a car, entered the church and opened fire before fleeing. No-one has admitted carrying out the attack.

In a news conference, Interior Minister Mazen al-Faraya said authorities were working to apprehend the perpetrators which he described as a "group of extremists".

Jordan's King Abdullah II, said the authorities would "confront the terrorist act that targeted Karak with the harshest penalties".

Initial reports indicated seven people had been killed in the attack. A government spokesperson later said six people had died, before officials again revised the death toll to seven.

CCTV footage, verified by the BBC, appears to shows three gunmen making their way from a road toward the church.

Two of the attackers are seen making their way on to what appears to be a vehicle entrance to the church grounds, before retreating briefly and returning with a third man. All three appear to be armed with handguns.

Separate footage believed to be from the aftermath of the attack appears to show several dead and wounded bodies on the ground amid some chaotic scenes.

Security officials and members of the public gathered outside the Greek Orthodox church on Friday

Witnesses have described scenes of panic inside the church as people threw themselves under pews searching for cover, moving away from the windows.

Prayers and screams could be heard throughout the building as some tried to flee.

Khalid al-Mdanat was at the church and told BBC Arabic the attack lasted around 15 minutes with "people trying to flee while the shooting was still active".

"People began pushing and throwing themselves to the ground," he said.

"Some were taking cover, some were cradling the dead, others were holding their sons close, some were grabbing their children and fleeing, while others were holding onto their friends who were with them at the church."

He said the gunmen left through the church doors and fled in a pickup truck "firing as they went".

Another witness said: "I heard something as if a war had just started.

"I thought it was an electrical short circuit. I looked, people lying on the ground, wounded. The scene was catastrophic."

A woman in the church said explosions rang out seconds after a prayer.

"The sound of machine guns started, and the sound of glass. We began to realise that something was happening.

The witness described how the situation "continued to escalate for 10 continuous minutes". Once she ran out the church she said "bodies were all in the courtyards".

Al-Faraya has described the incident as "painful and horrific" and "unparalleled in the history of the Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan".

There was a heavy security presence at the entrance to a hospital treating people injured in the incident in Karak, amid an atmosphere charged with anger among residents of the governorate, particularly the families of those injured and killed.

Many looked distressed as roads were closed around the facility and people were forced to park their cars and continue on foot.

Security measures were also tightened ahead of the visit by King Abdullah II to check on the injured.

The atmosphere around the hospital remained tense as the sound of crying and shouting was heard as families mourned their loved ones.

Many people appear shocked, frightened and angry. Some expressed frustration at what they described as "a failure to take a tougher approach towards terrorists".

Karak has a sizeable Christian community in the majority-Muslim Jordan.

Such violence is rare in the country, but deadly attacks have been carried out in the past.

Lebanon's President Joseph Aoun, a Christian head of state, said the "heinous terrorist attack" on the church "violated the sanctity of death and funeral mourners, contradicts all religious and humanitarian values," according to Lebanese media.

OpenAI is now training its most advanced AI models without internet access following a string of rogue hacking incidents. In a blog post on Friday, OpenAI says it built an offline testing environment where AI models can interact with indexed and cached web content “without risk of affecting external systems.”

OpenAI paused training its frontier AI models in September, after disclosing several incidents in which its AI agents broke out of their testing environments to hack third-party companies and government agencies, or swarm obscure websites. On October 1st, OpenAI resumed reinforcement learning training and internal inference with tighter security measures, the blog post says.

Even with these safeguards in place and no internet access, OpenAI found that agents “do not always need a network connection in order to communicate.” One test performed by the company asked AI models on separate virtual machines to exchange secrets without a network connection. To bypass this restriction, the AI agents found a way to communicate using the processor’s cache:

Reading data from the cache is faster than retrieving it from main memory, and the agents used this timing difference to communicate. One agent encoded information by evicting selected data from the cache. Another timed how long it took to reload that data, using the difference between fast and slow reads to recover the message. Once the agents agreed on a protocol for encoding and decoding these signals, they could exchange the synthetic secrets without changing a shared file or opening a network connection.

OpenAI says it has added checks to its monitoring system to detect this type of communication. It will also continue training its frontier AI models without a network connection until improved safeguards “justify broader internet access.”

In the meantime, OpenAI says it will work to improve the security of its virtual machines, while testing for weaknesses in its testing environments. It’s also going to continue testing its monitoring systems, which were recently undermined by its AI agents during internal coding projects.

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

41-year-old set on ‘dream to be able to race in Africa’

Rwanda to host despite criticism of human rights record

Lewis Hamilton has said he hopes to remain racing in Formula One until he can take part in the African Grand Prix which is set to take place in Rwanda in the 2030s. The seven-time world champion believes it is right that F1 is to hold a race there despite criticism of the country’s human rights record.

Last week F1 confirmed Rwanda was to host the African Grand Prix having concluded a 10-year agreement for the meeting on a purpose-built circuit, with the first race set for the early 2030s. It is lined up to be held on a circuit in Bugesera, 25 miles south of the capital, Kigali, which has yet to be constructed and marks a return to the African continent for the first time since it last held a race at Kyalami, South Africa in 1993.

Hamilton has been championing a race in Africa for some time. The 41-year-old is understood to be currently contracted with Ferrari until the end of the 2027 season, and speaking before this weekend’s US Grand Prix he said he would like to continue racing for at least another four years in order to make it to the meeting in Rwanda.

“I’m now forced to have to stay a lot longer because we have a race in Africa and that’s been my dream to be able to race in Africa,” he said. “So, I have to keep fighting to try and stay fit if I can. If I can still punch out good results over the next year, we’ll keep it going.”

Hamilton would be at least in his mid-40s if he was to continue until 2030. F1’s current oldest driver is 45-year-old Fernando Alonso.

F1’s deal with Rwanda is for a 10-year period but because of the country’s human rights record the award of the race has been criticised as an act of sportswashing.

Kenya has recorded a second Ebola death, the Health Ministry said on Friday.

Officials said the 65-year-old woman had developed symptoms during quarantine and was a close relative of a Kenyan man who died of Ebola earlier this month.

"Sadly, she succumbed to ⁠the ​disease early ​this evening," the ministry said.

Of some 135 who were identified as contacts with the patient, 118 were placed in quarantine, with eight testing positive. They included four health workers and four close family members.

The latest death is believed to have been among those quarantined family members. The ministry said she developed symptoms on October 13.

The remaining seven cases are all within "one cluster, with no evidence of community spread", the ministry added.

Product-market fit — when a startup’s product sells well — has become even harder to find in this AI era. Despite startups achieving record-fast growth, not all customers are sticky and products can be replicated more easily than ever.

Anu Bharadwaj, a partner at Iconiq; Rajeev Dham, a partner at Sapphire Ventures; and Rahul Vohra, the founder of Superhuman Mail, discussed the new rules for product-market fit last week at TechCrunch Disrupt.

“It’s never been easier to gain a bunch of pilots because lots of companies across the board are open to trying new things,” Bharadwaj said to the audience. “But going from pilot to production is really the hard thing.”

Right now, she said, a lot of founders pitch revenue metrics to investors without specifying which revenue they are talking about. “Is it true ARR or is it run rate?” she said. “There’s many ways to obfuscate the whole thing.”

Lately, much chatter on X has focused on AI founders inflating ARR numbers (which could mean “annualized run rate” or the more solid “annual recurring revenue”) to make it seem like their companies are doing better than they are. A common gimmick is counting pilot customers, who might only use the product for a couple of months, in the annualized run rate revenue number, implying that income is expected to continue for the entire year.

It should be obvious, but Bharadwaj pointed out, “It’s definitely in your long-term interest that you be very honest and upfront about what quality of revenue this is.”

She said companies need to separate hype from reality when looking at the quality of their revenue. It’s a red flag, for example, if only a few customers make up much of a company’s revenue or if the customers can easily go to another vendor for a better price. “Churn rate and retention [are] really dependent on that,” she continued.

That’s why Vohra advises startups to track customer engagement and dollar retention, especially for consumer products. “Figure out what benchmark [is] good, great, and excellent for your space,” he said.

For example, at Superhuman, he said that the company tracked ARR but also had their own internal metric called “delighted ARR,” which, he said, “was the ARR only from customers who are super engaged with the product.” He said that metric helped them see how sticky the customers were and how long they were likely to stay around.

“Those users are now still users, those companies are now still customers,” he continued. “We obsessed over serving that particular category of our user base and our customer base.”

The panelists also discussed green flags that indicate a startup has indeed found product-market fit. For Vohra, he said talk to customers to understand what value they find in the product and how they would feel if they could no longer use it. Then be honest with yourself when measuring those responses.

“If you’re 15%, 14%, 13%, or below, consider a complete pivot. Take the codebase, take the team, build something entirely new. “But if you’re 20%, you can totally optimize your way from 20% to 40%,” Vohra said.

Actually, Dham said, it’s important for a founder not to confuse having a vision with having a good business.

At the seed level especially, Barudvaj said that it’s fine to pivot. She doesn’t even look for product-market fit at that stage, but “does the founder have a thesis that seems plausible and do they have the agility to duck and wave as the situation changes?” she said.

Dham added that he values founders even more these days who have made numerous pivots because it shows they’ve had to make very hard decisions in this ever-shifting moment.

Product-market fit, therefore, is something founders will always have to redefine and re-earn. “Even at a billion in revenue, these companies are re-earning their product-market fit,” he said. “You constantly have to be innovating on products.”
