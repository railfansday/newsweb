#911. Why 36-year-old Jordan Henderson works in Xabi Alonso’s new-age Chelsea

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Please refresh the page or navigate to another page on the site to be automatically logged inPlease refresh your browser to be logged in

Henderson was expected to play a role that wouldn’t go much further than dressing room leader but has formed an unlikely midfield partnership with Moises Caicedo that Alonso has no reason to change

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

When a 36-year-old Jordan Henderson followed a 35-year-old Danny Welbeck through the door at Stamford Bridge in early August, it seemed Chelsea had overcorrected from their chaos of youth. The ageing pair had impressed for two thirds of the Premier League’s so-called “Bermuda Triangle” - Brentford and Brighton respectively - but there was hardly the impression they were signed as starters, more dressing room figures.

Barring a 71-minute cameo in the damning 3-0 loss at the Bees, Welbeck’s starts have been limited to the Carabao Cup. Henderson, on the other hand, finds himself towards the pinnacle of the pecking order in the Premier League, the priority competition, forming an unlikely partnership with the indispensable Moises Caicedo in the centre of midfield. Ahead of young guns Romeo Lavia and Valentin Barco, he has started and played the entirety of their last three outings and going into this Saturday’s derby clash with Tottenham, Xabi Alonso has no reason to drop him.

Alonso feels the drop-off from prime Henderson, the Liverpool captain that lifted the Premier League and Champions League, to now is miniscule. It’s why he disregards his age as just number. “He's 36, but he's in a very good shape physically,” the Spaniard says. “He has a lot of energy, but that hasn't changed. I see he's 36, but you tell me he's 28 when he was playing in Liverpool, and he was pressing and following the press and wanting the ball and having this impact, having this influence in the rest of the team. [That] hasn't changed that much.

“He did that in Brentford, and that's why we thought that he could work in that position with the team that we had. Now he's feeling well, he's feeling important, and he's having a very positive influence. That's what we wanted, that’s [what] we are getting that from Jordan, and he's enjoying his game.”

Henderson’s famed engine is no myth. In Chelsea’s last two games, the Englishman has covered more ground that any other player- 11.9km against Bournemouth and 12km against Everton - despite being the oldest on the pitch on both occasions.

Yet he’s not just running. Against the Cherries, he bagged an excellent brace - including the first direct free-kick goal of his career - as well as an assist.

Attacking output is appreciated even if it’s not what’s keeping Henderson in the team. “He's playing well, not for the goals he has, but for the impact that he's having in the team,” Alonso added.

Part of this impact relates to Henderson’s role in maturing what was an ill-disciplined and over-emotional squad when the Spaniard arrived. Alonso said he was aware of Chelsea’s horrific disciplinary record last season; they received eight red cards, four more than the next highest team in the Premier League, while also ranking second for yellow cards with 90 cautions to their name. “We could do better,” Alonso declared.

This term, there has been a noticeable change. Chelsea are committing fewer fouls per 90 and receiving fewer yellow cards. No players have been sent off.

Henderson’s presence is credited with contributing to this shift. “Absolutely, I think it helps,” said Alonso. “It helps for that experience and that composure that you need in certain moments. You have players that sometimes, even during the game, they take a step back to see, this is happening and now we need to keep focused and calm.”

Managing emotions will be imperative for Saturday’s visit of rivals Spurs. His first home London derby after being on hostile territory against Fulham and Arsenal, Alonso is expecting an “electric” atmosphere. His side’s newfound discipline will now be put to the test in their own cauldron.

“For me it comes a lot with emotional control, with the control of the game,” Alonso explained. “I think that if you are focused on what you have to do, you are prepared for what's going on. Normally you don't make that many mistakes and you don't overreact to certain situations. For me this is a fundamental part of the game, to have that emotional control, to deal with all this. The momentum is coming, [but] we keep calm, I don't lose my head and I don't get stupid yellow cards, make stupid penalties. You need to prepare for football to have better control of that.”

Henderson will be entrusted to keep his head more than anyone, though Alonso’s new-age Chelsea are without another elder statesman signed in the summer. Emi Martinez’s sickening head injury suffered against Everton will leave him sidelined for a “few weeks” after undergoing surgery. It means highly-rated understudy Mike Penders, 21, is set for an extended run in the team.

“If you know Mike, he's very calm, very mature for his age,” Alonso asserted, confident he can handle the pressure. The last young Belgian to stand in between the sticks for Chelsea was one Thibaut Courtois. His first Stamford Bridge appearance also came at the age of 21, albeit while on loan at Atletico Madrid. That night he played a part in denying the Blues a place in the 2014 Champions League final, maybe the most high-pressured Chelsea arrival possible.

Alonso spoke to Courtois at the World Cup explicitly to get his assessment of Penders. “He even said that he could see resemblances with Mike [and himself],” Alonso recalled. That’s a good sign.

#907. World Cup 2034: Does Saudi Arabia shelving Neom Stadium spell trouble?

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Since winning the right to hold the 2034 World Cup unopposed, Saudi Arabia has been pulling away from sport. With reports that its most ambitious stadium project will be abandoned, what does this mean for the World Cup?

It was billed as "the most unique stadium in the world," a key pillar of Saudi Arabia's World Cup 2034 plans. But now Neom Stadium, part of the kingdom's ambitious aims to build a 170-kilometer-long (111-mile) city in the desert known as The Line, looks set to be scrapped, with the broader project scaled back significantly.

A recent Reuters report said the stadium, which according to the country's bid book was going to be "350 meters (1,148 feet) above ground" and have "stunning vistas, and a roof created from the city itself," would no longer be built for the tournament, where it was to host a quarterfinal and several other games.

Observers of the gulf country saw this coming.

"When I was in Saudi Arabia in December there were already plenty of signals that the whole Neom project was being put on the back burner, if not abandoned altogether," F. Gregory Gause III, a regional expert from the Middle East Institute think tank in Washington D.C., told DW.

"I see it as part of a general reevaluation of their fiscal situation, both in light of the oil market before the current war and in light of the damage that the war is doing. The drawback from sports commitments is just part of a more general reassessment of priorities in the current, more straitened, situation."

Saudi Arabia's sports splurge that saw players like Cristiano Ronaldo join the country's football league and the kingdom invest in all sorts of sports in myriad ways has slowed dramatically. The country pulled funding for the multibillion dollar LIV Golf as well as events in snooker, tennis and rugby and also withdrew from ambitious plans to host the 2029 Asian Winter Games in Neom.

Sports had formed a key part of Saudi Arabia's Project 2030, which aimed to diversify the country's economy away from oil and increase its soft power. But Yasir Al-Rumayyan, the governor of the Saudi-government operated Public Investment Fund (PIF), which also owns Premier League football club Newcastle United, said earlier this year that the PIF was "reassessing its priorities" amid war in the region.

"The impact of the war has not only made the economic rationalization more important, but it's also accelerated the desire to rethink some of these projects in the sense that Neom is now being repurposed as an industrial center on the Red Sea, which has obviously assumed much greater urgency since the Strait of Hormuz was effectively blocked," Kristian Coates Ulrichsen, author of "Kingdom of Football: Saudi Arabia and the Remaking of World Soccer," told DW.

There are, Coates Ulrichsen continued, clear parallels with the 2022 World Cup in Qatar, where plans to build 12 stadiums resulted in only eight being used at the tournament. The Arab Spring uprising arrived shortly after the bid was accepted by FIFA and, though it didn't impact the country directly, it destabilized the region both politically and economically.

Neither Saudi Arabia's World Cup organizing authority or Sports Ministry has responded to questions from DW about whether we may again see fewer stadiums than the planned 15 for the 2034 World Cup, which could feature 64 teams.

"We're seeing the same obviously with Saudi Arabia now," said Coates Ulrichsen, who is also a fellow for the Middle East at Rice University in the US.

"[FIFA President] Gianni Infantino announced Saudi would be the only bidder on the 4th of October 2023. Three days later was October 7th, so within three days, the region was effectively in flames," he said.

Since then, there has been the Israel-US war against Iran, while Houthi attacks continue to hit Saudi Arabia as part of the conflict in Yemen, meaning that: "This whole hosting cycle is taking place against a backdrop of considerable uncertainty and kind of real tension."

But though the scope and cost of it may be reduced, Saudi Arabia's softening commitment to sport will not likely apply so firmly to the biggest sporting event in the world, said Gause.

"I think it continues to be a major priority, because of its global importance and because the Saudis will want to do at least as well, if not outshine, Qatar's hosting earlier."

Relations between Saudi Arabia and Qatar have improved since an agreement in 2021 but the pair remain regional rivals, something Coates Ulrichsen agrees will spur the 2034 hosts on.

As such, the 2034 tournament will still likely be a high-end affair, with Saudi Arabia building most stadiums from scratch. But, said Coates Ulrichsen, it may look somewhat different to what was planned.

"The fact that at least the Saudis were able to host a big event with 60,000 people in the stadiums, full stadiums without any security concerns [the Gulf Cup football final which they won this week] is a good sign," he said.

The regional expert thinks more matches could end up being hosted in the two biggest Saudi cities, Riyadh and Jeddah. This may be at the expense of some venues nearer the east coast such as Al Khobar or Al Ahsa. These are separated from Iran by the Persian Gulf, which includes the Strait of Hormuz, and could be reconsidered depending on the state of political affairs.

Saudi Arabia host football's Asian Cup in January, which Coates Ulrichsen thinks will be an "early test of whether that is viable to have a weeks-long major tournament under these conditions."

With nearly eight years until the World Cup, the political and economic situation in the country is difficult to predict. While the supposed shelving of Neom Stadium and general Saudi sporting withdrawal poses questions of commitment more broadly, it appears the World Cup show will go on. But probably not quite as billed.

#905. Bose goes more budget-friendly and acquires Skullcandy following a years-long partnership

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Skullcandy's products will no longer just feature Sound by Bose — they'll be an official part of the Bose brand, too.

Following the collaboration between the two brands that brought Bose-engineered audio and tech to the budget brand's products, Bose announced an acquisition of Skullcandy on Oct. 21.

This comes just over a year and a half after the release of the Skullcandy Method 360 ANC earbuds, the first pair of Skullcandy buds to come with the Sound by Bose designation. In my testing of those earbuds, priced at $99, I found they featured an impressive soundscape for the price point that felt worthy of the Bose name. While not exactly like a pair of Bose earbuds, they did feel like an elevation over what I expected sonically from Skullcandy.

The Skullcandy brand will remain its own distinct brand under Bose, with the headquarters remaining in Park City, Utah. The press release announcing the acquisition detailed that Bose wanted to retain "Skullcandy’s design, understanding of its consumers, and authentic community connections," as it helped create new products and growth for the brand, providing "new ways for Bose to meaningfully connect with consumers where it has less presence today."

In other words, the success of the collaboration between the two brands seems to have offered Bose the opportunity to go more aggressively after the budget consumer market, a corner that Skullcandy has been firmly situated in for years. For comparison, Skullcandy's cheapest earbuds, a pair of wired earbuds, go for $14.99. Bose's cheapest earbuds, the new wired ANC buds, go for $99.99. Wireless- and ANC-wise, Skullcandy's buds start at $104.99 while Bose's most affordable pair clocks in at $179.99.

The writing was on the wall with Bose acquiring Skullcandy, especially considering their latest Skullcandy collab, the Crusher 1080 headphones. Released just this past July, the press release called these headphones the "most advanced headphone in Skullcandy's history," powered with Sound by Bose, Bose QuietControl 6-mic adaptive noise cancellation, Bose TrueSpatial with head tracking, and the Bose WaveForm Audio Engine.

The president of Bose Audio Technology and Chief Strategy Officer Nick Smith said the collab was "an exciting example of how Bose innovation can reach more listeners through distinctive new products and experiences," which sounds like a line that could've easily fit in the acquisition press release.

This purchase of Skullcandy is Bose's third acquisition of the year, though the first of a consumer audio brand. On Oct. 1, Bose acquired Firelight Technologies, the maker of an interactive audio software program. Earlier this year, Bose bought StreamUnlimited Engineering GmbH, a connected audio platform.
