#118. Uganda says it has no active Ebola cases, but infections in Kenya and Nigeria are prompting fresh scrutiny

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Health officials are investigating whether Ebola patients in Kenya and Nigeria crossed paths at Uganda's main airport as conflicting accounts cloud the source of one infection.

The first confirmed Ebola patients in Kenya and Nigeria may have crossed paths at Uganda's Entebbe International Airport, according to African health officials, as new information from Switzerland contradicts Uganda's account of the Nigerian patient's travel history.

Uganda, a country reporting no active Ebola infections, has become a focus of investigations into cases thousands of kilometers apart.

Uganda insists that it has no confirmed active cases of the deadly virus. Yet health authorities in Kenya and Nigeria are investigating Ebola infections involving people whose travel histories or contacts are connected to the East African nation.

Both patients passed through Entebbe International Airport on October 3, according to Wessam Mankoula, head of emergency preparedness and response at the Africa Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (Africa CDC).

Health officials say the two may have crossed paths, although it has not been established whether they encountered each other or whether any transmission occurred at the airport.

The differing accounts have raised questions about whether Uganda was simply a transit point for infected travelers. Or could infections be going undetected along some of Africa's busiest cross-border routes?

The latest developments come after Switzerland disputed claims that the Nigerian patient had visited the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), where an Ebola outbreak is underway.

Nigerian Health Minister Muhammad Ali Pate confirmed an Ebola case in the capital, Abuja, involving a woman who had recently traveled to East Africa, including Uganda.

"Today we are confirming that there has been one imported case of Ebola virus disease in the Federal Capital Territory," Pate said.

The woman developed symptoms on October 7 and visited a private health facility on October 11. Further laboratory testing confirmed Ebola, and she was subsequently isolated for treatment.

Pate said the patient was stable and health officials had identified 44 contacts, including health care workers, caregivers, patients and people who may have encountered her during her travels.

But the woman's journey has become central to the investigation.

Speaking on Thursday, Mankoula said the first confirmed patients in Kenya and Nigeria were both known to have traveled through Entebbe International Airport on October 3. Investigators are examining whether the two patients were at the airport at the same time and whether their movements overlapped.

Uganda's Health Ministry says preliminary findings indicate that the Nigerian patient traveled to eastern DRCfor business before passing through Uganda without symptoms and returning to Nigeria.

"Travel through Uganda does not establish that infection occurred here. The location of exposure remains under investigation," the ministry said in an October 20 statement.

But, on Friday, Switzerland's Federal Department of Foreign Affairs directly disputed the account.

"She and her husband did not travel to the Democratic Republic of the Congo," the department said in a statement to Reuters news agency.

The woman, identified by Swiss authorities as a Swiss national of Ugandan origin, is married to a Swiss diplomat based in Abuja.

On Thursday, the Swiss government told Reuters that she was believed to have contracted Ebola while traveling with her husband in Uganda, although that assessment has not been independently confirmed.

The World Health Organization (WHO) and the Africa CDC also said Thursday that they had not received confirmed information establishing that the woman had traveled to the DRC.

The conflicting accounts raise fresh questions about where the woman was exposed to the virus and how accurately her movements were initially reconstructed. They do not, however, establish that she contracted Ebola in Uganda.

Uganda maintains that it has no confirmed active Ebola cases and says it is awaiting more detailed information from Nigeria about the woman's movements.

In Kenya, Uganda has emerged in a separate investigation involving the country's first confirmed Ebola patient, who died in the Kenyan capital, Nairobi, on October 5. The patient had traveled from the Democratic Republic of Congo through Uganda before reaching Kenya.

Health officials have now established that the patient passed through Entebbe International Airport on October 3, the same day as the woman subsequently diagnosed in Nigeria. Kenyan authorities have since reported additional infections linked to the initial case.

But one aspect of the investigation remains unresolved.

According to Kenya's Daily Nation, health officials are searching for a Ugandan national identified as a contact of the deceased patient.

"We know him (the Ugandan) by name and passport number and from his phone but we are not able to reach him," Kenya's director-general for health, Patrick Amoth, told the newspaper.

The Daily Nation reported that the man was using a South Sudanese telephone number, complicating efforts to establish his whereabouts.

Being an untraced contact does not mean that he is infected. However, locating potentially exposed individuals is essential to preventing further transmission.

Uganda shares a lengthy border with the Democratic Republic of Congo, where Ebola outbreaks have repeatedly challenged health authorities.

Cross-border trade, population movements and international travel make it difficult to establish where someone was exposed to the virus.

Ebola symptoms typically develop two to 21 days after infection. According to the WHO, people infected with Ebola are not considered infectious until symptoms appear.

Even well-functioning border surveillance systems cannot identify every infected traveler.

The discovery that both patients traveled through the same international airport on the same day illustrates the complexity of tracing potential exposures across multiple countries. It also highlights the importance of reconstructing travel histories and identifying close contacts.

Uganda says it is strengthening screening and public health controls at points of entry. Nigeria has intensified contact tracing and infection prevention measures.

The challenge is ensuring that authorities across different countries can exchange accurate information quickly enough to identify potential exposures.

In Abuja, the confirmed infection has revived memories of previous Ebola outbreaks. Resident Peace Kolawole expressed concern about the consequences of another outbreak. "So here is my hand sanitizer, so I'm actually very concerned about it," she told DW.

Others fear that Nigeria's health care system may struggle to respond while many citizens face economic hardship. "Because now people are not even worried about sickness anymore, people are worried about surviving," Abuja resident Jonathan Steven told DW.

Nigeria's health authorities say the patient is receiving medical care and contact tracing is continuing.

Uganda's position between the DRC and other regional destinations offers one explanation for its appearance in both investigations.

But the latest findings have added two significant questions: Did the patients from Kenya and Nigeria come into contact at Entebbe International Airport on October 3? And where did the Nigerian woman contract the virus if, as Switzerland insists, she never traveled to DR Congo?

Uganda's declaration of zero confirmed active cases does not establish that all infections have been ruled out. Equally, the travel histories do not prove an undetected outbreak.

For Kenya and Nigeria, the immediate priority is containing confirmed infections. For Uganda, the challenge is demonstrating that its surveillance systems are effective and reassuring both its citizens and neighboring countries.

#112. North Korea prepares to restart propaganda loudspeakers as ties sour with South

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Kim Yo-jong, sister of Kim Jong-un, issues warning after inspecting border fortifications against backdrop of rising tensions

Propaganda loudspeakers aimed at South Korea will be ready to restart within days along the entire border, Kim Yo-jong, the powerful sister of North Korean leader Kim Jong-un, has said amid a worsening of ties between the two countries.

The statement, carried by the state news agency KCNA on Thursday, came a day after she said the loudspeakers would be in operation along the whole border from some time in October.

Both North and South Korea have historically used giant loudspeakers to broadcast propaganda across the border, with the North in recent years playing haunting bestial howls, and the South opting for K-pop music ​and news criticising Pyongyang.

If the broadcasts resume, they would be the first loudspeaker exchange across the border in 16 months. South Korean president Lee Jae Myung suspended broadcasts from the southern side just days into his presidency in June 2025 to ease tensions with Pyongyang, and the military dismantled the speakers that August.

At a briefing on Thursday, a defence ministry spokesperson in Seoul said that if North Korea went ahead, “naturally there would have to be a proportional measure”, but made clear no decision had been made.

The joint chiefs of staff said routine maintenance of the North’s loudspeakers had been observed before Kim Yo-jong’s statements.

Tensions between the two Koreas have risen since September, when a landmine blast south of the military demarcation line injured three South Korean soldiers, two of them seriously.

Seoul and the UN command – which oversees the 1953 armistice that halted but did not end the Korean war – concluded it was a North Korean anti-personnel mine and an armistice violation. Pyongyang denied responsibility and called the incident a “self-staged farce”.

The loudspeaker threat comes as North Korea continues to fortify its side of the border. Since Kim Jong-un declared that the two Koreas were “two states hostile to each other” in December 2023, North Korea has laid mines, strung barbed wire and erected anti-tank barriers along the border.

On Wednesday, Kim Yo-jong inspected several fortification points along the border with Pak Jong-chon, a marshal and adviser to Pyongyang’s defence ministry.

The border had been “clearly blocked and turned into a fortress more thoroughly”, she said, adding that Kim Jong-un had told her it “should be a permanent line never to be crossed”.

Seoul says North Korean troops have repeatedly crossed the military demarcation line, the 250km line dividing the two Koreas, during the construction work, prompting South Korean soldiers to issue warnings over loudspeakers and fire warning shots.

North Korea has called the warning shots and broadcasts provocations by “armed rogues”.

Colossal Biosciences’ Ben Lamm and Anduril’s Palmer Luckey had an idea to genetically engineer war dogs for the U.S. military, law enforcement, and other emergency response teams. On Thursday, that idea came out of stealth as Warhound, backed by $200 million in funding from investors including Sequoia, 8VC, Corner Ventures, and In-Q-Tel, a venture firm backed by the CIA and other U.S. intelligence agencies. The round values the company at $1.2 billion.

Warhound’s dogs, which do not exist yet, will take on tasks that are dangerous for humans or difficult for machines, such as bomb detection, searches, and other specialized operations. In its launch announcement, the company said that more than 80% of the U.S. military’s working dogs come from Europe, and less than half of them are fit for service.

That means the supply of working dogs is dwindling, and the U.S. increasingly relies on foreign nations for dogs that play an important role in the military and in law enforcement. Warhound aims to change that. The hope is that Colossal can use its technology to engineer dogs with “characteristics suited to demanding missions,” the company said in a press release.

Lamm told TechCrunch he met Luckey about a year ago at a conference, where Luckey said more should be done for the military’s working dogs. The two kept in touch, and Lamm researched how working dogs are bred. He learned that many of them do not make it through training because of inherited predispositions to problems like poor eyesight or the high possibilities of cancer, hip dysplasia, or muscle atrophy.

Lamm believed dogs could be enhanced for health, longevity, and “for things like speed and agility or enhance olfactory sensors for bomb sniffing and whatnot,” he said. His team studied top-performing working dog breeds — the Belgian Malinois and German Shepherd — taking cell lines (living cells grown in a lab) from them and putting them in a biobank. From there, the team will edit the dogs’ genes, adding ones that will improve performance and removing ones that will not, to create “a new type of dog.”

Anduril, meanwhile, will provide software and hardware, such as protection vests and goggles, to assist the dogs and their handlers with situational awareness and coordination. Lamm said to imagine, for example, bulletproof vests with data sensors that send information back to the handlers so they can better understand what is happening.

“The idea is, if we make the dog safer, make it healthier, make it perform better, it’s going to perform better in the line of duty,” Lamm said. He added that Anduril’s additions will make the dogs safer and healthier beyond their genetics, “on top of the dog, not in the dog.” He believes the enhancements will also better protect the dogs’ human handlers.

Luckey is a partner in the venture but owns no equity in it, Lamm told TechCrunch. Colossal owns equity in the business and supports it by licensing its technology.

Colossal is currently known for its de-extinction efforts to bring back animals like the dodo and the mammoth. As Lamm previously told TechCrunch, the company has been using AI, buying and building technology to help with gene cloning and with identifying and copying animal genes.

Lamm said the first batch of dogs is about to be cloned and incubated, with births expected early next year and service ideally beginning within two years. Viagen, another Colossal-owned company, will partner with Warhound to clone the dogs. Lamm said Viagen’s technology has a high cloning success rate, and Colossal already uses it in its program to protect endangered species.

Warhound doesn’t know yet how much it will charge for the dogs, but it expects to sell to first responders, the Department of Defense, military service groups, and police forces. Asked about government approval, Lamm said the company has briefed many government groups about the business.

Asked about civilian safety once genetically enhanced dogs enter service, Lamm said each dog will be trained like any military working dog and managed by whoever buys it.

The company said it is working under the guidance of the American Humane Society to help certify Warhound’s training program for the new dogs. The hope is also that when these new military dogs retire, they will enter the Society’s Military Working Dog Reunification Program, which provides homes for retired military dogs — or another program the Society offers to help dogs.

When reached for comment, the American Humane Society said, “We applaud Warhound and Colossal for inviting the country’s first national humane organization, which is grounded in science and ethics, to have a seat at the table. We look forward to working with them to help ensure the animals in their care are treated humanely.”

#107. Japan-Russia ties erode as journalist sits in Moscow jail

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Japan is demanding access to a journalist who was arrested by Russian security services. The Kremlin claims that Japan is using journalists as spies.

Japanese Foreign Ministry officials were in Moscow this week in an attempt to contact a journalist arrested in Russia's far east on allegations of spying.

Journalist Yuki Imai was arrested on October 14 by Russia's FSB security service in the city of Yuzhno-Sakhalinsk on Sakhalin Island. Televised footage of the arrest showed FSB officers barging into his hotel room as he slept.

The correspondent for the Hokkaido Shimbun newspaper was accused of attempting to obtain information on soldiers in the region, which is near the Kiril islands, a geopolitical sore spot in Russia-Japan relations.

Russian authorities quickly transferred him to Lefortovo Prison in Moscow, where a court ordered him to be held in pretrial detention until at least December 12. If convicted, 34-year-old Imai faces up to 20 years in jail, according to the International Federation of Journalists, which has called for his immediate release.

Officials from the Japanese embassy in Moscow have been denied access to Imai. Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi has condemned the arrest and dismissed the claim that Imai was a spy.

Russian Ambassador Nikolay Nozdrev said Imai had been caught "red-handed," the official TASS news agency reported. Nozdrev has claimed that Japanese intelligence agencies use journalists to collect classified information with the goal of "undermining Russia's security and defense capability."

Yoji Gomi, a veteran journalist and author who has reported from across Asia, told DW that Japanese journalists may occasionally meet with diplomats in the course of their work and "share thoughts and impressions." However, he rejects the idea that reporters are paid spies.

"One-party nations often see foreign correspondents as spies because that is exactly what they use their journalists for," he said.

"I can completely see how the Russians decided that Imai was a spy, as it is how they operate, plus he was in a sensitive part of Russia where there are lots of North Korean laborers that they want to keep quiet," Gomi added.

Stephen Nagy, a professor of international relations at Tokyo's International Christian University, told DW that Russia might see Imai as a bargaining chip it can use to exchange for nationals detained overseas.

Nagy doubts that Japan will give in to pressure.

"They are going to resist because it means that Russia, or any other country, can then just seize a Japanese national, put them on trial for espionage and demand the release of one of their people," he said.

"It would set a dangerous precedent," he added.

The arrest comes after a member of the Russia-linked hacking group Qilin was arrested in Japan and extradited to Germany to face charges in connection with a 2024 cyberattack on a logistics center.

The last time a journalist was arrested in Russia was in 2019 for "attempting to obtain classified information about Russia's military potential in the Far East," Russia's Interfax news agency reported. However, in this case, the journalist was quickly released.

Imai's case is perhaps the most serious since 2023, when Wall Street Journal reporter Evan Gershkovich was arrested in Russia and sentenced to 16 years in a penal colony before being released in a prisoner exchange with Western countries in 2024.

Imai's arrest is the latest in a series of incidents that have sent bilateral relations into a tailspin.

Nagy said Imai's seizure was designed to put pressure on Tokyo. He added this is part of a coordinated approach with China to isolate Japan at a time when the US is preoccupied with domestic issues and the conflict in the Middle East.

"Moscow and Beijing are coordinating to create the image that Japan is not a reformed member of the international community but still the military state of the 1930s and that it poses a threat to stability in the Indo-Pacific region," Nagy said.

"This is all designed to smear Japan's efforts to lead diplomacy in the region at a time when the US is perhaps distracted," he said. "And it does not even matter much that it is not true; they will just continue to repeat the claim to discredit and weaken Tokyo."

Tadashi Anno, a professor of politics at Tokyo's Sophia University, told DW that he believes Japan had been working hard to maintain a stable relationship with Russia, even after Tokyo joined international condemnation of the invasion of Ukraine.

Anno said Russia's timing in turning up the heat on Japan was puzzling.

"Japan condemned the invasion of Ukraine in 2022 because it was a fundamental violation of international law, but Russia is only now pushing back hard," he said.

At the center of deteriorating ties is a territorial dispute about a chain of islands off the far north of Japan that were seized by Soviet forces after Japan's surrender in World War II.

There had been hopes in the past in Tokyo that a concerted diplomatic effort could be sufficient to convince Russia to return the islands — known as the Northern Territories in Japan and the Southern Kurils by Russia — to Japanese sovereignty.

That campaign has faltered in the past couple of years, and was further dented when Japan announced in the spring that it would be joining the NATO initiative to provide nonlethal assistance to Kyiv.

In August, President Vladimir Putin visited the disputed islands, becoming only the second Russian leader to do so. Earlier this month, a delegation of Chinese businesspeople was welcomed on one of the islands, and talks are underway on the possibility of economic cooperation.

#106. Woman with Ebola in Nigeria confirmed as wife of Swiss diplomat

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

This is the first case of Ebola in Nigeria in more than a decade

Switzerland's foreign ministry has confirmed that the woman who has tested positive for Ebola in Nigeria is the wife of a Swiss diplomat.

The woman, a 42-year-old Ugandan-born national who lives in the Nigerian capital, Abuja, tested positive for the virus on 20 October, becoming the country's first confirmed Ebola patient in more than a decade.

They both travelled to Uganda earlier this month. Her husband is currently in isolation but has not developed any symptoms, according to the Swiss foreign ministry.

The ministry told the BBC that all 25 staff at its embassy in Nigeria were in good health and did not need to isolate.

The ministry said the woman had spent about a week at a private clinic in Abuja with symptoms before her diagnosis.

It believes she contracted the virus while travelling in Uganda, although health authorities have yet to establish the source of her infection.

Uganda borders Democratic Republic of Congo, where some 4,600 have died in the country's most deadly outbreak of Ebola. Two people died in Uganda shortly after the outbreak began in May but it has been declared free of Ebola since August.

The ministry said the decision not to isolate the embassy's eight Swiss and 17 local employees was taken in consultation with Nigeria's Ministry of Health and the World Health Organization (WHO).

It added that the embassy remained open and operational, with health and safety measures in place.

The woman is receiving treatment in isolation and is showing signs of improvement, according to the WHO.

Investigators are examining whether they could have been exposed through shared equipment or other contact, the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (Africa CDC) said on Thursday.

Dr Wessam Mankoula, the agency's head of emergency preparedness and response, said this was "one of the possible scenarios", but stressed that no link had been established.

The Kenyan man had travelled from DR Congo through Uganda before arriving in Nairobi on 3 October. He died shortly afterwards, becoming Kenya's first recorded Ebola death.

Kenya has since confirmed eight other cases of Ebola.

The WHO said it was too early to determine where the woman in Nigeria became infected.

"The source of exposure is still not known. The travel history of the patient is still being pieced together," said Dr Patrick Otim, a WHO emergency response official.

He said investigators had struggled to obtain a complete account of her movements because opportunities to interview her were limited while she remained in hospital.

Nigeria's Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (NCDC) said it was carrying out genomic sequencing, which could help establish whether her infection is linked to other cases. However, it has been confirmed that she has the Bundibugyo species of Ebola, the same one which has caused the outbreak in DR Congo.

"The investigation is reconstructing the full travel and exposure history. Genomic sequencing is given a high priority," said Dr Pavel Ursu, the WHO's representative in Nigeria.

Ugandan health officials previously said the woman had travelled to eastern DR Congo for business.

However, the WHO, Africa CDC and Nigeria's health ministry said they had not received verified information confirming that she had visited the country.

Uganda has maintained that it has no confirmed active Ebola cases and says the woman's travel through the country does not necessarily mean she contracted the virus there.

Meanwhile, Nigerian authorities are tracing more people who may have been exposed to the woman, beyond the 44 contacts initially identified.

Kenya's Health Director-General Dr Patrick Amoth said all nine confirmed infections were linked to the first patient, with no evidence of wider community transmission.

He urged the public to remain vigilant and not panic but warned: "We are not out of the woods".

He said the surviving eight patients were still in isolation and responding well to treatment.

The developments have renewed concerns about the movement of infected people across international borders, particularly between DR Congo and neighbouring Uganda, where thousands cross daily for trade, work and family visits.

Dr Otim warned that the long and porous border between the two countries made it difficult to screen everyone entering or leaving, as many people used unofficial crossing points.

The WHO's regional director for Africa, Dr Mohamed Janabi, called for "regional vigilance" and urged countries to strengthen surveillance, investigate infections and protect frontline health workers.

Ebola is a serious viral disease that spreads through direct contact with the blood or other bodily fluids of an infected person, or with contaminated objects. It does not spread through the air like flu.

The virus can cause fever, severe weakness, vomiting and diarrhoea, and can be fatal. People are not considered infectious before they develop symptoms.

Health authorities say tracing contacts quickly, isolating suspected cases and providing early treatment are crucial to preventing further transmission.

#104. Magnitude-7 earthquake hits Tibet, killing at least 16 and destroying hundreds of homes

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Search and rescue efforts under way after quake strikes region’s north, with at least seven aftershocks recorded

A magnitude-7 earthquake has hit Tibet, killing at least 16 people, injuring 54 and destroying hundreds of homes in the northern regional city of Nagqu, official media have said.

Search and rescue efforts continued after at least 339 homes and 20 livestock sheds collapsed, the Nagqu media office said, citing city government officials. It was the biggest quake in the region in the past five years, according to China’s national broadcaster CCTV.

More than 1,600 people from the local county were deployed to help rescue efforts. Supplies were also sent into the region, including 1,000 prefabricated housing units, about 3,000 tents, folding beds, torches and winter clothing.

The US Geological Survey said the quake occurred at 10.33pm local time on Thursday, with its epicentre 119 miles (191km) east-north-east of Nagqu, in a sparsely populated, high-altitude area of northern Tibet. It put the earthquake’s depth at 11 miles.

The agency recorded at least seven aftershocks of magnitudes between 4.3 and 5.2, all of them with a depth of 6 miles. Videos posted by CCTV showed workers running out of restaurants and objects falling off shelves at supermarkets when the quake struck.

China’s official Xinhua news agency said on Friday that communication in some areas hit by the quake was down. Local railway authorities had taken emergency actions to stop trains passing through the region.

More than 300 police officers were dispatched for relief work while the regional fire and rescue services had sent almost 350 personnel to the affected areas, Xinhua reported. Rescue workers clambered through damaged buildings, some with collapsed roofs, footage on CCTV showed.

The earthquake caused rockfalls, forcing local authorities to close a section of national highway 317, allowing only emergency, rescue and official support vehicles through as crews worked to clear debris, according to Xinhua. Intermittent snow was expected in the region over the next three days, Xinhua said.

China’s president, Xi Jinping, called for an all-out effort in the rescue operation and medical relief work.

Nagqu is more than 185 miles north of Tibet’s capital, Lhasa. Earthquakes are relatively common in China. In January 2025, at least 126 people were killed in one in Tibet’s rural Tingri county.

In August, a disastrous flood hit the border of Tibet and Nepal. China’s tight grip on information prevented international media from visiting the affected regions on the Tibetan side, obscuring the scale of the damage.

In December 2023, a quake in remote Gansu province in north-west China killed at least 134 people, state media said. That quake was China’s deadliest since 2014, when more than 600 people died in the south-western province of Yunnan.

Tibet is a particularly seismically active area. The high-altitude region was taken under Chinese control in the 1950s and its people, who are predominantly ethnic Tibetans, have had a tense relationship with the authorities in Beijing since. As well as being one of the poorest regions in China, many Tibetans reject Beijing-imposed controls on their culture and religion.

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

During Meta’s earnings call on Wednesday, the company revealed its board had approved a new class of nonvoting stock, called Class C common stock. The shares give the holder an ownership stake but no vote on company decisions. This class takes effect in January. Meta says it will use the stock for capital raising, employee pay, and acquisitions — including its expensive push into AI — while maintaining the current voting structure that favors its founder and CEO, Mark Zuckerberg, who controls around 60% of the vote.

Now, however, the New York Times has reported there’s another reason for the new class: helping Zuckerberg pay for California’s proposed “billionaire tax,” if voters approve it. Because the new shares carry no votes, he could sell some of them to cover the tax without giving up voting control.

Proposition 40, which was drafted by Berkeley tax law professors and is being sponsored by California’s largest healthcare union (SEIU United Healthcare Workers West), would level a one-time 5% wealth tax on the net worth of several hundred California billionaires who primarily live in the state. Backers say the policy could generate as much as $100 billion over five years, the bulk of which would be used to pay for the state’s healthcare needs, with the rest being used for food assistance and education.

The Times also reports that Zuckerberg pressed Meta’s board to come up with a plan to protect him from the tax. According to the paper, he told board members in September that if they couldn’t reach an agreement, he’d list his Palo Alto property for sale, potentially kicking off a media firestorm. Not long afterward, the stock plan materialized, the newspaper writes.

The Times reporting is partially based on a recent SEC filing that Meta made, which documents the concern surrounding Proposition 40 and how it informed discussions that led to the new stock class.

The filing says that as far back as July, Zuckerberg was discussing the billionaire tax with board members. The topic came up again in additional meetings and communications throughout that month and August.

The filing also describes how the plan would work. Meta is creating a new class of nonvoting shares, called Class C, and will give shareholders one of them for every Class A or Class B share they own. The company calls this a one-time stock dividend and says it should work out to a two-for-one stock split. The dividend “will not change the voting power of any shareholder,” Meta says.

That is where Prop 40 comes in. The filing says the dividend “will allow Mr. Zuckerberg to immediately sell or transfer up to 20% of the shares of Class C common stock that he receives in the Dividend, representing 10% of his overall economic stake in the Company, for any reason, including so that he may pay his potential tax liability under California Proposition 40, if enacted into law, without affecting his majority voting control.” Because the dividend is one-for-one, half of his post-dividend shares will be Class C, so the 20% cap equals 10% of his total economic stake.

When reached for comment by TechCrunch, Meta referred us to a quote included in its recent earnings press release that reiterates that part of the motivation for the creation of the stock was to keep Zuckerberg onboard: “The Special Committee affirmed its belief that it was important for Mr. Zuckerberg to continue to lead the Company from California, including because it was crucial that Mr. Zuckerberg remains in close coordination with the Company’s management and in close proximity to Meta Superintelligence Labs.”

It’s been estimated that Zuckerberg would have to pay as much as $11 billion as part of the tax.

Prop 40 has notably riled many of Silicon Valley’s most prominent businesspeople. Google co-founder Sergey Brin has aggressively campaigned against the measure, even spending a small fortune in an effort to defeat it. Other business leaders and critics of the proposal have criticized it online, with many claiming that it amounts to economic suicide for California, driving a capital flight from the state. However, as Meta’s new policy would appear to show, the state’s mega-companies and the people who run them have creative ways of dealing with the tax, should it materialize.

“Looks like billionaires will stay and figure out a way to pay the tax,” Democratic U.S. Representative Ro Khanna, a vocal proponent of the tax, wrote on X, after news of Meta’s strategy spread online. “If we pass Prop 40, poor California kids can have healthcare,” he added.
