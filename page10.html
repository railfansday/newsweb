#3656. LG B6 OLED TV Review (2026)

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

It’s amazing what can happen in just one year. The LG B6 is the cheapest LG OLED model around (priced lower than the LG C6 and LG G6), but it also has some stiff competition. New art televisions like the Amazon Ember Artline (which costs under $1,000) use a matte display that renders masterpiece paintings in convincingly realistic glory. RGB televisions like the LG Micro RGB Evo and those from Samsung and Hisense have also burst onto the market, making a convincing argument for choosing an RGB screen over an OLED one.

Last year around this time, we reviewed the LG B5 and ranked it a solid 8/10, but there was less competition. The LG B6 is still a good budget OLED TV, it’s just beset on all sides by better, brighter options, including from LG’s own LG C6.

The first thing that struck me while setting up the LG B6 is that it’s incredibly thin—just 1.8 inches at the base and about a quarter-inch at the top and around most of the bezel. The OLED panel doesn’t need any backlighting like most LED or RGB televisions, so it can be extra thin. The lower portion of the TV is thicker because of the four HDMI ports (all of which support a 120-Hz refresh rate), an optical audio out, two USB 2.0 ports, and one LAN port. The TV only supports Wi-Fi 5, certainly not the latest standard.

The B6 comes with two legs that are easy to install (two screws each), though I generally prefer a single pedestal stand design. The 65-inch model I tested fit comfortably on two 38-inch IKEA SPIKSMED TV benches set side by side that measure 76 inches long.

Like all LG TVs, setup involves LG’s own WebOS interface or ThinQ app, not built-in Google TV or Fire TV. It was easy to complete the setup using the LG ThinQ app on my iPhone—in minutes, I was loading my usual assortment of apps like Prime Video, Disney+ and Apple TV+.

The B6 is packed with features too. A few of my favorites include a gaming mode that helps improve low latency gaming, AI picture enhancements that actually work and make a discernible difference, an artwork mode called LG Gallery+ that costs $5 per month, and an LG music app in case you don’t like Spotify or other music apps. It also has settings for audio dialogue sync, similar to what you can do with powered speakers like the Klipsch The Fives II. A desktop mode means you can mirror your laptop screen and even run a Gmail app.

I have mixed feelings about WebOS, though. My main gripe is that you cannot disable the pointer on the LG remote, and it jumps around a lot. When you press the center dial on the remote, it turns off temporarily as though LG user interface designers are admitting it isn’t that great. I also have mixed feelings about the remote—it’s hard to use in the dark by feel. The Home button is offset so it’s hard to find, and shortcuts for Netflix and other apps are situated at the bottom, but not backlit.

Based on the pricing, it’s obvious LG has positioned the B6 as the entry-level OLED in their line-up. That became crystal clear once I started testing movies and TV shows.

In the official specs, LG only lists “wide color gamut” and not a percentage for the specific BT.2020 spec, nor a specific brightness rating. This is where the benchmark testing comes in handy, and what I tested right away.

One win for the B6 is blacks and grays, which have great texture and clarity, a surprise given how many midrange televisions make dark, shadowy scenes look washed out. Scenes from Awake and The Creator, very dark movies, were clear and detailed, after tweaking a few picture quality settings (namely changing to Vivid mode).

Contrast and brightness are more of a mixed bag. I watched House of the Dragon and Game of Thrones (yes, the infamously dark season eight, episode three) to stress test these specs, and the B6 struggled. On better TVs (and home theater projectors), you can see trees in the background of a scene where a character emerges on a frozen lake. On the B6, they are completely black—e.g., a contrast problem.

Color saturation and luminance could be better too, especially compared to RGB TVs I’ve tested recently. The Super Mario Galaxy Movie, with its various blooming effects, wasn’t exactly terrible looking, but certainly not as crisp as on something like the LG Micro RGB Evo. (The less technicolor The End of Oak Street fared better.) During a demo reel benchmark, green grass by a fence in winter looked a bit too brown for my taste (again, color saturation issue), but skin tone variation was surprisingly good.

LG is not the first brand I think of for playing video games (it’s Sony), but the B6 deserves some extra credit here. The 120-Hz refresh rate screen does its job, and made all my movements playing Crimson Desert on PC crisp and responsive. Resident Evil: Requiem looked almost photo realistic, with gleaming statues and surprisingly good color quality, even zooming in on crumpled leaves.

Watching sports and news didn’t yield any surprises—a Baltimore Orioles baseball game looked rich and textured, but not very bright.

LG B6 may be an entry-level OLED, but, like an entry-level BMW, it has a lot of the bells and whistles of a flagship, with plenty of ports, features, and premium finishes. My main issue with it is brightness and contrast, both could be better. And LG’s WebOS is still not my favorite interface in the world.

The main issue might actually be price. At the time of writing, the LG B6 is on sale for $1,300. The superior midrange LG C6 is also on sale for $1,500. That’s only $200 more for a much better, brighter TV (both 65-inches). That’s not even discussing buying an older (but still brighter) model like the LG C5 (which I found on sale for less than the B6). It all depends on how much you want to spend.

#4058. New 3D thermal invisibility cloak makes objects disappear from infrared cameras

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Scientists have developed and tested the first three-dimensional thermal invisibility cloak, a device that can conceal objects from infrared cameras while protecting them from extreme temperatures. The breakthrough could lead to new ways to cool microchips, safeguard delicate electronics, and prevent equipment from being detected through its thermal signature.

The cloak can conceal objects with a wide variety of shapes, regardless of the direction from which heat approaches. Earlier thermal cloaks were limited to two dimensions or worked only when heat traveled in a particular direction. The new design overcomes these restrictions by directing heat around a hidden object rather than simply blocking it. As a result, infrared imaging shows a temperature pattern that makes the object appear to be absent.

The research was conducted by University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign civil and environmental engineering professor Shelly Zhang, postdoctoral researcher Weichen Li, and graduate student Yibo Wang, in collaboration with professor Ole Sigmund of the Technical University of Denmark. Their findings were published in Nature Communications.

"A real thermal cloak should work no matter where the heat comes from," Zhang said. "Our device can hide a complex 3D object in an infinite number of directions while keeping the temperature inside stable and protected."

A New Material Makes 3D Thermal Invisibility Possible

Creating a thermal cloak that functions in three dimensions has been a longstanding challenge. Previous experimental devices could redirect heat only across a flat surface or along one direction of heat flow, leaving them far short of the capabilities needed for a fully three-dimensional cloak.

To overcome these limitations, the researchers revisited the foundational principles of transformation thermotics, a field that explores how heat can be redirected through specially designed materials. They focused on a central question: "What kind of material structure could cover almost all the thermal properties needed for a perfect cloak?"

The solution was a new lattice-based material whose structure can be independently adjusted along three directions. These changes allow the researchers to control how efficiently heat passes through different parts of the cloak. Compared with previous designs, the material can achieve a much broader range of thermal conductivities, closely approaching the properties predicted to be necessary for ideal thermal cloaking.

Importantly, the researchers went beyond computer simulations and constructed a working device. They used 3D metal printing to produce an intricate aluminum lattice, which provides pathways for efficient heat conduction. They then used mold casting to fill the remaining spaces with a rubber-like material that conducts heat poorly. Together, these materials create a structure that can carefully control the movement of heat.

Infrared Camera Tests Confirm the Cloak Works

To evaluate the device, the team positioned it between hot and cold regions, establishing a temperature difference that caused heat to flow through the surrounding environment. They monitored the resulting temperature patterns with an infrared camera to determine whether the cloak could successfully redirect heat around the concealed area.

The experiments showed that the device could guide heat around the hidden region while preserving the surrounding temperature pattern. To an infrared camera, the area appeared much as it would if no object were present. At the same time, temperatures inside the cloak remained uniform, shielding the concealed space from the extreme conditions outside.

The researchers also tested whether the cloak could accommodate unusually complicated objects. They successfully concealed intricate three-dimensional geometries, including detailed shapes resembling human heads. According to the team, earlier experimental thermal cloaks have not demonstrated a comparable combination of geometric complexity and cloaking performance.

Potential Applications in Electronics and Stealth Technology

The ability to control heat in three dimensions could have important implications across several industries. In electronics, the technology could help engineers direct heat away from sensitive components, improve thermal management in microchips, and protect devices operating under demanding conditions.

The researchers also see potential applications in security and defense. Future versions of the technology might help conceal people or equipment from thermal detection systems or shield valuable assets from severe temperature conditions. These uses would build on the device's demonstrated ability to redirect external heat while keeping the protected region thermally stable.

"Any field that needs precise control of heat or needs to protect something from being detected thermally could benefit from this work," Zhang said. "But we also see it more broadly: it's about hiding and protecting information that is carried by heat."

The Next Challenge Is Hiding Objects That Generate Heat

Although the current cloak represents a major advance, the researchers want to develop more sophisticated versions capable of performing additional functions. One particularly important goal is to conceal objects that produce heat themselves, rather than only protecting them from heat originating outside the cloak.

Achieving this would require materials that can actively control heat within the concealed region. Instead of simply redirecting incoming heat, a future cloak would need to concentrate, distribute, or guide thermal energy as conditions change. Such capabilities could expand the technology's usefulness in electronics, thermal protection, and applications involving infrared detection.

"We've shown that a true 3D omnidirectional thermal cloak is possible," Zhang said. "The next step is to make cloaks that don't just hide and protect, but also actively manipulate heat in useful ways."

The research received support from the National Science Foundation, the Villum Foundation, and the Air Force Office of Scientific Research. Zhang is also affiliated with mechanical science and engineering and the National Center for Supercomputing Applications at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign.

#4065. Tiny magnetic vortices could help computers work more like your brain

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Tiny magnetic structures called skyrmions could help pave the way for a new generation of computers that process information more like the human brain while using far less electricity. Although most people have never heard of skyrmions, these microscopic magnetic vortices may eventually play an important role in how computers store and process data.

A study published in the journal Nature Nanotechnology provides new insights into how skyrmions behave and how scientists can control them. The findings could help researchers develop smaller, more efficient computing systems and advance emerging quantum technologies.

Most of the research was conducted at the Institute of Physics at Johannes Gutenberg University in Mainz, Germany, with contributions from a professor at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology (NTNU).

"Skyrmions are small, magnetic vortices that can store information in a completely new way," explained Asle Sudbø, who leads NTNU's Centre for Quantum Spintronics (QuSpin).

Unlike conventional technologies, which require increasing amounts of space and energy to handle growing quantities of data, skyrmions could offer a much more efficient alternative.

"Instead of the current technology, where we constantly need more space and energy to process data, skyrmions could make it possible to build much smaller and more energy-efficient data storage systems," explained Sudbø.

Tiny Magnetic Vortices Could Enable Brain-Like Computers

The potential applications of skyrmions extend well beyond conventional data storage. They are also important to spintronics, which explores ways to use the magnetic properties of electrons in electronic devices, and to research involving topological quantum effects.

One particularly promising possibility is the development of computers that operate in ways similar to biological brains.

"These nanoscale vortices could help develop computers that resemble biological brains," said Sudbø.

Rather than relying on conventional transistor-based computing, such systems could use magnetism to process information. In principle, this approach could dramatically reduce electricity consumption, potentially allowing future computers to perform complex tasks with very little energy.

Turning that possibility into practical technology, however, requires scientists to understand exactly how skyrmions behave and how to manipulate them reliably.

The latest research represents an important advance toward that goal.

Scientists Film Magnetic Skyrmion Lattices Melting for the First Time

Skyrmions do not always exist as isolated magnetic structures. Under certain conditions, they organize themselves into repeating patterns known as lattices, resembling the orderly arrangement of atoms in a crystal, but on a much smaller scale.

Scientists can manipulate these arrangements, a capability that could be essential for using skyrmions in future computing devices.

In the new study, researchers achieved something that had not been demonstrated before. They recorded the melting of a magnetic skyrmion lattice as it happened while simultaneously controlling individual skyrmions.

"For the first time, researchers have managed to film how a magnetic skyrmion lattice melts in real time, while simultaneously being able to manipulate each individual skyrmion directly as they film it. For example, both their shape and size can be changed 'on the fly'," said Sudbø.

The ability to observe this process directly provides a clearer picture of how skyrmions respond when their organized structures break apart. It also allows researchers to study how changing the size and shape of individual magnetic vortices affects their behavior.

A New Step Toward Energy-Efficient Computing

Understanding how skyrmions behave during the breakdown of their lattices could help scientists develop more precise ways to control them. That control is particularly important if these magnetic structures are to become useful components in future computer systems.

The findings therefore offer more than a new way to observe an unusual magnetic phenomenon. They provide information that could help researchers overcome challenges involved in developing practical skyrmion-based technologies.

"The findings give us new insight into how these types of particles behave. This could therefore become important for the development of more energy-efficient computer technology in the future," said Sudbø.

Although substantial development is still needed before skyrmions can be used in everyday computing devices, the research highlights their potential for improving data storage, enabling new computing architectures, and supporting advances in quantum technology.

By learning to observe and manipulate these tiny magnetic vortices in real time, scientists are gaining capabilities that could eventually help reshape the design of computers.

"This could revolutionize the way we think about computers," concluded Sudbø.

#4008. Can GOP Rep. Brian Fitzpatrick, a moderate who's bucked Trump, keep his Pennsylvania swing seat?

📅 26 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Over nearly 10 years in Congress, Rep. Brian Fitzpatrick has cultivated an image as a middle-of-the-road GOP lawmaker who operates above the partisan fray and is willing to buck President Donald Trump.

It's a formula that has allowed Fitzpatrick, who is among the most moderate lawmakers on Capitol Hill according to his hometown newspaper, to win swing voters and consistently outperform his party in a highly competitive district that opted for Joe Biden in 2020 and Kamala Harris in 2024. But in a year when Republican candidates across the country are being weighed down by Trump, Fitzpatrick is facing a stiff challenge from Democrat Bob Harvie in Pennsylvania's 1st Congressional District, and testing whether his personal brand can carry him in a difficult national political environment.

"Brian Fitzpatrick is Brian Fitzpatrick. I'm not Donald Trump. I'm not anybody else. And that's the way voters should approach this," Fitzpatrick told CNBC. "I'm not happy with [Trump]. I'm not happy with his tone. I don't think he's done a sufficient job. I think leaders are supposed to unite our country, unify people. He has not done that."

The battle between Fitzpatrick and Harvie comes as Trump's approval rating craters, the Iran war drags on and consumer prices rise. The contest is one of the approximately four dozen House seats considered in play in the Nov. 3 election that will decide control of Congress for the last two years of Trump's term. Pennsylvania voters are often an indicator of how the country will swing overall, and because the state is on the East Coast, Fitzpatrick's district is one to watch as returns come in.

Democrats are on offense and spending money in districts previously thought out of reach — including Fitzpatrick's, in a race that could signal Democratic success outside of just the most vulnerable seats.

But Harvie, a former high school social studies teacher and county commissioner, is still a long shot to win. The Cook Political Report with Amy Walter shifted the race in Democrats' direction, from "Likely R" to "Lean R," earlier this month. Erin Covey, who leads coverage of House races at the Cook Report, called Fitzpatrick Democrats' "white whale."

As part of Democrats' final push to unseat Fitzpatrick, Harvie was joined earlier this month by House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries, D-N.Y., and Democratic Pennsylvania Gov. Josh Shapiro for a rally in Bucks County.

"This is an incredibly important state," Jeffries told reporters after the rally. "The commonwealth is a big swing state, and Bucks County is a big swing county. And so this is a critically important race."

Fitzpatrick grew up in Bucks County, which makes up much of the district, and is a former FBI agent who succeeded his brother, Michael, in 2017. He's a co-chair of the bipartisan Problem Solvers Caucus and, according to a recent Philadelphia Inquirer analysis of his voting record, has voted against the White House and his party this Congress more than any other House Republican. The paper endorsed Harvie earlier this month.

Fitzpatrick is also part of a bipartisan group that's been outspoken as the White House has taken a more conciliatory tone toward Russia in recent weeks. He led a bill last week that would undo Trump's recent authorization of the U.S. purchase of Russian diesel. And he signed a bipartisan letter urging the Department of Defense to restore Ukraine's access to satellite communications that are critical to the country's defenses, after President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said the services were downgraded. Fitzpatrick threatened to pursue the firing of U.S. officials responsible for the slowdown, The New York Times reported.

Fitzpatrick is seeking his sixth House term and for much of his career has easily warded off Democratic challenges. Christopher Nicholas, a Republican consultant based in Pennsylvania, said Fitzpatrick "has been the most moderate member of the House."

"If they are able to beat him this cycle, it is definitely going to be a great night for Democrats," Covey said.

Harvie casts Fitzpatrick as a rubber stamp for Trump and questions the claims of bipartisanship. While Fitzpatrick was one of only two Republicans to vote against the final version of Republicans' 2025 signature tax and spending package, he voted in favor of an earlier iteration in the House that sent the measure to the Senate and set it on a path to passage. Fitzpatrick has said he supported the initial draft of the bill, but opposed cuts to Medicaid that were added in the Senate.

Harvie also called out Fitzpatrick's mixed record on tariffs. Fitzpatrick voted to overturn Trump's tariffs on Canada, but has also sided with most Republicans on procedural votes to limit Congress' ability to challenge some of the president's tariffs. Fitzpatrick voted in favor of a controversial, Trump-backed election bill, dubbed the SAVE America Act, which would require identification to cast a ballot and proof of citizenship to register to vote. And he backed a controversial GOP immigration proposal and Trump's bid to rename the Gulf of Mexico the "Gulf of America."

"When it comes to our economics in this country, people are suffering," Harvie, a Bucks County native, told CNBC. "The government is one that is totally detached from the people it's supposed to serve. Internationally, we've never been weaker and more isolated … and all of that is because of Donald Trump and because of people like Brian Fitzpatrick, who are just letting him run amok."

Fitzpatrick is still favored to win. But Brit Crampsie, a Democratic strategist based in Pennsylvania, said Democrats have a stronger candidate in Harvie than they have in past cycles.

"Bob Harvie, from his time as a school teacher to a local official, has deep ties to this community, and he's very representative of this community," Crampsie said. "He understands Bucks County and has high name ID."

In addition to Trump's low favorability and voters' economic concerns, Fitzpatrick and other Pennsylvania Republicans must also contend with one of the most popular governors in the country at the top of the ticket. Shapiro is expected to win reelection easily, according to recent polling. In Fitzpatrick's district, Shapiro is predicted to coast to victory by double digits, Covey said.

Pennsylvania also has three other neck-and-neck races that together could decide control of the House. Two Republican incumbents, Reps. Ryan Mackenzie in the 7th District and Scott Perry in the 10th, are seeking reelection in races labeled "Lean D." Republican Rep. Rob Bresnahan, in Pennsylvania's 8th District, is in a "toss up" race, according to the Cook Report.

Fitzpatrick, however, noted he won nearly 55% of voters in his district in 2022, even as Shapiro won 59% of the vote in the district, evidence to him that his constituents are more than willing to split their tickets.

Independent public polling on the race for the district is sparse, though a recent internal survey conducted by Global Strategy Group for the Harvie campaign showed him and Fitzpatrick tied. A September poll conducted by Public Opinion Strategies for the National Republican Congressional Committee and Fitzpatrick's campaign had the incumbent up by 9 percentage points. A poll released Sunday by The New York Times, Philadelphia Inquirer and Siena University found Harvie with a narrow 48% to 46% lead over Fitzpatrick — within the poll's margin of error.

Recent outside spending paints a competitive picture.

The Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee has committed more than $1.5 million to TV ads in the district since the start of October, while House Democrats' main super PAC has pitched in more than $7.5 million, according to AdImpact data. Before this election, neither group had bought TV ads in the district since 2020.

Republicans are also shelling out to shore up Fitzpatrick. The House GOP's main super PAC, the Congressional Leadership Fund, and the NRCC have committed just under $5 million and just over $1 million, respectively, to ads in the district, according to AdImpact. And Trump's super PAC, MAGA Inc., recently purchased $100,000 in ads in the district opposing Harvie, according to an Oct. 12 Federal Election Commission filing.

"Here's how you know where his loyalties lie," Pennsylvania Democratic Party Chair Eugene DePasquale told CNBC. "Donald Trump and MAGA Inc. coming in to spend $100,000 to bail him out because they know when push comes to shove, he'll be loyal to them."

Fitzpatrick has a significant cash advantage, with more than $709,000 in his war chest as of Oct. 14 according to FEC filings, compared with Harvie's just over $409,000 in cash on hand.

Reilly Richardson, a spokesperson for the NRCC, pointed to this fundraising disparity, saying Harvie had "a stagnant campaign" and "a significant cash disadvantage."
