📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Ukrainian President Zelenskyy says Russia is trying to cut electricity and heating to civilians ahead of winter. He also called the US president's waiver of new sanctions on Moscow a boost for Vladimir Putin.

Russia is stepping up its attacks on Ukrainian energy infrastructure in a bid to cut electricity and heating in the country as winter approaches, Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said on Saturday.

His remarks come as Moscow intensifies its attacks on civilian targets in the capital, Kyiv, and elsewhere, with key observers saying its objective is to render the country virtually unlivable.

In a post on social media platform X, Zelenskyy said Russia had just carried out one of its biggest attacks on Ukraine's energy sector since it began its full-scale invasion of its neighbor in February 2022.

"More than 15 critical power generation and transmission facilities were targeted across different regions of Ukraine," he wrote.

"Restoration work is ongoing, with all necessary services and all available resources of Ukrainian energy companies involved," he added.

Zelenskyy said that Russia was planning to leave Ukrainian citizens in the cold this winter.

"Russia is trying to cause a blackout in Ukraine — right before winter. Russia is trying to leave millions of our people without electricity and heating," he wrote, calling on allies to put pressure on Moscow to stop this happening.

He said Russia had "expanded ‌its campaign ​against the energy system" and was trying "to stretch ⁠Ukraine's ​reserves ​and repair ​capacity as ‌thinly as possible." ​

The Russian Defense Ministry, for its part, said on Saturday that its forces had attacked several infrastructure targets in Ukraine overnight.

Among those named was an electricity substation in the northwestern city of Zhytomyr.

However, it also said Ukraine's largest steelworks, in the city of Kryvyi Rih, had been hit, saying the facility supplied steel products to the Ukrainian military-industrial complex.

It said another steel plant in ​Zaporizhzhia, a city near the front ​line, had also been struck.

Ukrainian authorities have also said that one person — an ambulance driver — was killed ​and ‌19 others injured, including a child, in a Russian airstrike on Kherson in southern Ukraine.

Russian state news ​agency TASS has also reported the death of one woman as a result of Ukrainian drone strikes in Russia's Belgorod region that also injured a man.

Meanwhile, Zelenskyy also reacted to US President Donald Trump's decision to waive a package of new sanctions on Moscow, saying the move would only encourage Russian leader Vladimir Putin.

"A US sanctions blackout, easing pressure on the aggressor, financial rewards for Putin — all of this only encourages him to keep attacking," Zelenskyy said.

On Friday, Trump signed an executive order that waived sweeping US sanctions and tariffs targeting Russia's energy revenues, two days before they were due to take effect.

The waiver has been hailed by a Russian presidential envoy as a "historic moment."

"The Trump administration's suspension of the [Lindsey] Graham Act is an important and truly historic moment," Kirill Dmitriev said on social media.

#533. Astronauts’ hearts just passed a surprising test for Mars

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

A journey to Mars may be less challenging for the human heart than scientists once feared. A new study suggests that astronauts can adapt remarkably well to months of weightlessness, offering encouraging evidence that humans could endure the long trip to the Red Planet and function under its weaker gravity.

Researchers from Northwestern Medicine, UT Southwestern, and UT Houston monitored the hearts of 13 astronauts who spent approximately six months aboard the International Space Station. Their findings showed that although spaceflight initially affected heart performance, the astronauts gradually adapted, with intensive daily exercise playing an important role in maintaining their cardiovascular health.

One of NASA's major concerns about sending humans to Mars is how astronauts' hearts and circulatory systems will respond when they leave the weightless conditions of spaceflight (known as microgravity) and encounter Martian gravity, which is only about one-third as strong as Earth's.

Encouragingly, the researchers found that the astronauts showed no significant signs of cardiovascular strain when exposed to simulated Martian gravity after returning to Earth.

"With our findings, there is now reason to think that we would be able to traverse to Mars, get there and actually work on the surface of Mars," said study senior author Dr. James Thomas, professor of medicine in the division of cardiology at Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine.

The study, published last month in the journal Circulation, is the first to investigate how the heart responds to simulated Martian gravity following an extended period in space.

How Daily Exercise Protects Astronauts' Hearts in Space

Living without gravity creates unusual challenges for the human body. On Earth, the heart constantly works against gravity to circulate blood, while muscles must support the body's weight. In space, those demands decrease substantially, allowing muscles and even the heart itself to shrink over time.

Returning to a gravitational environment can also be difficult. Astronauts on previous missions have frequently experienced dizziness or lightheadedness when standing after landing. This happens because gravity pulls blood toward the lower body, sometimes causing blood pressure to drop suddenly. The condition is called orthostatic hypotension. (If you've seen Sandra Bullock crawling after returning to Earth in "Gravity," you get the idea.)

However, the astronauts involved in the new study generally handled this transition well, potentially because they had maintained demanding exercise schedules throughout their missions.

As part of NASA's Exercise Countermeasures program, astronauts exercised for up to two hours each day. Their routines included 30 to 45 minutes of endurance training, which strengthens the cardiovascular system, along with 60 to 75 minutes of resistance training to help preserve muscle mass and strength.

"You lose muscle mass and cardiac mass when you're in a weightless environment because you are working less up there," explained Thomas, who also is a Northwestern Medicine cardiologist. "You're floating around in the air, and you give a little tap with your foot, and you can sail over to say hello to your buddy."

"To counter that, exercise is crucial when you're in space," Thomas added.

Heart Scans Reveal How the Body Adapts to Weightlessness

To understand what happens to the heart during prolonged spaceflight, the researchers collected echocardiograms before, during, and after each astronaut's mission. These ultrasound scans use sound waves to produce moving images of the heart, allowing doctors to examine its structure and measure how effectively it pumps blood.

While aboard the International Space Station, astronauts were trained to perform these scans on one another every few weeks. The recordings were transmitted to Earth, where researchers could monitor changes in heart function throughout the missions.

Thomas worked alongside Dr. Benjamin Levine of UT Southwestern Medical Center and Dr. Michael Bungo of the McGovern Medical School at UTHealth Houston.

"It would be typically about 3 a.m. for me because the space station runs on Greenwich Mean Time," Thomas said. "I would watch the recordings come in live and coach the astronauts in real time to improve the images."

The scans revealed that the astronauts' hearts initially struggled to adjust to their new environment. During the first two weeks in space, researchers observed declines in cardiac filling, stroke volume, and several other measures of cardiovascular performance.

Cardiac filling refers to how much blood enters the heart between beats, while stroke volume describes the amount of blood pumped out with each contraction. Both measurements decreased early in the missions, indicating that the astronauts' hearts were temporarily moving less blood with each beat.

As the astronauts spent more time in space, their cardiovascular systems adapted. Cardiac filling and stroke volume gradually improved, approaching the levels recorded before launch.

These results suggest that the heart can adjust to prolonged weightlessness more effectively than early measurements might indicate, particularly when astronauts maintain regular physical activity.

Astronauts' Hearts Pass a Simulated Mars Gravity Test

The researchers also wanted to answer a particularly important question for future Mars missions: What happens when an astronaut who has spent months in weightlessness suddenly encounters gravity again?

Mars has roughly 38% of Earth's surface gravity. Although this is considerably weaker than what people experience on Earth, it would still force astronauts' hearts to work differently after months of floating in space.

To reproduce some of these effects, the researchers conducted a simulated Martian gravity test after the astronauts returned to Earth.

Each astronaut was tilted upward by 22 degrees from a lying position. This posture caused blood to shift toward the lower body, approximating the circulatory effects of Martian gravity.

The results were encouraging. After spending about six months in space, the astronauts' hearts performed just as well under simulated Martian gravity as they had when standing upright on Earth before their missions.

This suggests that astronauts who maintain their cardiovascular fitness during spaceflight may be capable of transitioning to the weaker gravity of Mars without experiencing major cardiovascular difficulties.

Although the findings come from a relatively small group of astronauts and a simulation rather than an actual Mars landing, they provide valuable evidence for planning future missions.

Combined with the benefits of daily exercise, the heart's ability to adapt to weightlessness and respond well to simulated Martian gravity offers another reason to be optimistic about sending humans to the Red Planet.

The study is titled "Impact of Prolonged Spaceflight on Cardiac Structure and Function: An Exploratory Longitudinal Observational Study." #532. Mark Zuckerberg Agrees to Stay in California Despite Billionaires’ Tax Ballot Measure

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg has agreed to keep living in Silicon Valley for eight years, potentially exposing himself to billions of dollars in one-time taxes if California voters approve a contentious ballot measure next month, according to a company regulatory filing released Wednesday.

During a dinner with Meta board members on September 9, Zuckerberg informed them that he was considering leaving California because of the measure, known as Proposition 40, and that he planned to list his Palo Alto, California, home for sale four days later, the 109-page filing stated. The ballot proposal, which could be subject to lawsuits even if voters approve it, calls for a one-time 5 percent tax on stock and other assets held by billionaires in the state as of January 2026.

Many wealthy individuals, including Zuckerberg, currently avoid significant tax payments by borrowing against their shares and taking low salaries—$1 annually in the Meta CEO’s case. Zuckerberg purchased a home in Florida this past February, The Wall Street Journal reported at the time.

His potential cross-country move concerned a special committee of the board, whose members included attorney Robert Kimmitt, venture capitalist Charlie Songhurst, and DoorDash CEO Tony Xu. “The special committee was mindful about the difficulties the company faced during the COVID-19 pandemic when Zuckerberg could not be physically present with the company’s management team and did not want the company to face similar challenges going forward,” according to Wednesday’s filing.

Within days of the Meta board dinner, the special committee and Zuckerberg reached an agreement that links his ability to sell special company shares to his continued residence in California. It was finalized a week ago, and on Wednesday, Meta announced the details. It plans to create a new class of shares that hold no voting power. Selling these shares could potentially help raise funds for Meta’s expensive investments in AI data centers, without diluting Zuckerberg’s existing voting control.

All existing shareholders will receive the new nonvoting shares automatically in proportion to their existing holdings. Under what Meta called a “Founder Alignment Agreement” with Zuckerberg, he can sell 20 percent of those new shares to help pay any taxes he may owe. The remaining 80 percent would be available for sale in chunks over the next eight years as long as he remains in California. Any attempt to sell them early would require offloading some of his voting shares “unless there are material unforeseen circumstances that would render it prudent or advisable for Zuckerberg to reside elsewhere.”

Notably, the agreement specifies that Zuckerberg merely has to remain wherever Meta maintains its headquarters, so it’s possible that both he and the company could move together to another state. A number of California companies have left for Texas, Nevada, and other states in recent years. Google cofounders Sergey Brin and Larry Page are among the handful of billionaires widely believed to have exited California over the potential tax.

But with much of the most pioneering AI development happening in Silicon Valley, leaving the city of Menlo Park could be a difficult proposition for the Meta board to stomach. “The goal is to keep Zuckerberg continuing to lead the company from California and in close proximity to Meta Superintelligence Labs,” the filing stated, referring to the company unit behind its recent Muse AI models.

In the end, the exercise may have been for naught. Recent polling in California suggests that with about $129 million spent campaigning against Prop 40, it will fail to muster enough votes.

#531. Here's who Netflix just brought in to run content — and what it means for the streamer

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Netflix is shaking up its C-suite in an effort to fix its engagement woes.

On Thursday, the streaming giant announced that former Disney executive Peter Rice will take over as the company's chief content officer, overseeing global content organization, including scripted and unscripted series, film and live events.

Rice is replacing 10-year Netflix veteran Bela Bajaria, who most recently led the company's global content team with a focus on its live sports strategy. Bajaria was initially hired in 2016 to help the streamer launch an unscripted division as well as licensing and studio co-productions. She later transitioned to the top creative role, spearheading Netflix's $20 billion content budget.

She oversaw popular TV series like "Stranger Things," "Wednesday," "Bridgerton," "Squid Game," "Adolescence" and "Beef," as well as Netflix films like "Frankenstein" and "KPop Demon Hunters." Bajaria also helped push Netflix into live sports and events, including deals with the WWE and the NFL as well as programs like "The Roast of Tom Brady."

Prior to his stint at Disney, Rice was president of 21st Century Fox and CEO of Fox Networks Group as well as Fox's lead executive on the Hulu board. He was also the president of Fox Searchlight Pictures and helped release "Slumdog Millionaire," "Juno," "Little Miss Sunshine" and "Sideways."

The executive change follows a tumultuous period for Netflix as it faces stagnating viewership and explores avenues for growth. It comes just days after the streaming giant laid off roughly 1,000 employees, or 6% of its workforce.

In July, Netflix reported viewership increased just 2% in the first half of the year as the streamer suffers from a lack of shows with water-cooler-level hype.

Co-CEO Ted Sarandos admitted last month during the Bloomberg Screentime conference that the company is "not growing as fast as I want us to."

Those warnings, and now Bajaria's exit, have Wall Street bracing for more turbulence ahead.

"The sudden departure of Bajaria likely signals to us that user engagement may have remained soft throughout [the third quarter]," Pitz wrote.

Netflix is set to release fiscal third-quarter earnings on Tuesday.

In July, Nielsen reported that Netflix had generated 7.8% of TV viewership, while YouTube accounted for nearly double that, at 14.2%.

YouTube has been Netflix's biggest threat in the battle for viewers' time. In response, the streaming giant has sought to diversify its content in recent years, expanding into live sports, documentaries and video games in order to compete for share of consumer attention.

"If the opportunity is to recast [Netflix] into a broader content hub, the risk is missing the watercooler originals," Steven Cahall, equity analyst at Wells Fargo, wrote in a research note last month. "We see breakout hits as a must for the stock to work again."

Rice has a wealth of experience in television and film, most recently serving as the chairman of Walt Disney Television and chairman of general entertainment for the company. He managed a wide swath of channels across broadcast and streaming, including ABC, Disney Channel, Hulu and FX Networks. During his tenure, he oversaw the production of more than 300 shows, including "American Idol," "Modern Family," "Abbott Elementary," "The Bear," "Only Murders in the Building" and "Shogun."

He also headed up major sporting events such as coverage of the Super Bowl, NBA Finals and FIFA World Cup.

Under Rice's leadership, Disney earned roughly 300 Primetime Emmy nominations between 2021 and 2022. Of those nods, it won nearly 60 awards across its television networks and streaming platforms.

Meanwhile, Netflix's Emmy wins have slowed.

In 2021, it tied the record for most trophies won by an outlet in a single year with 44. This year, however, it only took home 16 prizes, its lowest Emmy haul in a decade.

With Rice, Netflix is also getting an executive with theatrical experience. After he was ousted by then-Disney CEO Bob Chapek in mid-2022, he transitioned into an independent producer role. Since 2023 he has produced films like "Saturday Night" and "28 Years Later." He's currently developing a movie adaptation of the video game "Elden Ring."

Late last year, Netflix struck a deal to buy Warner Bros. Discovery's film studio and streaming assets, only to be outbid months later by Paramount Skydance, which completed its acquisition of WBD in early October.

Some on Wall Street have speculated that the streamer may be interested in further M&A, including acquiring assets from NBCUniversal after it splits from parent company Comcast.

"[Rice's] long tenure at Fox, and then Disney after its acquisition of Fox, gives him unique insight into the consolidation of two major studios, relevant if Netflix chooses to go after another studio," Alicia Reese, senior vice president of equity research at Wedbush, wrote in a research note Thursday. "Even if it doesn't, Rice's experience fits well as Netflix pushes deeper into theatrical and builds a broader platform with varied content across the entertainment ecosystem."

Researchers think they might have pinpointed the exact night sky that we see depicted in Vincent van Gogh's iconic painting "The Starry Night."

Perhaps one of the most famous paintings in modern history, Van Gogh's "The Starry Night" carries a legacy that continues to inspire and mystify generation after generation. But while you might see in its swirls of color and bright spots of starlight a night sky derived purely from imagination, some scientists think it represents an actual view — one that includes a waning crescent moon, Venus and the Hyades cluster. And they may know exactly when this sky appeared..

In a new study, researchers suggest that "The Starry Night" depicts the pre-dawn sky seen around July 22, 1889 from Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, where Van Gogh painted it. But while this sky happens to perfectly match the one depicted in the artwork, art historians have long thought that Van Gogh painted this piece in June. Could these findings shift the date of this legendary artwork?

This investigation started decades ago with art historian Albert Boime and astronomer George Abell at UCLA in 1984 and continued in the following years with astronomer Charles Whitney at Harvard University. However, these earlier analyses did not agree with one another, and there was no true consensus about exactly when the sky matched with the painting.

So the question remained, lingering in the minds of those with a shared love for both art and space. And a team led by Elijah Jeong-hyun Kim, the CEO of South Korean robotic telescope company SLLAB and a Ph.D. candidate at Kyung Hee University's School of Space Research, picked up the search once more.

"When they see a fragment of a starry sky, observational astronomers like me and amateur astronomers alike will want to solve the puzzle. I am simply someone who pursued that process a little more actively," Kim told Space.com.

But how did Kim's team come to their conclusion, after others had failed to nail down a date over the decades? In short, "we analyzed the painting astronomically, analyzed documents about his situation in July 1889, and then carried out ecological and meteorological investigations," Kim said.

To start with, the team analyzed the sky from the exact location in Saint-Rémy where Van Gogh is said to have painted "The Starry Night." They then used observational geometry to map the historical night sky.

"Modern telescopes are equipped with a function called astrometry, which compares an image of the night sky with celestial coordinates stored in a computer and automatically extracts the coordinates of the image," Kim said. "We used Procrustes analysis, a method that compares only the 'shape' of two sets of points. After matching their position, size and rotation, it measures how similar the two shapes are from the differences that remain (the residuals)."

"Because a star cluster on the canvas may be larger or smaller than in reality and slightly tilted, this method was the right fit. It allowed us to explain, in quantitative terms, what earlier studies had described only qualitatively as 'resembling a certain constellation,'" Kim added.

But this quantitative analysis wasn't enough to say for sure that this was the right-fitting sky, as different constellations could fit to a similar degree, Kim explained. To get closer to their answer, the team combined their analysis with a deep dive through history.

"We also checked whether the stars were actually in that part of the pre-dawn sky on that date, whether they fall within the painter’s field of view, and whether the appearance of the moon and Venus matches the painting. It was the Hyades that satisfied all of these conditions," Kim said.

As mentioned, the date of this matching sky comes in late July, a month after art historians think Van Gogh painted "The Starry Night." But the researchers suggest that their findings could provide evidence to support the idea that the work was actually painted in July.

Letters written by Van Gogh as early as 1888 describe his desire to paint a star-filled sky. A letter dated April 12, 1888 reads: "A starry sky, for example, well — it’s a thing that I’d like to try to do, just as in the daytime I’ll try to paint a green meadow studded with dandelions."

Kim followed these letters through the months to try and find more information that could potentially alleviate the discrepancy between the matching skies and the actual month the painting was created. Kim pointed to a letter from between late May and early June 1889 that reads: "This morning I saw the countryside from my window a long time before sunrise with nothing but the morning star, which looked very big."

Kim drew on "personal imagination" to find ties between Van Gogh's sky observations in these months and what they might mean for the painting's timeline, Kim shared, so this doesn't definitively change the view of this painting's history. However, Kim suggested that these records could indicate that Van Gogh was repeatedly looking up and watching the pre-dawn sky, which could have led to him seeing this exact sky on July 22.

The painting itself even ignites uncertainty among art historians. As the researchers cite in their new paper, leading art historians see "The Starry Night" as stylistically different from Van Gogh's other works during the month of June, drawing further questions over the date painted.

"What our study shows is that the sky in the painting matches, in both observation and shape, the real pre-dawn sky around July 22. So, what we propose is the possibility that Vincent conceived the idea in June and completed the painting after July 22," Kim said.

However, "I think further research in art history and conservation science could shed more light on this question," Kim added.

This work was described in a study published Oct. 22 in the journal Scientific Reports.

#526. People are taking an unapproved weight-loss drug. AI found something surprising

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

An experimental weight-loss drug that has not yet received regulatory approval is already attracting users willing to obtain unapproved versions online. Now, scientists are using artificial intelligence to investigate their experiences, uncovering unexpected symptom reports that differ from those documented in clinical trials.

The drug, retatrutide, belongs to a new generation of GLP-1 medications designed to help regulate appetite and body weight. Although it remains under investigation and is not approved for use, people are purchasing unapproved formulations that are not intended for human consumption and discussing their experiences on internet forums.

Researchers at the University of Pennsylvania analyzed nearly 150,000 Reddit posts and comments from approximately 40,000 users to investigate these accounts. Their study, published in JAMA Network Open, provides the first systematic examination of potential side effects reported by people claiming to use unapproved retatrutide.

One finding stood out. Among users whose reported symptoms could be classified, increased appetite was the most common complaint. That was particularly unexpected because retatrutide is designed to suppress hunger, and its Phase 2 clinical trials primarily identified digestive problems as the most frequent side effects.

AI Uncovers Unexpected Weight-Loss Drug Reports

"Our goal is to identify hypotheses in online conversations that we could test. AI helps us find recurring patterns in people's self-reports, including experiences that may be missed by conventional drug-safety monitoring," says Sharath Chandra Guntuku, Research Associate Professor in Computer and Information Science (CIS), Director of Penn's Computational Social Listening Lab and senior author of the study.

However, the researchers emphasize that the findings should not be interpreted as proof of previously unknown side effects. Reddit users represent a very different population from clinical trial participants, and their accounts cannot establish whether the drug caused the symptoms they described.

There is also considerable uncertainty about what these individuals are actually taking. Products sold outside regulated medical channels may differ from the genuine experimental medication in their ingredients, purity, and dosage.

"We can't say that retatrutide is causing these symptoms, or even that Reddit users are actually taking genuine retatrutide, since the drug is not legally available, and gray market formulations vary," says Lyle Ungar, Professor in CIS and a co-author of the study.

Rather than confirming new risks, the research offers scientists an opportunity to identify questions that warrant more rigorous investigation.

"This is the first glimpse into the experiences of a population that is otherwise outside conventional drug safety monitoring because they are taking unapproved versions of a drug that has yet to finish clinical trials," says Neil Sehgal, the study's first author and a doctoral student in CIS.

How Researchers Analyzed 150,000 Reddit Posts

The project builds on previous research by the University of Pennsylvania team. In that earlier investigation, scientists used large language models (LLMs), the type of artificial intelligence that powers many modern chatbots, to examine more than 400,000 Reddit posts discussing semaglutide and tirzepatide.

That analysis revealed reports of possible side effects, including menstrual irregularities, that may not have been fully documented in clinical trials or official drug safety records.

"We wanted to apply the same methodology to gray-market retatrutide," says Sehgal.

Retatrutide is particularly interesting because it works differently from existing GLP-1 medications. Semaglutide acts on one hormone receptor involved in blood sugar control and appetite regulation, while tirzepatide targets two. Retatrutide activates three receptors, potentially producing a different combination of effects throughout the body.

To investigate what people were experiencing, the researchers used AI to examine nearly 150,000 Reddit posts and comments. They identified approximately 14,000 users who claimed to have personally taken an unapproved version of retatrutide.

The AI systems then extracted descriptions of symptoms that users associated with the medication and translated those descriptions into standardized medical terminology. This allowed researchers to compare reports even when different people used different words to describe similar experiences.

After excluding expected effects such as weight loss and reduced appetite, the team identified 7,823 users who reported at least one symptom that could be medically classified.

"A researcher might be able to examine a few thousand Reddit posts by hand, but looking at nearly 150,000 would be an enormous undertaking," says Sehgal. "AI makes it possible to analyze these conversations at scale, identifying patterns in what people are sharing that would otherwise be difficult to recognize."

The most striking difference involved hunger.

In retatrutide's Phase 2 clinical trials (the drug is currently in Phase 3 trials, the final step before FDA approval), participants most commonly experienced gastrointestinal complaints, including nausea. These effects are familiar concerns with medications that influence appetite and digestion.

But among Reddit users who reported taking unapproved retatrutide, the pattern looked different. Increased appetite emerged as the most frequently described classifiable symptom, despite the substantial weight-loss effects demonstrated in clinical testing.

The researchers also compared the new findings with their earlier analysis of semaglutide and tirzepatide.

Compared with those earlier Reddit reports, discussions involving unapproved retatrutide contained proportionally more mentions of appetite-related changes, sleep disturbances, increased heart rate, and muscle-related symptoms. Reports of nausea and other digestive complaints were proportionally less common.

These differences could reflect how retatrutide interacts with the body. However, they could also result from differences between the groups of people discussing the medications, their other drug use, or the contents of the unapproved products.

"Retatrutide works differently from existing GLP-1 drugs, so it's possible that its effects on the body are different, too," says Jena Shaw Tronieri, Senior Research Investigator at Penn's Center for Weight and Eating Disorders and a co-author of the study.

"But the people discussing it on Reddit also may be different," she notes. "Users willing to obtain an unapproved medication on the gray market likely differ from other clinical populations. Some may be taking multiple experimental peptides, and we can't verify exactly what they're taking. We need more research to understand what, if anything, these differences in self-reported symptoms mean."

Can AI Detect Drug Safety Concerns Earlier?

Beyond the findings about retatrutide, the study demonstrates how AI could potentially improve the early detection of drug safety concerns.

Traditional monitoring systems depend heavily on clinical trials, reports submitted to regulators, and information collected by healthcare professionals. People obtaining experimental medications through unapproved channels may not be represented in those systems.

Social media offers another source of information, allowing researchers to identify recurring experiences that might otherwise escape attention. However, these reports must be independently investigated before they can be considered reliable evidence of a drug-related problem.

The Penn team has already seen an example of how this approach can help guide further research.

In their previous investigation of semaglutide and tirzepatide, researchers identified online reports of menstrual irregularities. Those findings subsequently encouraged another scientific team to examine similar symptoms using the FDA's database of reported adverse drug events.

The follow-up study, published in Obstetrics & Gynecology, also detected disproportionate reports of certain menstrual changes. Although those findings did not establish causation, they provided additional reasons to investigate the potential connection.

The researchers hope their retatrutide findings will lead to similar independent studies, particularly as the drug advances through clinical development.

"That's what we hope will happen with this study," says Sehgal. "Other researchers can take what we find on Reddit and look for the same patterns in other datasets, using entirely different methods. Whether those signals hold up or not, the goal is to build a better understanding of what people are actually experiencing."

No conflicts of interest were reported by the other authors.

#522. A first for CRISPR: powerful prime editing alters DNA directly in the human body

The method called prime editing can make fine-scale edits to DNA (artist’s illustration).Credit: Patrick Landmann/Science Photo Library

A powerful gene-editing technique has been used to edit DNA directly in a person’s body — the first known instance of such a treatment.

The milestone treatment is part of a clinical trial of prime editing, a technique that can alter DNA with more precision than standard CRISPR–Cas9 methods can. In this case, prime editing was used to treat a condition called Wilson’s disease by rewriting DNA in liver cells, to erase a mutation that prevents them from eliminating excess copper.

Prime Medicine, a biotechnology company in Cambridge, Massachusetts, announced that it had treated the first person in its trial earlier this month. It expects to announce the first results from the study next year.

Bigger than CRISPR? A guide to the latest genome editors

Bigger than CRISPR? A guide to the latest genome editors

“I never dreamed that in my lifetime I would see this applied to my rare disease,” says Rhonda Rowland, president of the Wilson Disease Association in Atlanta, Georgia. “It’s going to be a really exciting year.”

In launching the trial, Prime Medicine joins a host of companies aiming to engineer cells directly in the body, rather than using the standard method of extracting a person’s cells, editing their DNA in a laboratory and then reinjecting the altered cells. Rewriting DNA in the body, or in vivo, is appealing because manufacturing and administering the treatment might be much cheaper and faster than making a bespoke therapy for each person from their own cells.

But in vivo approaches have their own challenges, says Krishanu Saha, a biomedical engineer at the University of Wisconsin–Madison. “It depends on what types of risks you’re willing to accept,” he says.

Wilson’s disease is caused by mutations in a gene called ATP7B, which encodes a protein that transports excess copper out of the liver. If left untreated, the resulting build-up of copper can cause liver and nerve damage, as well as neurological disorders.

People with Wilson’s disease are eager for a one-and-done treatment for their condition, says Rowland, rather than the current, complicated regimen of copper-clearing molecules and other medicines. And gene editing could, in theory, provide a lasting solution by correcting the faulty ATP7B gene.

So far, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved only one gene-editing therapy, called Casgevy, which is used to treat two inherited blood disorders. Casgevy relies on CRISPR–Cas9 gene editing, which is often used to disable genes rather than to rewrite their sequences.

Bizarre CRISPR enzyme kills cancer cells by shredding their DNA

Bizarre CRISPR enzyme kills cancer cells by shredding their DNA

Another form of gene editing, called base editing, can rewrite individual DNA letters, but cannot make the particular corrections needed to fix the most common ATP7B mutations. That left prime editing, a more complex and versatile system that can insert, delete and rewrite segments of DNA. In another clinical trial, Prime Medicine used the technique to treat two people with a rare immune disorder, by rewriting DNA in each participant’s blood stem cells outside of the body and then injecting them back in.

So far, no gene-editing therapies have been approved for use in vivo. But the FDA is expected to issue a decision early next year about an in vivo CRISPR–Cas9 therapy for a condition called hereditary angioedema, which causes severe swelling.
