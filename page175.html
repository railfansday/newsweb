#180. Attack on actor Tiger Shroff is dark reminder of Bollywood's gangster past

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Actor Tiger Shroff, who made his debut in 2014, is known for his action roles

On Sunday evening, Bollywood actor Tiger Shroff went to play football in Juhu, a leafy, affluent Mumbai neighbourhood where film stars live, fans congregate and celebrity sightings are a part of life. But as the 36-year-old was leaving the ground in his BMW, shots rang out. His driver was wounded, as was a vlogger who had come to film the actor.

Shroff, whose films usually include elaborate action sequences and choreographed escapes, had just survived an actual shooting attempt.

Four days later, the case still dominates headlines in India, with each new revelation spawning fresh theories, competing claims and a growing list of alleged conspiracies.

Police have detained or arrested 10 suspects, with investigators tracing alleged weapon deliveries through several states and examining payments made to the alleged attackers.

The attempted shooting has also brought the name of Lawrence Bishnoi, one of India's most notorious gangsters, back into the headlines. Soon after the attack, a screenshot of a social media post purportedly made by an alleged associate of the Bishnoi gang circulated online, claiming responsibility. A separate audio recording emerged in which a man identifying himself as Anil Pandit repeated the claim and said Shroff had been targeted over his alleged links to actor Salman Khan, who Bishnoi has been threatening for years.

Mumbai police have not verified the claims or established that the Bishnoi gang was behind the attempted shooting. Investigators are still trying to determine who ordered the attack and why.

The targeting of Shroff follows a series of threats and attacks involving prominent Bollywood figures, including gunfire outside Khan's home in 2024 and filmmaker Rohit Shetty's house earlier this year. Together, the incidents have revived fears about the reach of organised crime into the film industry, stirring memories of a darker era in Mumbai's history.

During the 1990s and early 2000s, the city's underworld exerted formidable influence over the film industry, financing productions, demanding protection money and sometimes using violence to enforce its demands. It took years of police operations, and the gradual arrival of institutional financing, to loosen that grip.

Now, as fresh allegations and theories emerge almost daily, a question that once seemed to belong to Mumbai's past has returned: is the underworld trying to control Bollywood again?

Watch: Bollywood actor Tiger Shroff survives shooting attempt in Mumbai

Those who confronted organised crime in Mumbai at its peak say the answer is not as straightforward as the attempt to shoot Shroff might suggest.

D Sivanandhan, who took charge of Mumbai's Crime Branch in 1998, remembers a time when a phone call from Dubai, where some of the most notorious gang leaders were based, could decide the fate of a film - who starred in it, financed it or profited from its distribution.

In an industry dependent on private money, the line between investor and extortionist could be perilously thin. And the threats were real. In 1997, music mogul Gulshan Kumar was shot dead outside a Mumbai temple. Three years later, filmmaker Rakesh Roshan survived an assassination attempt.

Sivanandhan investigated the financing of Chori Chori Chupke Chupke, a Salman Khan film whose producer and his assistant were each sentenced to six years in prison under Maharashtra's organised crime law for underworld links. The film's financier received a one-year sentence for withholding information about those links. Police seized the film's negatives, and its release eventually happened under court supervision.

Gangsters were not always imposing themselves on a reluctant industry. Some filmmakers willingly sought their money, Sivanandhan recalled, in arrangements that could benefit both sides and proved difficult for police to dismantle.

But Sivanandhan is reluctant to see the latest violence as a return of the mafia he once fought. The men he pursued - Dawood Ibrahim, Chhota Shakeel, Arun Gawli and others - belonged to criminal networks rooted in Mumbai, even when their leaders operated from abroad.

"This is not the Mumbai mafia which we know in the last 60 years," he said. "This is something else."

Gangster Lawrence Bishnoi has been locked up in prison since 2015

That something else has become inseparable from the name Lawrence Bishnoi. A gangster with roots in northern India who has been in jail for years, Bishnoi has acquired an aura of folkloric menace: a prisoner said to command gunmen across continents, his name invoked in threats and claims of responsibility that spread almost instantaneously online.

Bishnoi has publicly threatened Khan over a 1998 poaching case involving blackbucks, a species of antelope revered by the Bishnoi community. In April 2024, gunmen fired five rounds outside Khan's Mumbai home. Six months later, Baba Siddique, a politician close to Khan and other actors, was murdered. Mumbai police have linked both attacks to the Bishnoi gang, though they have not established any personal involvement by Lawrence Bishnoi in Siddique's killing.

Some allegations against his network are backed by police investigations; others remain untested. But the distinction between what the gang has demonstrably done and what is claimed in its name can become blurred in the public imagination, doing little to diminish Bishnoi's notoriety.

Rakesh Maria, a former Mumbai police commissioner, has cited Bishnoi as an example of a new generation of gangsters who are more technologically adept and internationally connected than their predecessors. "The old underworld was visible and territorial, but the new one is dispersed, fluid, making it harder to pin down," Maria told The Print, external last year. "They no longer need to reside in the city to command it."

Bishnoi's gang claimed responsibility for killing Baba Siddiqui (right) in 2024, citing his ties to Salman Khan (left)

The relationship between gangsters and Bollywood has changed in another way, too, in who gets to shape their image.

In the 1990s, Bollywood itself helped turn Mumbai's feared underworld figures into objects of fascination. Films such as Satya (1998) and Company (2002) brought a gritty, visceral realism to their world, while Manoj Bajpayee's portrayal of gangster Bhiku Mhatre in Satya became, in the words of film scholar Ashvin Immanuel Devasundaram, "mythologised and talismanic".

The fear and fascination of that era were "seared into the collective consciousness", Devasundaram told the BBC.

Each new attack brings those memories back, he added. But today's gangsters no longer need cinema to build their mythology. Social media allows them to shape their own image, circulating threats, claims of responsibility and carefully staged photographs to audiences far beyond Mumbai.

After the latest shooting, police reportedly stepped up security outside the homes of Khan and Rohit Shetty. The All Indian Cine Workers Association demanded greater protection, asking: "Today it is Tiger Shroff. Who is next?"

There is little evidence so far that criminal networks have regained their former influence over Bollywood's finances and decisions. But the latest attacks have exposed a different vulnerability.

Gangsters may no longer need to control an industry to unsettle it. A single shooting attempt, amplified across millions of phone screens, can spread fear far beyond its intended target.

Sivanandhan, who spent years confronting Mumbai's gangsters, knows the limits of even the tightest security.

"There is always a crack in the armour," he said.

#174. ‘I’ve killed people’: Beau Lamarre-Condon allegedly confessed to friend and asked for help cleaning ‘mess’, court told

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Friend tells jury Lamarre-Condon told him in early 2024 ‘I shot the first one through the head and the second one through the door’

Beau Lamarre-Condon allegedly confessed to killing two men, telling his friend it was an “out-of-body experience”, before attempting to play it off as a joke, a court has been told.

Lamarre-Condon, 31, is accused of fatally shooting couple Jesse Baird and Luke Davies with his police service weapon in Baird’s Paddington home on 19 February 2024.

He has pleaded not guilty to two counts of murder and one charge of aggravated break and enter.

Lamarre-Condon’s friend, whose identity is suppressed, told the New South Wales supreme court on Friday that the officer arrived at his workplace the day Baird and Davies were killed.

Lamarre-Condon told him “I’ve done something”, and when the friend asked for an explanation, the policeman said “I’ve killed people”, the jury was told.

“I said ‘are you fucking kidding me?’, he said ‘I wish I was’,” the friend told the court. “He said it just felt like an out-of-body experience and it just wasn’t him.”

The friend said when he asked how Lamarre-Condon did it, the officer said with a gun.

He then made a gesture with his fingers indicating a firearm and said “pew pew”, the friend told the court.

When asked for more details, Lamarre-Condon allegedly said: “I shot the first one through the head and the second one through the door.”

Lamarre-Condon then said the second person wasn’t meant to be shot but he “couldn’t leave any witnesses”, the friend told the jury.

The officer asked his friend to leave work to help “clean up his mess”, noting the bodies were heavy and he needed to buy a new door.

The friend then returned to his workplace and explained the situation to his team lead, who told him to tell Lamarre-Condon he had a meeting he couldn’t miss.

Lamarre-Condon later messaged the friend and asked “I should get an Oscar, hey?” before going on video call and saying “got you”.

The friend then got upset, saying, “it’s not a fucking joke, you’re mentally fucking with me”, the court was told.

The policeman responded: “OK it is real but I just didn’t want you to tell anyone.”

The friend told the jury that on 20 February 2024, the day after the alleged murders, the pair went to dinner and Lamarre-Condon said he needed to “throw out bloody clothes and things”.

He then made a gesture to indicate his legs were sore and said: “I didn’t realise bodies would be heavy.”

Shortly before Lamarre-Condon handed himself in to police, the friend contacted a lawyer who attempted to connect him with NSW police.

But police said: “Is it worth our time coming out? It’s really busy,” the court heard.

The lawyer responded: “You’ll want this information.”

Earlier on Friday, the friend revealed he had travelled overseas in late 2023 for a trip with Lamarre-Condon.

He said that while overseas, Lamarre-Condon told the friend: “I need to find a spot … to purchase a silencer.”

The officer explained he was going hunting on a mate’s farm, the jury was told.

The two found what they believed to be a gun store, but it had either closed down or there had been a fake ad, the friend said.

Back in Sydney, Lamarre-Condon drove his friend to a national park and took a police service weapon out of a paper bag, the jury was told.

Lamarre-Condon gave his friend a police-issued radio and asked him to listen for any reports of police movement towards them after the officer shot the firearm.

“I said ‘you can’t really shoot your police-issued firearm’. He then told me that they were training bullets he had taken previously from a police shoot they do once a year,” the friend told the court.

He said Lamarre-Condon then fired the weapon and asked his friend how loud it was.

Then, he tried again, shooting a bullet into the bushland before asking “does this make any difference on the sound?”, the jury heard.

“He’s asked me whether it would be louder or would you hear this in a house?” the friend said on Friday. “I proceeded to say ‘you could hear that anywhere, it was super loud’.”

On their way home, Lamarre-Condon then allegedly said “you can’t tell anyone about this”, the court heard.

The defence team claims Baird and Davies were accidentally shot when the accused acted in “self-defence”.

Lamarre-Condon then “panicked considerably”, cleaning up the scene and disposing of the bodies, the defence lawyer John Stratton SC said.

The trial before Justice Natalie Adams continues.

#173. Rubio turns to small Indian town as US faces high oil prices

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

US Secretary of State Marco Rubio was in India this week

The western Indian port town of Jamnagar is not a usual stop for foreign leaders, yet it featured prominently in US Secretary of State Marco Rubio's recent visit.

So, why did Rubio visit the town? And, more importantly, why did he skip the Indian capital, Delhi?

The answer to both questions may have less to do with India and more to do with Donald Trump administration's domestic compulsions â or, more precisely, its domestic oil needs.

Rubio arrived in the financial capital Mumbai on Tuesday, where he met Indian Foreign Minister S Jaishankar and some of the country's top business leaders. He officially ended his trip in Jamnagar - in the western state of Gujarat - home to the giant oil refinery owned by one of Asia's richest men, Mukesh Ambani.

It was his second trip to the country this year and experts say what is significant this time is the timing of the visit as well as his choice of destinations.

The Trump administration is under pressure to keep fuel prices in check ahead of November's US midterm elections, while rising tensions in the Middle East threaten global energy supplies.

With little new refining capacity expected in the immediate future in the US, India's ability to process large volumes of crude oil offers Washington a potential way to ease pressure on the fuel markets.

And that puts Reliance's Jamnagar refinery at the centre of the equation.

The visit, however, comes at a tricky moment in India-US relations. Trump has imposed tariffs on Indian goods, there are sanctions against Delhi for buying Russian energy (though currently paused) and there is a trade deal between the two countries that has remained elusive for around 20 months now.

Rubio (left) held talks with foreign minister S Jaishankar (right) in Mumbai

Announcing the visit, the US State Department had earlier said that it would focus on energy cooperation, commercial investment and economic ties.

The official itinerary also did not include Delhi, which raised some questions, especially from Indian opposition politicians. But analysts say the choice was strategic.

"Delhi is where the trust gap is largest. Officials there are unhappy with a number of decisions and policies from Trump's administration," Michael Kugelman, a senior fellow at the Atlantic Council, told the BBC.

But former Indian ambassador to the US, Meera Shankar, cautions against reading too much into him skipping the capital.

"This was primarily an energy-focused visit," she says. "And after all, Rubio did meet the foreign minister in Mumbai."

The meeting between the two foreign ministers at the Taj Hotel covered trade, investment, energy security and defence cooperation, according to official statements. India's foreign ministry also said both sides welcomed progress in trade negotiations and reaffirmed their commitment to concluding a deal, which has long been in the works.

But once the diplomatic meetings were over, Rubio headed to Jamnagar, where the crown jewel of the Ambani business empire is located. This came to define the trip.

Shankar says the refinery visit "showed how much importance the US is attaching to getting more refined fuel into the market before the November midterms".

Reliance's Jamnagar complex can process about 1.4 million barrels of crude oil a day, roughly 1.5% of global refining capacity, according to the company website.

It is also a major exporter of diesel and other petroleum products, giving it a crucial role in global fuel markets.

During a tightly controlled visit on Wednesday, with limited media access, Rubio toured the facility and met members of the Ambani family, including Mukesh Ambani.

The company later released a statement, saying, "the visit included wide-ranging discussions on the global energy landscape and opportunities for commercial partnerships across conventional and new energy". It, however, did not specifically address any commitments to buy crude oil or supply refined products.

Just a day earlier, while speaking to the press, Rubio had called India a "refining superpower" and said the US wanted Reliance to process more Venezuelan oil.

"They have the capability to refine heavy crude. They've done so in the past, and I think as more and more Venezuelan oil comes online, that's something they could do," he told reporters.

Rubio added that oil from Venezuela "can certainly provide relief" in the short term and can possibly help ease supply pressures amid tensions in the Middle East.

Reliance was once among the largest buyers of Venezuelan crude before US sanctions disrupted trade. After Venezuelan President NicolÃ¡s Maduro was captured in a US intervention earlier this year and Washington eased sanctions on the country's energy sector, Reliance re-emerged as a major importer.

Rubio's visit to the Jamnagar refinery was closed to the media, with images released afterwards by the Reliance Group

Experts also say the visit might ease some tensions in the Delhi-Washington relationship over India's purchase of Russian oil.

India became one of the biggest buyers of Russian oil since Moscow invaded Ukraine in 2022.

Between December 2022 and July 2026, it accounted for 37% of Russia's crude oil exports, according to the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air (CREA).

And Reliance was among the Indian companies that processed Russian crude.

In August 2025, US President Donald Trump imposed an additional 25% tariff on Indian goods over its Russian oil purchases, taking total tariffs on many Indian goods to 50%. The US, however, withdrew the additional penalty in February 2026 but India's import of Russian oil has continued to be an irritant in Washington-Delhi ties.

India has always said that securing affordable and reliable energy for its 1.4 billion people was its priority.

Then earlier this month, the US itself temporarily lifted sanctions on Russian diesel.

In that context, experts say Rubio's Jamnagar visit is aligned to three goals that the US appears to be pursuing: reducing India's dependence on Russian crude, encouraging greater imports of Venezuelan and American oil, and advancing a trade deal in which energy cooperation could become a key area of agreement.

There is also another possible business angle behind this push.

Kugelman says Rubio had two reasons to meet the Ambani family. First, to secure greater refining of Venezuelan oil and second to seek assurances that Reliance remained interested in backing a proposed Texas refinery.

In March, Trump announced a proposed refinery at the Port of Brownsville in Texas on his Truth Social platform. He said that it would be the first major new refinery in the US in 50 years.

Reliance has not publicly commented on it and there are no details on the project or when the work is expected to begin.

"The Texas facility may not be as immediately important to US interests as the arrangement tied to Venezuelan oil, but I still think it's part of the story," he says.

Trump hopes to bring down gas prices as the US heads to midterms next month

However, analysts point out that closer cooperation with Reliance and progress on energy security do not necessarily mean a trade deal is imminent.

"I don't think anyone really expected a trade deal to be announced at this point, but this trip seems to have helped get the relationship back on track, to the extent that it can, given the significant points of tension that remain," Kugelman said.

Rajani Sinha, the chief economist at credit rating agency CareEdge, told the BBC that greater cooperation could allay energy security concerns in both countries, help push the stalled trade negotiations forward and "boost trade and sentiment" in India.

Meera Shankar says that India is possibly "waiting for the US to formalise its tariff positions so that we know whether Delhi has a preferential trade deal".

She adds that, although ties between the two countries have been strained in recent times, their bilateral trade continues to grow.

India's merchandise exports to the US was $50.81bn between April and September, up 11.1% from the previous year, according to India's ministry of commerce. Its imports rose to $32.33bn from $25.66bn in the same period. India's merchandise trade surplus with the US narrowed to $18.48bn from $20.07bn a year earlier.

"The economic relationship remains strong, but a long period of uncertainty could eventually affect it," Shankar said.

So, for now, the visit may have possibly helped get the relationship back on track, but whether that translates into a trade agreement or a lasting shift in energy ties remains to be seen.

#172. Canva releases its first image generation model

Design platform Canva released its first image generation model, called Canva Craft, developed by its research team to compete with same-category models from Google, OpenAI, ByteDance, and Meta.

The company said its model works in two stages. In the first stage, it interprets users’ prompts and creates a plan with details like spatial relationships of elements, relative scale, visual hierarchy, and information for text generation. In the second stage, its model generates an image of up to 2K resolution in a possible 30 different aspect ratios for various platforms.

Canva said that this approach allows it to create images with accurate details of objects and legible text. Plus, users would be able to make precise edits without changing other parts of the image.

The company noted that rather than using photographs, it used designs to train this model using human-designed templates from Canva’s library, including posters, social posts, presentations, infographics, menus, and typography-rich layouts.

That means images generated with Canva Craft could be converted into layered designs using Canva Magic, the company’s image-to-design transformer. Last year, the platform released its own model that understands designs and layers.

Canva Craft is available on the company’s platform to all users, who can access the model from the Dream Lab tab. The model will also be available through the Canva API. Plus, the company plans to support image generation in third-party chatbots like ChatGPT and Gemini through Canva’s MCP.

Canva has used a bunch of image models for generation in its platform, so it’s likely that the new models will help save API costs for the company. It said that besides costs, the new model also allows the platform to have better quality and speed control.

In 2024, Canva acquired media generation platform Leonardo.ai. It said that the researchers from Leonardo contributed to the new image model.

Canva said that it is developing two cost-efficient versions of the model, called Craftfast and Craft-ultrafast, which will be released soon.

The company was on track for $4 billion in revenue last year. However, it cut its growth forecast for 2026, causing its valuation to reportedly plummet from $42 billion to $35 billion, according to external analysts.

#165. Hispanic voters helped bring Trump to power - in Texas, the tide may be turning

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Father Roy Snipes is known locally as the Cowboy Priest - he resisted plans to seize chapel land to build Trump's border wall

It was just before dawn in the Rio Grande Valley in Texas and the congregation were arriving for the Friday service at the tiny La Lomita Catholic Chapel.

The whitewashed building, which has been here since the days of the American Civil War, rises from the surrounding scrub and sits about 200 yards from the Rio Grande river itself.

As cars pulled up to the chapel, the sound of Father Roy Snipes' barking dogs signalled his arrival. He has been the priest here for almost half a century - and is known locally as the Cowboy Priest.

"I'm not sure why I picked that up, but it's nice," he said of the nickname.

In truth, it is pretty obvious why. Snipes wears a Stetson in place of the more traditional priest's hat. And moments later, the chapel was filled by the sound of a Roy Rogers song - the singer and actor known as the King of the Cowboys.

During the first Trump administration, Snipes resisted plans to seize the chapel's land to build the US president's border wall. Eight years on, at the early Friday service, he focused his short sermon on the actions of Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE).

"There's no good excuse to detain, incarcerate and deport poor, good folks who are our neighbours," Snipes told the congregation of about 30, mainly Hispanic worshippers.

Texas accounted for roughly one-third of all ICE arrests nationwide in July 2026 - a larger share than any other state - according to government figures obtained by the Deportation Data Project.

"They said we're gonna seek out, capture, detain and deport the worst of the worst," Snipes said in his sermon. "Well, we're here on the border and the cartel is the worst-of-the-worst. So we thought, well, that's a good thing.

"But it turned out, you can hear almost every day, some poor guy going to work and they stop him and detain him and start a big fight."

After the service, two members of the congregation stopped to chat. "We're educators, and we have a lot of students that are having to go back because their parents are getting deported," one said.

"The cruelty that we're seeing - I think that's turning the tide, at least I hope so," their friend added.

Turning the tide politically is what Democrats are hoping they can do in Texas in the midterm elections on 3 November. But that will not be easy.

This stretch of the Rio Grande was once one of the busiest crossing points on the US-Mexico border. Under former President Joe Biden, hundreds of thousands of migrants came through here, with more than two and a half million apprehensions recorded in a single year.

Trump promised to stop these illegal crossings during his 2024 election campaign, and in large parts he has delivered.

Border crossings are now at their lowest level in decades. And it was that promise which in part drove Trump's astonishingly high level of support in the state, taking around 55% of Hispanic votes in Texas alone - a record for a Republican presidential candidate.

Javier Villalobos is the Republican Mayor of McAllen, one of the biggest cities in the Rio Grande Valley. He expects Republicans will find it tough this time around, but credits the Trump administration with stemming the border problem.

"I remember I put out a videoâ¦ thanking the administration for immediately securing the border. That was great," he said. "And I think generally most people are okay with the deportation of criminals, violent people, or people that really are not productive at all."

But if you start deporting people that are productive and law-abiding, that is where the problem is, he added.

Mario Guerrero voted for Trump three times in a row - but is planning to vote Democrat in November

Recent polls suggest much of Trump's support among Hispanic voters has ebbed away since 2024. Surveys taken since late summer indicate a majority of Texan Hispanic voters disapprove of the president. They also suggest Hispanic voters are favouring Democrats over Republicans by a significant margin.

"When I voted for President Trump, I voted for him because we saw an influx of immigration during the Biden administration," said Mario Guerrero, a young Mexican-American businessman who leads the South Texas Builders Association. It needed to be fixed, he said.

"We thought that we were in the right by voting for Trump, and that he was going to do what was best in order to fix the crisis on the border.

"And I think to myself, we were really not told the extreme that they were willing to go to make a point," he added, before referencing recent shootings involving ICE agents in US cities.

Guerrero is a reminder that, for many who live and work close to the US-Mexico border, security and immigration enforcement are two distinct issues.

A voter here may be supportive of securing the border and stopping illegal crossings, but oppose the actions of ICE and how the administration is enforcing its crackdown on undocumented migration.

Guerrero voted for Trump three times in a row - but is planning to vote Democrat in November, in part due to his unease with that crackdown.

Even though Guerrero plans to vote Democrat, he describes himself as neither Democrat nor Republican. And that, according to Mike Madrid, a leading Republican consultant and expert on Hispanic voters, is key to understanding the largest ethnic demographic voting group in the country.

"Latinos are more likely than any other racial or ethnic group to identify as independents rather than Republicans or Democrats," he said.

It was in part the belief that Hispanic voters were shifting permanently towards the Republican Party that led Trump to press states such as Texas to redraw their congressional maps early, before the once-a-decade census.

The strategy was intended to capitalise on what Republicans saw as a realignment in Hispanic voting behaviour, particularly in border districts that had been Democratic strongholds for more than a century.

Texas's 15th congressional district was among those that were redrawn, and has become one of the clearest tests of whether electoral maps can guarantee political outcomes.

The seat was redrawn after the 2020 census and was won by a Republican in 2022. It was redrawn again in 2025, creating a notional 18-point majority for Republicans in these midterms. Even so, current polling suggests a Democratic lead of five or six points.

Bobby Pulido is the Democratic candidate hoping to win the district. As a popular Tejano singer and winner of two Latin Grammys, he is already a household name in the Rio Grande Valley.

At an event just weeks before the vote, he delivered a stock political speech followed by a musical performance. "A lot of people are disillusioned with both parties," he told the BBC shortly before he took the stage. "And I hear them."

To win in this part of Texas, you need to be a conservative. Pulido has distanced himself from the national Democratic Party brand and focused deliberately and consistently on economic issues.

"This isn't New York, this isn't LA," he said. "When you lead with issues like social justice, climate change and other topics, that's not what people want to hear first. They want to hear about the economy."

That message is consistent with what many current polls are suggesting - the economy, and the cost of living specifically, rank top on the list of most voters' priorities.

At a local avocado festival, a fruit that is a staple among Hispanic communities in the valley, a couple, Dave Baldwin and Veronica Salazar, began discussing rising prices as soon as they were asked about the upcoming elections.

When asked who they blame for spiking costs, the answer was swift. "The president," they said.

"He is in control of our destiny with all that, and right now he's not doing a very good job," added Baldwin.

"The midterms are just purely a game of turnout," he said. "It's not trying so much to convince individuals to vote one way or the other, it's about trying to get our people mostly to show up."

Tano Tijerina, the Republican candidate in the neighbouring, Democratic-held 28th district, credited Trump with "breaking through barriers" but acknowledged the "national political headwinds are real".

The Hispanic shift towards President Trump in 2024 was one of the most striking political facts of the past decade. While this expanding demographic may not have much love for either party, in a two-party system voters are mostly forced to make a choice.

And if polling is correct - and there is no guarantee of that - even a modest swing back to Democrats in November could reshape races not just across this state but far beyond.

In Texas, it could even be enough to put a US Senate seat in play - and Democrats haven't won one of those in the state in more than 30 years.

Concerns have swirled over the access that consumers are increasingly entrusting to AI agents. Now, security services are trying to catch up.

Password manager 1Password on Thursday launched a new tool by which users can allow agents to log into their personal accounts on their behalf, without the agent ever having access to a user’s credentials.

The new platform, dubbed 1Password for Agents, accomplishes this by creating a system of communication between the agent, the user, and the password manager. When an agent attempts to log in on a user’s behalf, a unique digital key is sent to 1Password to confirm the agent’s digital identity. The user is then sent an alert about the login attempt and can personally confirm the request. 1Password then signs in the agent, filling in the credentials on the digital assistant’s behalf, which allows it to log in and complete whatever task is at hand.

1Password for Agents allows users to choose their agent of choice — whether that’s Claude Duet from Anthropic, OpenAI’s Dots or ChatGPT Work, Grok Bot, Meta’s Muse, or agents from Town, which is an AI assistant startup. The company says it is working with other companies to expand the service.

“Handing over a password, secret, or credential is a common shortcut to an agent, but can leave people with sensitive information in places they can no longer control as a copy gets saved for the next time,” the company said in a blog post. “1Password for Agents gives people a trusted way to delegate tasks to personal AI agents while keeping control of the passwords, secrets, and credentials those tasks require.”

Online agentic activity has increasingly unsettled major institutions. Indeed, 1Password’s new platform is launching on the same day that Axios reports that U.S. banks have begun to detect agentic activity inside user accounts.

This isn’t necessarily a case of rogue agents pilfering accounts but, rather, an example of how consumers are increasingly allowing automated services to act on their behalf, including on financial matters. However, the potential for agent-related problems is still large. The big issue appears to be that most banks cannot tell if the agentic activity is tied to user requests or not.

#161. Greece joins countries putting age-limits on social media, banning access for kids under 15

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Greece has passed legislation banning social media access for children under 15. New rules will come into effect on January 1, 2027, and are designed to protect children's mental health.

A bill to "ban the use of online social media services by minors" was passed by a majority vote of the Greek parliament Thursday and will go into effect on January 1, 2027.

The New Democracy party was the only bloc in parliament to support the measure; while opposition parties SYRIZA, Greek Solution, KKE, Niki and Freedom Course voted against it, and PASOK abstained.

Minister of Digital Governance and Artificial Intelligence Dimitris Papastergiou called the step, "necessary, effective, proportionate and EU-law-compliant," according to Greek newspaper Kathimerini, adding that social media platforms would have until the first of the year to, "determine whether the accounts they host and the services they provide are intended for adults or not."

Pointing to similar age limits on alcohol and tobacco sales to minors, Papastergiou said lawmakers had no time to wait for Brussels to pass "the perfect European law," saying, "let's send the message that the problem of uncontrolled internet use — which leads children to addiction at the expense of their mental health — cannot continue."

The move to put legal age limits on social media use began last year in Australia, with numerous countries around the world swiftly following suit.

Greece is one of seven EU countries seeking to introduce standards for age-verification on social media, which proponents argue will curb the "endless scrolling" that deprives kids of sleep, destroys their attention span and ability to concentrate and keeps them from playing outdoors and spending time with their friends.

Papastergiou defended the use of an app known as "Kid's Wallet" as a means to that end, saying that it had been established according to codes and technical specifications set by the EU.

The minister was emphatic that the app, "does not provide any personal data whatsoever about children or users, other than whether they are over or under 15 years of age — not even their date of birth."

"We owe our children their childhood," said Papastergiou. "That is what we are doing today. We have a duty to protect them. Given the current challenges, the social media bill delivers what society has been waiting for."
