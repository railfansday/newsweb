#3603. Grok releases the first seven minutes of its AI-generated ‘Odyssey’

Three months after Elon Musk declared that Grok would release a full-length, “historically accurate” movie version of “The Odyssey,” Grok has published a whole seven minutes, which the company is now calling “Episode 1 of our Odyssey series.”

Musk had previously lashed out at Christopher Nolan’s version of “The Odyssey”  because Nolan cast nonwhite and transgender actors. And Nolan is, in many ways, a perfect foil for Musk — while SpaceX and Tesla CEO hypes up data centers in space and posts an unending stream of AI-generated content, Nolan has described himself as “a techno-skeptic” and said that before AI, he’d never seen a technology “so completely rejected by the public.”

And while Nolan’s version of “The Odyssey” certainly used plenty of visual effects, it’s primarily a feat of practical filmmaking, with a shoot spanning six countries and a cast and crew of thousands. After its release in July, the film became an extraordinary box office success, selling out Imax screenings for months and becoming the likely front-runner at the next Academy Awards.

As for the Grok version of “The Odyssey” (created in collaboration with Wonder Studios and director Dev Walker), well, you can watch for yourself.

Watch Episode 1 of our Odyssey series made with Grok Imagine.Let us know in the comments if you'd like a full breakdown of how it was done! Creative collaboration with @WonderStudiosX and director Dev Walker @glumlot_ pic.twitter.com/xpgpkqSCpq

I’ll admit it’s not as spectacularly ugly as I’d expected, and certainly more impressive than other videos that Musk has posted recently. If I didn’t know better, there were moments when I might have thought these were real actors sailing across a real sea. And while I can’t speak to Grok’s historical accuracy, it does seem that some fudging was necessary, since no one’s speaking in ancient Greek. But hey, all the characters come in a Musk-approved skin color, while the narrator uses a British accent that will sound familiar to anyone who’s played a fantasy video game or watched a generic Hollywood epic from the past 25 years.

What the footage really lacks is any sense of artistry, inspiration, or human interest. The backstory — delivered via the aforementioned voiceover — seems to go on forever, finally giving way to one bearded face after another, each shouting stilted lines that make Nolan’s questionable dialogue (“Our age of bronze is collapsing!”) sound like Shakespeare.

While a few individual shots might provide a reasonable simulacrum of reality, as a whole, the clip feels completely airless, offering viewers no sense of momentum, no reason to care about anyone or anything on-screen. And where I had no trouble paying attention for the nearly three hours of Nolan’s “Odyssey,” I was listlessly checking how much time I had left before the Grok version was even halfway through. If it was a video game cutscene, I would have hit the “skip” button.

It’s hard to imagine being interested in another seven minutes of this, much less a full movie’s worth.

#3453. Serbia election: Vucic concedes defeat to student-led movement

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

In a seismic political shift, Serbia's longtime strongman leader Aleksandar Vucic has conceded defeat to a student-led opposition movement in the country's parliamentary elections.

Serbia's longtime leader Aleksandar Vucic conceded defeat to a student-led movement in the country's parliamentary elections on Sunday in what represents a seismic political shift.

High turnout and growing tension marked a hotly contested parliamentary election that tuned into a showdown between the Balkan country's longtime leader and a diverse movement of university students who have led protests against him for almost two years.

The result brings an end to more than a decade of rule by Vucic and his Serbian Progressive Party (SNS).

Pre-election polls had suggested a tight race between the SNS and the newly formed, youth-backed party Students' List, which emerged from a mass student protest movement.

lija Srdanovic, a physician who leads the student movement's list of 250 candidates, said after the results came in that "We are moving toward a more-civilized and better Serbia."

"Based on a stratified sample, it is clear that we lost these elections," Vucic said as he addressed supporters at SNS headquarters in Belgrade.

In his concession speech, Vucic said projections had the youth-backed Students' List taking between 135 and 140 seats, whereas the SNS was projected to win around 105.

Vucic blamed Serbia's independent media for the loss, while also alleging interference from unnamed European countries and the "US deep state," which he claimed, without elaborating, is also "against President [Donald] Trump." Vucic also has maintained close ties with Russia and President Vladimir Putin throughout his tenure.

Celebrations erupted on the streets of the capital as soon as Vucic conceded defeat. Passing cars honked and crowds began to party near the election commission's headquarters.

At Students' List headquarters, supporters cried tears of joy, cheering, hugging and chanting — "Victory! Victory!"

Sunday's result is the culmination of nearly two years of protests after the deadly collapse of a train station canopy in the city of Novi ‌Sad in 2024.

The disaster killed 16 people and stirred widespread anger over alleged corruption, poor construction standards and a lack of accountability for public infrastructure projects.

Protesters accused the government of allowing corruption and political patronage to undermine public safety, allegations authorities have rejected.

Demonstrations demanding accountability have ultimately snowballed into a movement larger than anything Serbia has experienced since the fall of Slobodan Milosevic in 2000.

What began with university students staging blockades and marches soon drew support from teachers, farmers and other segments of Serbian society, turning demands for justice over the disaster into a broader challenge to Vucic's grip on power.

SNS won parliamentary elections in 2012 and has dominated Serbian politics ever since. Vucic himself first came to power as prime minister in 2014 before becoming president in 2017.

After calling snap elections last month, he stepped down as president to run for prime minister.

His second presidential term was due to expire in May 2027, and Serbia's constitution prevents him from serving a third term.

Critics have accused him of concentrating power, undermining independent media and weakening democratic institutions. He and his party deny the allegations.

The student movement has managed to channel the energy of the street protests into an electoral challenge the likes of which neither Vucic and his ruling party nor Serbia's established opposition has ever seen.

​Serbia's election commission put turnout at nearly 70%, the highest since the breakup of Yugoslavia in 1992.

The election was closely watched in Europe, where Serbia is a candidate for European Union membership.

EU Commission President Ursula von der Leyen congratulated the Students' List on their victory. "We stand ready to support reforms on the rule of law, media freedom, the fight against corruption and strong democratic institutions, and to work together to advance Serbia's future in the European Union," von der Leyen said on X.

Vucic has also refused to join Western sanctions against Moscow over its invasion of Ukraine.

The vote comes six months after Peter Magyar's victory in Hungary , when he defeated Vucic's ally, Viktor Orban. Magyar congratulated Serbia’s student-led Students’ List on its victory in a post on X.

Serbia's bid to join the European Union has been hampered by concerns over democratic standards and the rule of law. But the election result has prompted a positive response from another European, non-EU institution — the Council of Europe.

"The Council of Europe stands ready to work with Serbia’s democratically elected leadership to strengthen human rights, democracy and the rule of law," the organization’s Secretary-General Alain Berset said on X.

#4250. U.S. measles cases more than double what they were last year as outbreak spreads

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

A sign is posted outside a Pennsylvania Department of Health measles vaccination clinic located in Lancaster County in Peach Bottom, Pa., Tuesday, Oct. 20, 2026. Matt Rourke/AP hide caption

The U.S. is grappling with its worst resurgence of measles in decades, with cases particularly high in the state of Pennsylvania.

New data from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention shows there have been more than 4,580 measles cases reported in 2026 across the U.S. as of Oct. 22, which is more than double the number for the whole of 2025.

The vaccination status of 96% of those who contracted measles in 2026 was "Unvaccinated or Unknown," according to data from the CDC.

A measles outbreak across Pennsylvania has grown as officials urge residents to get fully vaccinated against the disease.

At least 1,338 cases of measles have been confirmed in 44 counties across the state so far this year, according to the Pennsylvania Department of Health on Friday. There were 21 new measles cases as of Oct. 21. More than 250 people have been hospitalized and 5 people have died, the department also said.

In Lancaster and Mifflin counties, two of the counties with the highest number of cases, local Amish communities, which have low vaccination rates, are being impacted the most. Of the five who have died, three lived in Lancaster County

On Thursday, Pennsylvania Secretary of Health Dr. Debra Bogen held a roundtable with Mifflin County healthcare providers to assess their needs in combatting the disease.

"Our state metrics and data tell part of the story of the measles outbreak in Pennsylvania, but listening to the people on the ground caring for people in affected communities like Mifflin County is essential to refining our public health response," Bogen said in a statement.

One of the best ways to protect oneself from getting the measles is vaccination, according to health officials. Two doses of measles, mumps, and rubella vaccine known as MMR, is nearly 97% effective against measles, according to the CDC. One dose is about 93% effective.

In Pennsylvania, the health department said healthcare providers have seen more residents interested in getting vaccinated for measles. Across the state, more than 39,000 measles vaccines were administered during the first three weeks of October – higher than an average of 25,000 vaccines administered across the state in one month, the health department said.

Earlier this month, New York Gov. Kathy Hochul declared a disaster emergency after rising measles cases. There have been over 160 confirmed measles across the state, according to Oct. 20 data from the New York State Department of Health.

Utah has also experienced a high number of measles cases. More than 700 people in Utah have been diagnosed with measles this year, according to Oct. 20 data from the Utah Department of Health and Human Services. There have not been any measles diagnoses in the state in 3 weeks, according to the department.

In South Carolina, which was dealing with an outbreak earlier this year, there have been at least 997 measles cases in 2026, according to the South Carolina Department of Public Health. The outbreak ended in April.

A previous version of this article wrongly stated that 4,580 measles cases had been reported since Oct. 22 across the U.S. That is in fact the number reported until Oct. 22 for 2026.

#3611. ‘Cyberselfish’ author takes aim at techno-libertarianism as her book returns from the dead

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Disrupt doors open Oct. 13. Get your pass and bring someone with you at 50% off. REGISTER NOW.

It’s hard for books about technology to age well. They always risk becoming outdated as companies rise and fall, and as one hot new product is replaced by the next.

But last year, “Nerd Reich” author Gil Durán declared that it was time to revive the career of Paulina Borsook, who he said “saw it all very clearly and tried to warn us 25 years ago.” Later that fall, The New York Times reported that Borsook’s “Cyberselfish” — first published in 2000 — was finding new fans who want to understand why the tech industry “lost its way.”

Now, long after going out of print, “Cyberselfish” is returning in a new edition from Tin House.

Borsook told me in a Zoom interview that the book is meant to be “almost a travelogue” of her attempts to understand what she calls the techno-libertarian influence on Silicon Valley culture.

“That’s a 12-year-old view of the world,” Borsook said, describing it as “a kind of basket of beliefs” that’s “violently anti-regulation,” despite the fact that “no sector of society had benefited more and suffered less from the government than these guys.” In her telling, “even after the dot-com crash, that attitude was just there, percolating around, contaminating the aquifers. It was uncorrected, and it just spread out more and more.”

Borsook has yet to publish a second book, and she said that after “Cyberselfish” was first released, “no one was interested in anything I might have to say about anything, tech or not tech, forever after.”

But last year, she began to notice that prices for used copies were beginning to creep up, and she was starting to see “very out-of-the-blue, I-did-not-know-these-people posts on social media about how important this book was,” as well as inquiries from “European academics,” eventually leading to more press coverage and the new Tin House edition, with a new introduction by Durán.

Keep reading for our full conversation, edited for length and clarity, in which Borsook discusses AI, how Silicon Valley has transformed in the past 25 years, and what advice she has for young people seeking a career in tech.

Do you want to talk a little bit about how your interest in techno-libertarianism started, and how you decided that there was a book in this?

Everything with me is always inadvertent. I started knocking around tech circles, however we define it, in the early-middle ‘80s, in the Bay Area. And then I would say, starting around maybe the early ‘90s, I would hear these — to me — very weird, offhand remarks that some people would make that I couldn’t make any sense out of.

It’s not my nature to argue with people. It’s my nature to listen, and so I didn’t have the term for it except libertarian. But it seemed very screwy because initially, I grew up in Pasadena, which was [the home of the] Caltech-JPL space program, and so the parents of the kids I knew were another STEM culture. When I first got into Silicon Valley, I thought, “Oh, this is just another STEM culture like I’m familiar with.” And those guys [at Caltech and JPL]  had a very clear sense of what the government can do for you, were not into enlightened self-interest. It was a different personality type, and they certainly didn’t self-anoint with how glorious and grand and magisterial they were, even though they were doing great things.

I was just listening, I guess, and being confused. Even then, I knew no sector of society had benefited more and suffered less from the government than these guys. So why are they acting like this?

So, backstory: My agent picked me up right after my MFA in Columbia, and he said to me, “You know, it’s really hard to get fiction published. Do you have a nonfiction book in you?” And I said, “Well, there’s this weird subculture that I’ve been observing that I don’t understand, and any time I don’t understand something, I want to explore it.” That got me down the rabbit hole of looking at this stuff and writing about it, and I consider it really almost a travelogue, in the sense of “Black Lamb and Gray Falcon” or that kind of stuff, almost more than, “This is a polemic.” It’s more like: snapshot, snapshot, snapshot.

I just fell into it, and also I’m sufficiently narcissistic that I felt I was uniquely qualified to write about this, and no one else was paying any attention, and that was very important to me.

I haven’t done a backread of all the other books written about the tech industry before “Cyberselfish.” My sense is that you do recognize, say, reading Steven Levy’s work that there is this alternative philosophy, libertarian streak running through Silicon Valley. But no one really pulled it out and put it under the microscope in that way.

That’s really true. And then most of the writing — certainly in the ’90s, but maybe later — was very celebratory. It wasn’t, “Wait a minute, what’s going on here?”

I heard [New York Times reporter] John Markoff give a talk about six months ago, and he said something I really liked. He said, “Silicon Valley is a multicultural place.” By which he didn’t mean your country of origin. What he meant is there’s all these different kinds of cultures in there, and he was always more focused on these countercultural [elements,] that kind of thing.

I’m never saying that wasn’t there. I’m just saying there was this other thing that I was more interested in. So they’re running in parallel. I don’t even think they’re in conflict.

It sounds like you don’t see the techno-libertarian side of it emerging from the counterculture, but rather [as] distinct cultures within Silicon Valley.

One of the distinctions I made in — I call it TDB, that damn book — is that distinction I made between Ravers and Gilders, and the Gilders being the social conservatives. I named them after George Gilder, who was this repellent thought leader of the early ’90s, very conservative, very reactionary, very backward-looking. And then the Ravers, in homage to John Perry Barlow, drugs and sex and rock and roll, “we’re going to Burning Man,” much more culturally liberal.

And that distinction has held up, because you have that in contemporary Silicon Valley. You have these Christian nationalists and conservative Catholics in positions of leadership, and then you also have the Burners and people having interesting experimentations with sexuality and drugs and the [effective altruist] and AI worlds. And so, what’s the counterculture bit of it? Maybe the personal freedom aspect of it is countercultural.

Another weird thing we always have to point out is that the [San Francisco] peninsula votes blue, still. Ro Khanna is the congressman from there. So I’m really talking about culture rather than electoral politics. [And I’m talking about] the leadership class here, the tech overlords. It’s not so clear to me how much the rank and file are like this, though probably more than when I was more familiar with what was going on there.

Because I think a lot of people have been attracted to work in crypto or AI, and they’re not thinking about the greater political surround. But they’ll drink whatever they’re handed, and they don’t much care.

We can point to people who are in charge, who have these [right-wing] beliefs or are allied with the Trump administration. There’s another executive class that maybe doesn’t have exactly the same beliefs, but is able to make themselves very comfortable with it.

And then you have that same diversity that you were talking about. There’s different communities, different cultures underneath. I’ve certainly spoken to people who work for someone like Elon Musk, and they’re like, “Yeah, I don’t love this guy, but right now, I can’t afford to lose this job.”

Right, I think there’s a lot more of that going on than in times past, because there’s greater precarity in terms of employment, and so people [say,] “I’ve got a job, I’m going to hold on to it.” […] Or, “I don’t espouse the beliefs that they’re giving out, but my project is okay.” Those kinds of things always go on.

For you folks who aren’t familiar with the book and maybe have a general sense that there is a libertarian strain in Silicon Valley, can you say more why — if somebody thinks, “I believe in technology being liberatory, and maybe it can free us from the shackles of government bureaucracy and things like that,” why is techno-libertarianism a bad thing?

First of all, that’s a 12-year-old view of the world.

I didn’t really have a good word, but it was a kind of basket of beliefs, and now we’d say maybe a cloud, and it was violently anti-regulation. It was bad borrowing from biology. “Let’s just let things evolve and they will be fine.” They have no idea how much government has subsidized and made possible their life, their world, all the R&D.

Government can do bad things. I’m an old hippie. I know about the war in Vietnam. I know what the CIA did with Allende. So I don’t want to come across [as if] I think it’s all okay. I don’t. But this sector of society has less to complain about.

And one thing that just makes me grit my teeth right now is a lot of these guys are moving to Nevada to live on the Nevada side of Tahoe, because they want to escape California taxes. Sergey [Brin] saying, “California is turning into Soviet Russia.” Are you nuts? Because the thing is, they all love Tahoe. Why do they think the water of Tahoe is so pure and blue? That is decades of government intervention and interference, environmental scientists, regulation. I mean, there’s this inability to connect the dots and lack of history.

To what extent is what we’re seeing now with the tech industry, with their alignment with the Trump administration, with all these other dystopian things going on, how much of that is a shift? And to what extent is this all one story, continuous with what you were seeing before?

Unfortunately, I see more the continuity. Excuse me for [my own] bad borrowing from biology, but […] I really see the Netscape IPO is when things really began to change, and Silicon Valley was invaded by the finance bros, and a real change in attitude. You got the culture of flip and flee, all that kind of stuff. Even after the dot-com crash, that attitude was just there, percolating around, contaminating the aquifers. It was uncorrected, and it just spread out more and more.

When you have things like Airbnb or Uber, which are strictly financial plays, they’re not technology innovation by my definition, but there was too much investment money in the world that didn’t know where to go. It would invest in these things, and these people would parrot this language. So I thought, “Ah, this is left over from 15 years ago,” in terms of their self-justification.

And the way that something like Airbnb fought so hard against any kind of regulation and anything that would ask them to be any kind of a good community actor in the communities they basically destroyed, in a lot of ways, in terms of the housing market. It’s like, “Yeah, that’s that attitude I was talking about way back then.” It’s like Giardia in the water system.

You can say, with good reason, that there was a lot of wonderful stuff that came out, and all the children of General Magic and all the great things that they created. I would never say that’s not true, but I’m not talking about that. I’m talking about the business culture of these people, how they see themselves. Sociopathy is a business virtue in a way that was never there before.

How do you see the rise of AI through that lens?

It’s perfect. “We don’t need to be regulated. We’re the most brilliant, genius, wonderful people in the world.” I see AI right now as a big Ponzi scheme.

You probably saw that really horrible ad that Apple did a couple of years ago, for one of their new computers, and they had all the musical instruments and all writing and [it all went into] the hydraulic press. How did Apple become so tone deaf? This is not the Apple that all the quote “creative class” loved for so many years. It’s not the Apple of the 1984 ad. But they still see themselves in this liberatory way.

If you buy the stockholder theory of value, it justifies whatever the AI companies are doing because of the stock play, not because it’s intrinsically valuable.

In the last couple of years, [“Cyberselfish”] has had this renaissance, leading up to the current reissue. When was that something that you started to notice?

This was maybe an exercise in masochism, but a couple of times a year, I’d look at the used book platforms, and it was selling for three bucks, which means that the seller is making more money on the shipping and handling than on the book, which is really humiliating.

I would say starting maybe a year and a half ago, I began to notice the prices are creeping up — not hugely, initially, but they were creeping up. And also, I was beginning to be contacted by European academics who wanted to talk to me. And I thought, “How did you even hear about this?” And then there were some very out-of-the-blue, I-did-not-know-these-people posts on social media about how important this book was, it shouldn’t have been forgotten, this is wrong.

Long story short, I got led to Gil Durán, who wrote “The Nerd Reich,” and then we went for lunch, and we realized we are kindred spirits. Around that same time, Steven Levy, who was working on a piece for Wired about “How did I get it so wrong?” — and of course, I’m sitting here thinking, “Yeah, Steven, how did you?” —  Steven contacted Gil and talked to him for 45 minutes, and Gil said, “You should really talk to Paulina, she’s been tracking this stuff forever.”

So Steven never quoted Gil. He never contacted me. And when Steven’s piece came Gil put this incredible rant on Bluesky saying I got everything right. “We really need to support her. Let’s try to bring this book back into print.” And between that and the podcast I did with him, all of a sudden, you could not get a copy of TDB for love or money.

[In the new edition,] there’s the new introduction by Gil, but otherwise it’s the book as it appeared [in 2000.]

Absolutely, and then I wrote a little introduction, going into more, “This is how this came back from the dead.” The acquiring editor at Tin House, she said to me, “Don’t change a word,” which is exactly what I wanted to hear. Because I think it’s an artifact of a time and place.

And with a book like this, if you start thinking, “Well, should we update this? Should we add to that?” it turns into a different book. People were glomming on to the book as it was. They weren’t saying, “Oh, it would be great if we updated this.” So, no, it’s not updated.

Hopefully, it’s not naive of me to imagine that there are people who might read this interview, read your book, and they are mostly on-board with what you’re saying about the tech industry and techno-libertarianism. Maybe they work at some big tech company [or] they have a startup themselves, and they’re like, “I don’t agree with the Peter Thiels and Elon Musks of the world.” Do you think that those people can reform or change the tech industry from within?

I want it to be true, because one of the questions that I’m getting these days, and I wish I could answer it better, is running into young people, they’re in CS or engineering or something, and they want a career in that, and they don’t buy this toxic stuff. “What can we do?”

And I go, “I’m not sure, because first of all, AI is sucking up all the investment money right now. So if you want to do almost anything, if it’s not AI-related, you can’t do it. Or a military-industrial thing. Government is so broke right now. Can you get a job in government? I’m not sure. Even the state governments, or I’d say the nonprofit sector, but that’s not doing well, either.”

I have this frantic thing of, I adore these kids and I want to support them and I don’t know what to tell them to do. It used to be that you could start a small company, a small consultancy, just you and a friend or two, and it didn’t have to elephantise as a grotesque growth, you could just bootstrap, but the revenue supported the growth, and you could do that. I’m not too sure how much of that world still exists. I think during the app era, you could also do that. I’m not sure now, but I never want to underestimate the both desperation and ingenuity of people, and so it may be possible.

I think your larger question — which is, do I think these big companies can be reformed from within — I think all it takes is a change. They’re amoral enough that if it’s not in their interest to link arms with the MAGA thing, they’ll do something different. I’m very cynical about it.

But I think the people who might be thinking about what used to be called appropriate technology, I’d love to see those people get funded or find a company that does that. It could be possible — whether they can do it within the venue of the hyperscalers, I don’t know, but I would hope that they could spin off and maybe do something else.

#4042. 6 stellar images of the early universe

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Published Oct 25, 2026 8:01 PM EDT

Millions of miles away from Earth, the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and Nancy Roman Space Telescope are revealing the early days of our vast universe in extraordinary detail. A team at the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian have developed Lumina, one of the largest and most detailed simulations ever created of the early universe.

The new model is detailed in a study published today in the journal Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society. It recreates how ordinary matter, dark matter, and radiation all interacted during the universe’s first 2 billion years. This gives scientists a new tool to test out their theories about how the cosmos evolved after the big bang. Comparing observations from JWST, Roman, and other telescopes with Lumina’s predictions, also can help astronomers determine whether their understanding of the laws governing our universe remain accurate or if new discoveries require them to reconsider existing theories.

Creating Lumina required more than 15,000 graphics processing units. Take a peak at some of Lumina’s stellar images and videos in the gallery below. (Click to expand images to full screen.)

“With JWST, the early universe has become an exciting new frontier in astrophysics,” study lead author Oliver Zier, a computation astronomer and ITC Fellow at the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian, said in a statement. “To understand what we are seeing with this telescope, we need to translate our theories into detailed predictions. After several years of work, with plenty of ups and downs, it is incredibly rewarding to see the simulation results and watch our predictions come to life in video.”

Lumina is the largest cosmological radiation-hydrodynamics simulation ever created. This kind of simulation models how the universe’s fundamental ingredients interact to form stars and galaxies. These ingredients include dark matter, light, and gas. Lumina is tracing how the earliest galaxies emerged and transformed the vast regions of gas between them over the universe’s first 2 billion years.

“We feel incredibly fortunate to be building simulations like Lumina,” added Aaron Smith, an assistant professor of physics at the University of Texas at Dallas and study co-author. “For the first time, we can follow along as both hydrogen and helium changed into their modern form across such an enormous volume of the universe, while still resolving the galaxies and other luminous objects driving that transformation.”

I know many of you are mourning the loss of the Arc browser, and I get it. It felt like the first app in a long time to really offer a new approach to browser design. Atlassian bought the company last year and shifted its focus to developing Dia, its even more AI-focused browser. And soon enough, Arc will officially be no more, leaving dedicated users in a lurch. But I abandoned Arc quite some time ago for the Zen Browser and haven’t really missed it. If you’re willing to deal with some minor inconveniences, I think you’ll find Zen is the Arc replacement you’re looking for.

Zen unabashedly rips off all the best bits of Arc. Visually, they’re almost indistinguishable. It has the launcher-like pinned sites, tab bar on the left, siloed spaces, and mods that let you customize the browser’s UI to your liking. It takes the bits that made Arc’s interface feel fresh, then strips out everything that made it bloated and drove me into the arms of another.

There’s no agent in a sidebar hounding me to let it handle tasks. I’m not worried about Zen training an LLM on my browsing activity. It’s just a web browser.

Zen prides itself on its privacy features: “Beautifully designed, privacy-focused, and packed with features. We care about your experience, not your data,” it proudly proclaims. Now, I’m no fool. I know that nothing I do on the internet is really private, but with Arc, it always felt like my data was an integral part of the business plan. That’s not the case with Zen. It’s a free and open-source project that relies on volunteers and donations.

In addition to Zen Mods that can do anything from tweak the look of an active tab to overhaul how PiP videos are handled, you can also install Firefox extensions. Yes, you can install Chrome extensions in Arc, but anecdotally, I’ve found that Zen handles extensions better. Both Zen and Arc are different enough from a UI perspective that not all extensions display properly, but Zen offers more workarounds.

Zen also just kind of lets me focus on the task at hand. Links open in “glance” view, which is a temporary pop-over tab, which is really great when you’re doing some window shopping or light research. If I want to open it in a proper tab, it’s just a click away. Zen also has a floating bar that pops up whenever you press Command+T or CTRL+T. From here you can launch a new tab, switch to an existing tab, perform a search from the search engine of your choice, and even trigger an extension.

Zen isn’t perfect. I love its clean UI and organizational features, but it can’t play DRM-protected video content right now. That means Netflix, Spotify, HBO, Disney+, and more do not work in Zen. The Zen Team is working to obtain a Widevine license from Google. In the meantime, I use Chrome when I want to watch a movie, which is fine. Zen is built on Firefox, and Firefox’s Web MIDI support is lacking. So I have to fire up Chrome semi-regularly anyway.

I also miss Arc’s Boosts in some cases, but I’ve learned to accept the web as it is. Zen implemented a similar Boost feature in May, but it’s not super obvious and, if there is an official, easily browsable repository of installable Boosts, I’ve yet to find it. Instead, you have to tweak individual sites’ appearance yourself.

If you tried Zen a long time ago and were turned off by its buggy early builds, rest assured it’s come a long way. Look, if you are all in on Arc’s AI features, Dia is probably more your speed. Personally, I always loved Arc’s interface, but never really dug the vibes. Zen just feels right.

#4094. Tiny Atomic Defects Reveal a Quantum Secret Hidden Inside an Unusual Metal

📅 25 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Scientists at Columbia University have discovered that electron waves can become trapped inside a metal, even when pathways remain available for them to escape. The finding, published in Science, provides the first experimental evidence of a long-predicted quantum phenomenon in a metallic material.

The trapped waves were found near atomic defects in a metal called Pd5AlI2. Unlike electrons that move freely through conducting materials, these waves remain confined because of quantum interference.

“Bound electrons shouldn’t occur in a metal, but they do in Pd5AlI2. What we have observed is the first clear evidence of quantum interference trapping a state in a metal,” said Columbia theoretical physicist Raquel Queiroz.

Quantum mechanics describes electrons as waves that can travel along multiple possible paths. When these waves overlap, they can reinforce or cancel one another. Under certain conditions, destructive interference can prevent an electron wave from spreading, even when other electron states can move through the same material.

This phenomenon is called a bound state in the continuum (BIC). “Quantum mechanics predicts that an electron wave can, theoretically, remain trapped even when there are infinitely many paths for it to escape. This effect is known from light waves, but it has never been seen before for electrons,” said Queiroz.

The discovery began in Columbia chemist Xavier Roy’s laboratory, where researchers developed Pd5AlI2, a metal that can be separated into thin, two-dimensional layers while retaining its metallic properties. Measurements showed that its electrons moved more slowly than expected.

Working with Queiroz and experimental physicists Cory Dean and Aravind Devarakonda (a former Columbia postdoc who is now an assistant professor of applied physics at Columbia), the team traced this behavior to electron hopping frustration. The metal’s atomic structure creates competing paths for electrons to move between atoms, allowing destructive interference to suppress their movement. The findings were published in Nature Physics.

Researchers working with Columbia physicist Abhay Pasupathy later detected unexpected patterns while examining Pd5AlI2 using atomic-scale scanning and spectroscopy techniques. Further analysis revealed electron waves trapped near defects in the crystal lattice. Queiroz developed theoretical models explaining how quantum interference produced these localized states.

Bound states in the continuum have previously been demonstrated with light, where they have helped advance lasers, optical sensing, and nonlinear optics. The Columbia findings establish that a similar effect can occur with electrons in metals.

“Trapping a wave is an idea as old as quantum mechanics itself, but it took almost a century to figure out how to do it with light. We have now realized it with electrons in a material,” said Roy. “And if you trap a particle, you can manipulate it.”

Electronic bound states could eventually contribute to highly sensitive quantum sensors, quantum information processing, and quantum chemistry. However, controlling their formation remains a challenge.

In Pd5AlI2, the electron traps form around defects that arise randomly during crystal growth. Roy and Pasupathy are now investigating ways to deliberately position these defects and control the resulting interference patterns. The discovery also offers a way to identify other metals with frustrated crystal structures that could support trapped electron waves.

The owners of Elvis Presley’s music catalogue are suing the artificial intelligence (AI) company Anthropic for allegedly using Presley’s songs to train their models without permission.

Authentic Brands Group, the licensing organization that has ownership of both Presley’s music and likeness, is accusing Anthropic of infringing on the rights of 274 songs by Presley. The lawsuit, first reported by Billboard, was filed on Friday.

While AI firms have already faced copyright infringement claims from the Recording Industry Association of America (RIAA) and music labels such as Sony, Universal and Warner Records, this case represents one of the first major legal challenges brought directly by an artist’s estate against an AI developer. The result could set a broader precedent for how AI companies are allowed to use existing music catalogues.

“The Elvis plaintiffs bring this action to enforce these exclusive rights against the defendant that has built its entire business model based on copyright infringement at a scale never seen before,” lead attorney Richard Busch wrote in the lawsuit.

The lawsuit claims that “from its inception, Anthropic’s AI model, ‘Claude,’ has created infringing output of copyrighted material of virtually every song from every catalog available on the internet”.

It further accuses Anthropic of utilizing 274 of Presley’s songs to train its AI model. These works include Can’t Help Falling in Love, All Shook Up and A Little Less Conversation. The plaintiffs are seeking $150,000 per infringed track, or about $41m (about £31m).
