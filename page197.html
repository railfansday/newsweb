Carlson has broken with Trump largely over the war in Iran. But on Ukraine and immigration, the two speak very much the same language.

Republican presidential nominee and former president Donald Trump shakes hands with Tucker Carlson at the conclusion of a conversation during Carlson’s Live Tour at the Desert Diamond Arena on October 31, 2024.

Earlier this month, Tucker Carlson performed a miracle: In a podcast conversation with the United Kingdom politician Jacob Rees-Mogg, a hard-right figure who was one of the architects of Brexit and who has been outspoken in his criticism of immigration, Carlson made Rees-Mogg appear both reasonable and moderate.

The two began sparring first over support for Ukraine, with Carlson coming up with a series of rationales for Russia’s war that could have come straight from a Kremlin talking-points memo, ridiculing Western governments for their ongoing support of Ukraine. From there, he moved to defending Hitler’s invasion of Poland in 1939, mocking the UK’s decision to go to war against the Third Reich, and declaring that it wouldn’t have been such a bad thing if the Germans had emerged victorious from the conflict. He argued that mass immigration today is a far bigger threat to the UK’s survival than were the Nazis and accused successive British governments of abandoning their obligations by not sending the military in to repel the “invasion” of immigrants arriving from across the English Channel.

One can, in this interview, see Rees-Mogg physically recoil in horror as Carlson picks up steam, and then gird himself to give the podcaster a history lesson and to issue what ended up being a quite stirring defense of the necessity to defeat Hitlerism.

Carlson has, these days, broken with his onetime BFF Trump, largely over the war in Iran. But on Ukraine and immigration, the two speak very much the same language. Both see immigrants as invaders and want to train military firepower on poor, non-white, non-Christian migrants who have the temerity to dream of a better life. In Trump’s case, he is presiding over ICE operations that, increasingly, resemble death squads—the weapons of choice being guns and cars. Day after day, stories emerge of ICE agents methodically mowing down immigrants and protesters with their SUVs, the most recent case in Woodland, California, where a man filming ICE agents was knocked down by a car in what looks like a deliberate hit and run (thankfully, he survived)

The methods of sadism being implemented as official government policy wouldn’t have been out of place in Nazi experimenter Josef Mengele’s tool kit of horrors. Trump has also lost no time in blaming the recent riots in France on “migrants, jihadists, and Communists who hate Christianity and Western civilization itself,” remarks that French President Macron felt compelled to denounce as “unacceptable and stupid.”

But it’s not only in their loathing of immigrants that Carlson and Trump are fascist birds of a feather. The duo also sees Ukraine as a duplicitous nation that has somehow conned the West into a futile battle with an ascendant Russia, and at the same time see Putin’s leadership as a template for the sort of illiberal, Christian-nationalist country, led by strongman leaders, that they intend America to become.

For years now, Carlson has gone out of his way to praise Putin, and, as detailed in a recent Mother Jones piece, over the past year he has resorted to increasingly shrill defenses of Russia’s arguments in favor of the invasion of Ukraine. Trump, too, no longer bothers to hide his pro-Russian sentiments. #3573. “NAZA” and Israel’s History of Automated Warfare

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

As violent technologies like spyware, predictive targeting algorithms, and 2,000-pound warheads become ordinary instruments of military rule, once-abhorred practices become routine.

IDF intelligence illustrating warfare tactics in Naza.

On July 22, 2002, an Israeli Air Force pilot dropped a one-ton bomb on a block of apartments, four stories tall, in the Karkash neighborhood of Gaza City. The target was Sheikh Salah Shehadeh, one of the founders of Hamas’s Izzedine al-Qassem brigades. The operation flattened the residential building he called home, killing him, his aide, his wife, three sons, and nine other civilians; 150 more were injured. It was the latest outcome of a relatively new Israeli military strategy adopted at the height of the second intifada: aerial assassinations.

In Israel, the carnage set off a wave of dissent among Air Force pilots. Some had begun to question what, exactly, the air strikes were achieving. For months, Palestinian testimonies from the West Bank and Gaza, reprinted in major global newspapers, had telegraphed horrific scenes: shrapnel slicing through the torsos of bystanders; students taken out while exiting their school. The operation that day in June was, however, a tipping point. Twenty-seven IDF pilots penned a letter refusing to play a part in operations they deemed “illegal and immoral.” Thousands more rallied in support. It was the first coordinated protest among Israeli pilots against such policies in the occupied Palestinian territories. It was also the last.

NAZA, a new documentary by Yuval Abraham and Rachel Szor, the Israeli half of the team behind the Oscar-winning No Other Land, helps elucidate why, offering a portrait of the technological war machine that developed in the years since. Composed of interviews with anonymous reservists and officers from Israeli units responsible for planning and coordinating air strikes, the film digs into how Israel’s military carried out one of the most destructive air campaigns in modern history in the weeks following October 7, 2023. The documentary’s name is not a combination of “Nazi” and “Gaza” as some reviews have insinuated, but rather taken from the Hebrew acronym for collateral damage. Previous reporting on the film has focused on the dystopian array of machine-learning technologies used to kill Palestinians—one officer calls Israel’s air war the “perfect use of AI.” Less remarked upon, however, though no less consequential, is how NAZA showcases this arsenal’s political effects on the country’s liberal camps: how Israel’s embrace of automated warfare has made policies that once seemed exceptionally cruel, like intentionally killing dozens of innocents as they sleep in their beds, feel routine to the intelligence officers who helped coordinate Israeli air strikes in Gaza. And with them, how ideologies once relegated to the margins of political discourse have become mainstream.

For those who have been paying attention to reporting on Israeli military operations in Gaza for the last three years, NAZA’s revelations are not exactly new. Palestinians in Gaza have been documenting the astronomical toll of these air strikes since October 2023. Abraham and others have also reported on the operational details, like how the army hacks children’s phones with spyware to kill a Hamas operative and his family and military lawyers sign off on these operations. What is new with NAZA, though, is the way the film articulates these points: through testimonies from secular, ostensibly liberal young Israelis who served in elite military units. The film’s subjects sit on Tel Aviv rooftops, describing how they helped destroy Gaza from a distance, with the click of a button. “Fun” is how one officer describes the work of coordinating air strikes that kill families sleeping in their homes. “I understand how a man can blow someone up and go home to his degree in clinical psychology,” another officer admits. “It was like a video game,” still another tells Abraham. “Everything was done remotely.”

It would be amiss to view these technological developments in a vacuum; they are part of Israel’s shift to the far right over the last few decades. The IDF began to adapt these systems en masse in the 2000s. The land, sea, and air blockade that began after Hamas was voted into power in 2006 spurred a dramatic change in the form of Israel’s military rule over the Gaza Strip. Israel’s army poured resources into expanding the use of drones, cell phone tracking systems, and wiretaps prototyped and refined during the Second Intifada. The idea was to leverage new military technologies to maintain Israeli control without deploying boots on the ground. Intelligence units—whose conscripts were often plucked from Israel’s affluent urban centers—tracked down members of Hamas and Islamic Jihad by listening to their cell phones, hacking into their e-mails, or poring over aerial images of Palestinian cities, compiling them into long lists of military targets for drone or fighter jet pilots to bomb.

The effects on the ground were gruesome and bloody, but the technology at the disposal of airforce and intelligence units meant that many involved—unlike their compatriots patrolling the West Bank—were increasingly shielded from its effects. The names of targets were displayed in spreadsheets, the coordinates of their mobile phones as blinking dots on a virtual map, and they were killed by munitions launched from armed drones, hovering hundreds of feet in the air. “It reminds me of a Play Station game,” one intelligence operative told a Haaretz reporter after Operation Cast Lead back in 2009, a 22-day assault on the Gaza strip that killed 1,400 Palestinians, an unprecedented number. “You hear cheers in the war room after you see on the screens that the missile hit a target.”

Though Operation Cast Lead was roundly condemned by institutions like the United Nations and International Criminal Court, following the event the Israeli military only set about making its surveillance and targeting arsenal more potent. Israeli conscripts developed predictive algorithms to synthesize the reams of data taken from the Palestinian territories, where Israel’s control of the telecommunications infrastructure and airspace give soldiers panoptic powers. NAZA describes one named Lavender, which culls through automatically translated and transcribed calls, text messages, social-media content, and location history stored on phones to assign Palestinians in Gaza scores, which, in turn, determine whether someone is a military “target” or a member of Hamas or Islamic Jihad’s armed wings.

Technological innovations often go hand in hand with political ones. With time, long lists of algorithmically generated military targets helped make wars on Gaza more frequent and gruesome: By 2021 they had become an annual event. Israel’s growing right-wing camps were galvanized by these spectacular displays of military force. Politicians like Benjamin Netanuyahu and Itamar Ben Gvir boasted to their supporters of expanding lists of Palestinians killed, who had taken to shouting “death to Arabs” at rallies. Meanwhile, the daily violence necessary to maintain Israel’s occupation in the West Bank, with settler vigilantes killing Palestinians outside their homes and burning their agricultural lands, armed soldiers searching high school students’ phones at makeshift checkpoints or raiding homes in the dead of night, allowed a militant strain of Jewish supremacy to creep from the margins of Israeli political discourse into the mainstream.

Among Israel’s shrinking left-wing camp, the same arsenal helped the possibility of unending military rule disappear from view. Soldiers from Israel’s more liberal constituencies circulated from elite technological units into high-paying jobs at multinational technology firms setting up shop in central Israel. They might have gotten their start using spyware to hack into Palestinians’ phones or tracking a reaper drone as it zeroed in on a house in Gaza city, but on LinkedIn the work was reframed as “user experience research” or “development operations.” Though violence gestated a few miles away, as settlers torched Palestinian agricultural lands in the West Bank and air strikes pummeled Gaza, an influx of foreign capital transformed cities like Tel Aviv into hermetically sealed environments.

That seemed to change on October 7, 2023. The atrocities Hamas inflicted were an unprecedented national trauma, one that Israelis from across its ever-narrowing political spectrum desired to avenge. The technological arsenal detailed in NAZA allowed the army to act with unrestrained force on that day. One officer in the film describes how, though Lavender had a 15 percent error rate, soldiers were instructed to use it to mark even the lowest-ranking Hamas operatives out for assassination. Another describes using a program called “Where’s Daddy?” that alerted him when targets returned to their family homes by automatically flagging messages from their children. A third explains how this arsenal allowed the army to systematically kill hundreds of Hamas operatives as they slept alongside their wives and children at night.

On camera, the officers Abraham interviews sketch out the user-friendly interfaces they used to coordinate destruction. Some had no qualms about the unprecedented bloodshed, while others explain how specific military policies helped rationalize skyrocketing civilian casualties. As various investigations have shown, directives from the upper echelons of Israel’s government to wage war without restraint meant that military lawyers authorized strikes that could kill anywhere from tens to hundreds at a time, all in accordance with the army’s flexible interpretations of laws of war.

Reporting by Palestinians in Gaza has long evinced the cost these weapons, and the policies that come with them, exact on their victims. If that was not enough, NAZA now makes the consequences for their perpetrators impossible to ignore. When extraordinarily violent technologies—like spyware, predictive targeting algorithms, and 2,000 pound warheads—become ordinary instruments of military rule and occupation, practices that once seemed abhorrent, like dropping a bomb on a house full of children, become routine. The result is the Israel featured in the documentary: a place where 18-year-old soldiers dispatch drones to blow up houses with families inside of them in between espresso breaks at a shopping mall.

NAZA should be a warning to other countries—if it is not already too late. The United States military forged its AI arsenal through two decades of drone warfare in Iraq, Afghanistan, Pakistan, Syria, and beyond, where the killing of unarmed civilians by pilots sitting on airbases in Nevada was routine. It was released in full force in Iran, as large language models offered up long lists of targets for drones and fighter pilots to strike in operations that left hundreds of civilians dead. Today, many Americans rally behind politicians who champion unrestrained force and expansionist policies, parroting Israel’s own brand of hawkish nationalism. Others simply do not have to think about the gruesome outcomes of their country’s military adventurism, even those who engineer the algorithms now being used to determine where the Pentagon drops bombs.

The protests that rocked Israel back in 2002 were summarily condemned by IDF officials in the months to follow. Speaking to reporters later on that year, Dan Halutz, then the commander of the Israeli Air Force, famously told his soldiers they could “sleep well at night” whilst carrying out aerial strikes in Gaza or the West Bank, even when the mission might entail bombing civilians. They should trust the chain of command, from intelligence analysts who hunted down purported militants to commanders who reviewed the data and dispatched a pilot to kill. “When I release a bomb,” Halutz said, “I feel a light bump to the plane as a result of the bomb’s release. A second later, it’s gone. That’s all. That is what I feel.” NAZA showcases the technical arsenal that helped generations of soldiers heed his advice.

#4163. Long After Maui Fire, Residents Found With High Levels of Toxic Metals in Their Bodies

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

This article was originally published by Grist, in partnership with The Intercept, and is reproduced here as part of the Climate Desk collaboration.

Damage from the 2023 fire that destroyed the Hawaiian town of Lahaina. Matt McClain / The Washington Post via Getty Images via Grist

More than a year after a devastating wildfire destroyed the Hawaiian town of Lahaina, residents were found to have high levels of arsenic and other toxic metals in their bodies.

In August of 2023, a series of wildfires broke out across Maui, killing more than 100 people, destroying thousands of homes, and causing billions of dollars in damage. Noelani Ahia, who is Indigenous Kanaka Maoli and cofounder of Maui Medic Healers Hui, helped mobilize nearly 600 volunteers in Lahaina, including doctors, nurses, and therapists.

Three years later, the housing and health crises remain acute. Maui Medic Healers Hui runs a free clinic, but Ahia wishes it had 10 more to address the community’s needs. “The fire may be out, but the catastrophe is not over by any stretch of the imagination,” she said.

The devastation extended well beyond the lives lost and buildings destroyed. Research published last year by researchers with the University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa’s Maui Wildfire Exposure Study, or MauiWES, found that more than 1 in 5 residents had reduced lung function.

Now, new research from the same project has found elevated levels of toxic metals in the island’s residents months after the fires, with higher exposure associated with impaired lung function. The findings offer further evidence that the health consequences of catastrophic wildfires can persist long after the flames are extinguished and the smoke clears.

“What we consider and sometimes look at as a one-time event isn’t really one-time anymore,” Alika Maunakea, who is Kanaka Maoli and codirector of MauiWES, told Grist.

Researchers don’t yet know how much of the contamination came from the fires and how much was already present in the environment. That distinction matters as wildfires increasingly race through towns like Lahaina and cities like Los Angeles, where burning of homes, cars, and infrastructure can leave contaminants that people encounter for months or years afterward.

“We have traditionally thought about wildfire exposure primarily through the lens of smoke,” Maunakea said in a press release. “But when an entire community burns, we are dealing with a much more complicated exposure environment.” Researchers must also understand what contaminants were already present, what the fire may have released, and what entered residents’ bodies, he said.

The research, which studied 1,400 adults across Maui six to 18 months after the fires, measured 24 metals in urine samples. It found that participants had higher concentrations of several metals than people in a nationwide health survey conducted before the fires. That included 2.2 times as much arsenic, 2.3 times as much barium, and 3.8 times as much manganese. Antimony, which is used in flame retardant materials and has been previously linked to lung irritation, stomach pain, and other symptoms, was found at levels nearly 40 times higher.

Because the researchers lack comparable data from before the fires, they could not determine how much of the metal exposure resulted from the disaster. Maunakea told Grist that answering that question is the next step for the project, which has received a $3.6 million grant from the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences. That work will include investigating whether and to what degree past agricultural and industrial activity contributed to the contamination.

“It’s important to realize that these are all part of settler colonialism,” Ahia said. “So what we’re looking at is confounding factors that are to the detriment of the health of our local people.”

Decades of plantation agriculture altered water systems, introduced herbicides, and displaced native plants, leaving a legacy on the islands that is still felt today. Disasters like fires and storms can compound those ongoing environmental problems.

“If we begin the story when the fire starts, we miss the decisions that shaped the landscape and the community’s ability to respond,” said Katie Kamelamela, who is Indigenous Kanaka Maoli and an assistant professor at Arizona State University based in Hawaii. “Micro climate change acts on places already shaped by histories of land ownership, water diversion, development, and unequal access to resources.”

Kamelamela said true recovery must look beyond the fire. “I want recovery to be measured by whether people can remain in their communities, care for ʻāina, and pass on their knowledge and practices in place,” she said, using the Hawaiian word for land, which carries deeper meaning and relationship than property or mere place. “Rebuilding should not recreate the same conditions that left people at risk. Caring for ʻāina and caring for people need to be funded and planned together.”

Maunakea said the multitude of intersecting pressures is one reason why he wanted to pursue this research. As climate change continues to accelerate, longstanding issues facing Native Hawaiians like housing, the harms of tourism, and the legacy of plantations can amplify problems. “We worry that disasters like this that we experience on Maui, as well as the floods and other storms that we’re experiencing now, is just going to continue widening the gaps in health disparities that already exist in those populations,” he said.

Moving forward, the project will not just identify health risks but also help residents respond to them. Study participants receive their results, discuss them, and meet with health professionals. “People now know that there’s these types of potential hazards, and what to do about them is a big concern,” Maunakea said. “We still don’t have all the answers for sure, but we are taking the next steps to get a better picture and inform our community around it.”

#3592. The Decades-Long Battle Within the Democratic Party Establishment Between the Heirs of Dean Acheson and Franklin Roosevelt

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

The calamitous history of America’s wars of choice.

As Trump’s disastrous war against Iran continues to grind on, it might benefit the incoming class of Democratic policymakers who seem poised to take over not just the House but possibly the Senate to reflect on the calamitous history of Washington’s wars of choice. The first such war was President Harry Truman’s, in Korea, begun by North Korean forces in June 1950. As Nation contributing editor James W. Carden shows in The Great Betrayal: How the Democrats Became the Party of War, Truman and his hawkish secretary of state, Dean Acheson, steered the party in a direction not intended by Truman’s predecessor, Franklin D. Roosevelt. Truman and Acheson’s embrace of the burgeoning national security state and their adoption of the paranoiac anti-communism that heretofore had been characteristic of the American right led them headlong into an unnecessary war of choice in Korea. Korea set the precedent for Vietnam, for Iraq, and for Iran—it was the first of many wars waged in the absence of constitutional authorization on the flimsiest of pretexts.

The invasion of South Korea by North Korea’s Kim Il Sung provided Washington’s Cold War planners with just the story they needed at just the right time. As President Harry Truman reached the midpoint of his first elected term, he surprised some of his Defense and State Department planners by not immediately accepting his national security staff’s more hawkish recommendations. “We were sweating over it,” one Truman adviser admitted later, “then thank god Korea came along.”

The idea that South Korea represented a core US national security interest was a novel one. In fact, there was, in the years and months leading up to the conflict, broad agreement among US policymakers that it represented no such thing. At Yalta, a four-power trusteeship was created to oversee the former Japanese colony. In April 1948, Truman and the Joint Chiefs of Staff (JCS) admitted that Korea was “of little strategic value to the United States.” In a speech on January 12, 1950, Acheson famously left both Korea and Formosa (Taiwan) outside the boundaries of the US defense perimeter in East Asia. North Korea launched its attack on June 24, and even then the Joint Chiefs of Staff warned that it would be “militarily unsound” for Washington to commit large forces “in an area of slight strategic importance.”

But once the war was joined, Truman made the ever-dubious historical parallel with Neville Chamberlain’s decision to “appease” Hitler at Munich. “The free nations have learned the fateful lesson of the 1930’s. That lesson is that aggression must be met firmly. Appeasement leads only to further aggression and ultimately to war,” declared Truman on July 19. Truman later claimed that upon learning of North Korea’s invasion, he “remembered how each time that the democracies failed to act, it had encouraged the aggressors to keep going ahead. Communism was acting in Korea just as Hitler, Mussolini and the Japanese had acted ten, fifteen and twenty years earlier.”

“If this was allowed to go unchallenged,” he continued, “it would mean a third world war, just as similar incidents had brought on a second world war.” In his seminal study on the “Origins of the Cold War,” the realist scholar Hans Morgenthau noted, “The misinterpretation of the North Korean aggression as part of a grand design at world conquest originating in and controlled by Moscow resulted in a drastic militarization of the cold war.” Indeed, Truman and Acheson viewed Asia solely through the prism of the Cold War. Not surprisingly, Acheson blamed the Russians for starting the war, but there is no evidence the project originated with Stalin.

Dean Rusk, an Acheson protégé then serving as assistant secretary of state for the Far East, blithely dismissed China as a puppet of the Soviets. China, in Rusk’s view, was merely a “colonial Russian government, a Slavic Manchukuo on a large-scale.” Acheson also thought he saw Moscow’s hand behind Ho Chi Minh’s insurgency in Vietnam. Acheson worried that Ho might establish a “communist-dominated, Moscow-oriented” regime. For Acheson and his disciples like Rusk, Moscow’s was the not-so-hidden hand behind every problem everywhere.

Paranoia is a poor substitute for strategy, but driven by their fears of a monolithic communist movement, Truman and Acheson provided $15 million to France to bolster its efforts against Ho’s insurgency. Truman also created a US Military Assistance Advisory Group, Indochina, to provide material support and advisers to the French forces fighting the Viet Minh. On October 9, Army Brigadier General Francis G. Brink arrived in Saigon with a contingent of 65 American troops—the first of many more to come. As a recent, and sympathetic, Acheson biographer admits, Acheson “was in the vanguard in leading the United States into Indochina’s Big Muddy.” As with many of his Cold War policies, Truman’s intervention in Vietnam was diametrically opposed to the intentions of his predecessor.

At the Tehran Conference, Roosevelt said the Allies should not fight to restore Vietnam to the French—in his view, after 100 years of French colonialism, the Vietnamese were worse off than ever. In Bohlen’s judgment, Roosevelt’s views on Vietnam reflected the president’s “strong dislike of colonialism,” which Roosevelt frequently expressed. Yet by the time Truman and Acheson left office, Washington was funding nearly half of France’s costs in Vietnam. Ultimately, these decisions set in motion a war that would eventually kill upwards of 3.5 million Vietnamese and 58,000 American soldiers, sailors, and airmen.

By 1952, Truman was a deeply unpopular and polarizing figure, so much so that he was defeated in the New Hampshire primary by a margin of 11 points (55 percent to 44 percent) by Tennessee Senator Estes Kefauver. By the end of March, he had abandoned his bid for reelection. Truman left office with a 32 percent approval rating, the second-lowest approval rating for a departing president after Richard Nixon, who had sunk to 24 percent in 1974. Yet in the three-quarters of a century since leaving office, Truman’s reputation has witnessed a remarkable turnaround. By the time the new century dawned, Truman’s decision to drop the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki; his headlong rush into a Cold War with the Soviet Union; and his unconstitutional war of choice in Korea were all treated as acts of statesmanship by both popular historians and the Democratic elite. In September 2000, President Bill Clinton presided over a ceremony naming the headquarters of the State Department in honor of Truman. “Thanks, in no small measure to President Truman, we have won the Cold War,” said Clinton.

Truman’s stock has only risen further in the quarter-century since Clinton spoke those words. A Siena College survey released in 2022 ranked Truman as the seventh-greatest president. The 2024 Presidential Greatness Project at the University of Houston ranked Truman as the sixth-greatest. There are precious few dissenters from the pro-Truman consensus. Among the most astringent was the novelist and critic Gore Vidal, who noted, “The inexorable beatification of Harry Truman is now an important aspect of our evolving imperial system.”

But it was Acheson who lent an intellectual sheen and respectability to Truman’s parochialism. It was he, not Truman, who, as the main driver behind institutions like NATO and programs like the ERA, spawned generations of imitators among the Democratic foreign policy cognoscenti. Kennedy’s secretary of state, Dean Rusk, learned at his knee; Clinton’s secretary of state, Madeleine Albright, said Acheson was her model. Republicans also now line up to praise him. Henry Kissinger observed in 2006—and perhaps with not a little envy—that “Accolades for him have become bipartisan.” Later in life, working to defeat congressional opposition to President Nixon’s anti-ballistic missile program, Acheson served as an early mentor to the young neoconservative operatives Paul Wolfowitz, Richard Perle, and Edward Luttwak. Acheson spent his years out of office crafting one of the most enduring and pernicious of Cold War myths: that of Roosevelt’s credulousness in the face of Stalin. Acheson succeeded in selling the story that the Cold War was unavoidable, and the course he and Truman chose could not have been otherwise. Yet others, like Harriman, who knew the Soviets, Stalin, and Roosevelt far better than Acheson, thought otherwise. “FDR was basically right in thinking he could make progress by personal relations with Stalin,” said Harriman some years later. Harriman, like Bohlen, was at Tehran and Yalta and remembered that “Stalin was very much impressed with Roosevelt; you could almost say he was in awe of Roosevelt.” “I don’t know what would have happened if FDR had lived,” said Harriman toward the end of his long, eventful life. “I only know things would not have been the same.”

The militarization of the Cold War that took place under Truman and Acheson was a conscious choice. But it was not a choice with which everyone agreed; and so began a decades-long battle within the Democratic Party establishment between the heirs of Acheson and the heirs of Roosevelt.

#3587. Tom Hayden’s Legacy and the New American Left

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Standing in front of protesters on a misty day in Seattle, a 59-year-old Tom Hayden spoke into a bullhorn, declaring his hope. “I never thought the time would come that a new generation of activists would part the waters, the waters in which your idealism is supposed to be drowned.” The venerable activist, cofounder of Students for a Democratic Society (SDS), had spent almost two decades in the California State Legislature. But like other progressive legislators, he, too, was stymied by the conservative politics of the late 20th century. Earlier that day, tens of thousands of people—a historic coalition of union members, environmental activists, and anarchists—overwhelmed city officials and shut down the World Trade Organization (WTO) Ministerial Conference on November 30, 1999. It was the first major blow against the Clinton-era free-trade consensus.

The crowd repeated Hayden’s words through the “people’s mic” method and mirrored the then–state Senator’s visible giddiness toward the end of his remarks. “You have slowed the machinery of destruction down. But it can’t be about slowing the rate of destruction. It has to be about speeding the rate of creation of a new world, a better place.” Decades after he organized the landmark anti-war protests at the 1968 Democratic National Convention, Hayden was inspired by what he witnessed in Seattle. He could once again imagine a left that could win.

Tom Hayden passed away 10 years ago, on October 23, 2016, just weeks before Trump’s unexpected victory. As a result, his worst fears of an authoritarian right’s limiting the gains of the 1960s came true. Still, his life has become an even more relevant case study for those trying to figure out the most effective way to resist the current administration’s assault on democracy. As a lifelong insider-outsider, a Freedom Rider who later advised presidential candidates, Hayden was a passionate proponent of both radical direct action and electoral campaigns. Whether in the streets of American cities fighting back against ICE or in the offices of activist politicians like Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez or Mayor Zohran Mamdani, today’s progressives are finding creative ways to build on the work of Hayden’s generation. As memory of the Vietnam War era fades, a reexamination of ecumenical organizers like him will strengthen our understanding of the links between the victories and defeats of the past and today’s attempts to build a better democracy.

Born in 1939, Thomas Emmet Hayden was a product of middle-class America. Coming of age as the American left was decimated during the Red Scare, he did not consider himself political until he learned about the sit-in movement in 1960. He was first arrested as a Freedom Rider in Albany, Georgia, on December 10, 1961, the moment he began work on the first draft of SDS’s manifesto, the Port Huron Statement. Completed by Hayden and other founders of the organization the following June, the document promoted participatory democracy, offered a powerful rebuke of the era’s Cold War consensus, and soon became required reading for organizers for decades to come. What began as a modest network of student leaders would grow dramatically following the expansion of the war in Vietnam, with approximately 100,000 members at its peak in the late 1960s.

Hayden became one of the leading figures of the New Left, a figure whom many reporters treated as an entry point to a new age of radicalism. Together with other SDS leaders, he helped establish community organizing projects in several cities, traveled to Vietnam, where he once facilitated the release of three American POWs, and was put on trial for conspiracy to riot as part of the Chicago Eight. Over time, the times increasingly radicalized Hayden, who vacillated between calling for “Two, Three, Many Columbias” and clinging to his battered but lingering hope for mainstream politicians like Robert F. Kennedy during his presidential campaign.

However, Hayden’s work stretched far beyond the 1960s. Like other New Left leaders, he is often tied to a narrative of decline about the New Left’s rise and fall, centered on the well-documented implosion of SDS in 1969. The truth is that Hayden’s life tells a far more complicated and representative story of the decade’s activism, its ongoing influence on today’s progressives, and the porous relationship between radicals and reformers in America.

Unlike some high-profile leaders of his era, Hayden remained dedicated to movement work. Instead of burning out, he gradually shifted toward more conventional tactics in the early 1970s. Together with his then-wife Jane Fonda, he led the Indochina Peace Campaign, which helped sustain the anti-war movement’s political power and cut off funding for the Vietnam War. Following an insurgent Senate campaign, he and Fonda established the Campaign for Economic Democracy in California in 1977. By the early 1980s, CED had become a statewide organization that helped pass rent control initiatives and elect dozens of local and statewide officials. Hayden won a State Assembly seat in Santa Monica in 1982, serving a decade as a state representative, then another eight years as a state senator. Despite Republican governors’ limiting him for most of his time in Sacramento, Hayden still championed environmental reforms, including the Safe Drinking Water and Toxic Enforcement Act of 1986, and pushed for peace among street gangs in Los Angeles.

After he was termed out of the State Legislature, Hayden taught classes on social movements, joined The Nation as an editorial board member, and once again became a fixture at anti-war rallies and other meetings in progressive spaces. During his brief stint as a fellow at Harvard’s Institute of Politics, he led a series of workshops, “Activism Now!: Students, Sweatshops and Globalization,” that culminated in a trip where four students were arrested while documenting planned protests against the Free Trade Area of the Americas. After Occupy Wall Street, Hayden was in regular contact with new movement figures such as Debt Collective cofounder Astra Taylor after the two appeared on a panel together in 2014. “Good luck to Strike Debt. As your generation’s prospects decline and a new Dark Age rises, I do think you have a window of glory ahead though,” he wrote to Taylor. It was one of many interactions where Hayden cheered on a new generation shaped by the WTO protests, the post-9/11 wars, and the 2008 financial collapse.

In his final years, Hayden tried to situate the Obama presidency within a longer history of protest that contributed to his 2008 victory. He encouraged progressives to celebrate the best elements of the 44th president’s foreign policy (Cuba, Iran) while remaining critical of the war in Afghanistan and the administration’s tepid domestic agenda. He drew links between the Black Lives Matter movement and his work in Newark and lauded the lobbying efforts of the Dreamers. In 2016, Hayden infuriated many progressives when he backed Hillary Clinton over Bernie Sanders just days before the New York primary. He saw it as a pragmatic choice, rooted in fears of a Trump administration and the future of the Supreme Court. Those fears were legitimate and have proven prescient, but his endorsement underestimated the Sanders campaign’s potential impact.

If he were alive, he would celebrate that 2016 was a real turning point for the American left, when countless young organizers began to seriously engage in electoral politics. While he never would have predicted that the Democratic Socialists of America would become the post-2016 left’s primary beneficiary (even though he spoke at their annual convention in 2013), he would praise its electoral work, especially in cities like New York City, Chicago, and Los Angeles.

Much as he felt witnessing the Battle of Seattle in 1999, Hayden would have been ecstatic to see how a broad cross section of ordinary people has stood up to masked ICE agents occupying American cities over the past two years. The Minneapolis protests and general strike were, in many ways, the model many others from the 1960s generation had dreamed of for decades. The former community organizer would have been moved by how Minnesotans courageously faced down the president and won.

It is, in fact, a new dark age, but it has also benefited from those who built on the work of figures like Tom Hayden. He recognized the long battle but was also often driven by his desire to take urgent action to confront injustice. His story reflects that of many from his era who spent decades searching for different channels for progressives to win political power. Hayden had a searching mind, open to experimentation and adapting to history. Throughout it all, he thought that radical grassroots movements could merge with conventional politics to create a new democracy.

#3405. Bird Flu Is Raging on a Mink Farm. It Could Spark a Human Pandemic, Experts Say, but Officials Won’t Act.

Investigative Journalism in the Public Interest

These highlights were written by the reporters and editors who worked on this story.

The strain of bird flu that’s ripped through American barns for the last four years has killed two people who caught it from sick animals. But as far as anyone can tell, humans can’t spread it. The virus has remained a true “bird” flu, tailored to attach to avian cells, not invade human throats and noses, where coughing and sneezing reliably spew droplets.

Under the right conditions, however, that could change. A single mutation could give the bird flu a foothold in people and spark a pandemic that experts fear could be deadlier than COVID-19.

For that to happen, this bird flu would likely need to find an intermediate host — an animal vulnerable to viruses that infect both birds and humans. These animals would be crowded together, each infection raising the odds that a new strain could arise through mutation. Then, for that strain to jump across species, it would have to have access to humans and time to spread among them undetected.

That perfect petri dish exists right now, a ProPublica investigation found. For over two months, an outbreak of bird flu has raged uncontrolled on a Utah farm that raises thousands of mink for fur. Mink have lungs and airways that on a cellular level are remarkably similar to those of people; they have caught and spread other flus and infected some humans with COVID-19.

Instead of trying to stop the outbreak and prevent it from spilling into humans, state and federal officials have done little except try to keep its existence quiet, our investigation shows. The failure to act amounts to a cascade of missteps flu experts called “irresponsible” and “puzzling.” One called it “playing with fire.”

Utah agriculture officials learned in mid-August that mink were dying of bird flu on C&T Fenn Farms, about an hour’s drive south from Salt Lake City, according to communications released under the state’s public records law.

Had poultry been infected, the playbook would have been clear: The strain is so virulent that if a single chicken tests positive, the U.S. government requires farmers to kill the whole flock to protect other farms as well as public health. Poultry farmers are reimbursed for the loss.

But there are no federal rules for how to deal with sick mink.

Handling Utah’s response was state veterinarian Amanda Price, who sought help from the U.S. Department of Agriculture, which takes the lead on bird flu outbreaks involving poultry and cows.

USDA officials said they didn’t regulate mink or have a vaccine that was approved to give them. And though officials with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention spoke with Price’s health department counterparts who were less involved with the outbreak, they never talked to her, and she never reached out.

The threat of spillover has been far from the minds of Utah officials, records and interviews show. Instead, front and center have been the interests of a business raising animals to skin for luxury pelts to ship overseas.

“It has been devastating,” Price told ProPublica of the outbreak and its impact on the farm’s owner. “This is his life’s work that he’s seeing be destroyed. I really hope that you understand the humanity behind this situation.”

Flu experts were unequivocal that the government should have ordered the farm to cull its mink, as other countries have done.

But Utah never seriously considered it. A fur trade representative warned Price that doing so would kill the business and, ultimately, the industry. Farms spend decades breeding mink to develop unique genetic lineages.

“There’s just not a lot of options out there other than kind of sitting and waiting and seeing what happens,” Price said, noting that USDA officials did not urge her to kill the mink or make her feel like bird flu in mink was any riskier than infections in other animals that have caught it, like ducks and cows.

Flu experts found that response surprising.

“Every other country that I’m aware of that has had H5N1 in fur farms, depopulation has happened pretty quickly,” said Tom Peacock, an avian influenza researcher at the Pirbright Institute. “The fact that the first instinct wasn’t just to depopulate and figure stuff out later is very concerning to me.”

And all the while, officials didn’t monitor the mink workers for symptoms, as guidance from the CDC urges.

Their exposure is significant: Mink sneeze. They escape. They attack. “The mink are fierce,” said a Spanish-speaking “minkero” who works on a nearby farm, showing the scars he got from the aggressive, solitary creatures. “You get scared because the fucking thing bites, and it claws at you.”

Right now in Washington state, officials are working to track down the contacts of a symptomatic person exposed to dairy cattle infected with bird flu. The patient is isolating at home. Utah public health officials used to do similar, proactive monitoring. But they stopped after complaints from their colleagues in the agriculture department that farmers were annoyed by it.

“Us doing active monitoring was making it so that farms didn’t want to work with them,” said Pamela Gomez Pinedo, a respiratory disease epidemiologist for Utah’s health department. As a result, public health workers had a “pretty difficult time” getting details from agriculture officials, she said.

As part of a 2025 agreement, the Utah Department of Agriculture and Food agreed to share information about bird flu outbreaks on farms — so long as the health officials didn’t do anything to track workers’ health.

“We will only do active monitoring in the case of evidence of human-to-human transmission,” Pinedo said in emails with a CDC epidemiologist. As the outbreak stretched to a month, CDC officials expressed concerns, asking how anyone would know if a worker had been infected if they weren’t getting tested.

Price said the state’s policy not to monitor workers is meant to allow farmers to decide whether to contact the health department, rather than “forcing it on them.” “If we started seeing people actively dying from this, obviously that would change how we respond to this,” she said.

A telltale sign of bird flu infection in humans is eye redness. It is present in over 90% of cases and is often the sole symptom a person displays.

Last Saturday, a ProPublica reporter tracked down two people who worked at the Utah mink farm. One had a visibly red eye.

The CDC explicitly urges testing for the symptom.

After ProPublica reached out to the farm, its owner, Chad Fenn, contacted the Utah County Health Department about the red eye, which had cleared up by Monday, he told ProPublica. He said the worker got dust in his eye; the department didn’t recommend testing.

“They’re not going to do anything,” Fenn said. “They said they don’t see that there’s a need.” He said the department recommended testing only if other symptoms, like a cough or sore throat, developed.

A half dozen flu experts had no shortage of adjectives to describe their disbelief at the government decisions not to cull the mink or monitor and test the workers.

“It’s amazing to me,” said Gail Hansen, a former Kansas state public health veterinarian and epidemiologist. “Utah doesn’t want to be the epicenter of the next human flu outbreak, right?”

Most of the new viruses that have shaken humanity in the past century have spilled over from animals. Ebolas likely came from fruit bats; HIV, from chimpanzees. The leading theory for COVID-19 is that it jumped from bats to humans via an intermediate host mammal such as a raccoon dog. And all the modern flu pandemics have been bird flu viruses that mutated to spread in humans.

ProPublica reported extensively on spillover in a 2023 series called “Roots of an Outbreak.” To prevent more deadly spillovers, experts worldwide urge governments to take a “one health” approach to viral threats, treating dangers to animals as dangers to humans.

That’s not happening in Utah, experts said after reviewing ProPublica’s findings. Both the state and federal government are to blame.

“There’s no leader of this ‘one health’ team to take charge,” said Erin Sorrell, a senior scholar at the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security. “That is going to allow the virus to continue to circulate, potentially adapt and potentially spill over into another host, whether it’s avian, mammalian or human.”

This strain of bird flu, which began ripping through American poultry farms in early 2022, has proved adept at jumping to other species. It took hold in cows in 2024 and since then has run rampant through dairies.

Yet federal guidance to prevent the spread into mink farms hasn’t considered cows a threat. Farmers are encouraged to keep wild birds from infiltrating their barns and avoid feeding them raw poultry. It has said nothing about feeding beef parts to mink, which farmers commonly do in summer months to encourage the animals to put on weight and grow.

Dairy cows, which are not “depopulated” if exposed to bird flu, can still be slaughtered with the meat sold to farms and humans even though researchers have found the virus in the tissues of sick cows. USDA officials have insisted that beef for human consumption is free of bird flu, in part because the virus concentrates itself in sections of the cow people don’t usually eat. Fenn said that the mink are routinely fed raw organs of livestock, including cows.

The genomic data from the samples of mink lungs and brains sent from Utah to a USDA lab shows that the mink were stricken with the same strain of the virus that has spread in dairy herds. Officials said they didn’t know whether contaminated beef was the way the mink got infected.

In mink, the dairy strain of bird flu may have found a much better host for mutating into a human flu, because mink airways have an abundance of the type of cell receptors in people’s throats and noses that are a common pathway for infection.

A 2024 study of the dairy strain of the bird flu found that a single mutation could switch the virus from preferring the bird-type receptors to human-type receptors. And in studies using ferrets, a close relative of mink, the virus acquired mutations that let it attack human-type cell receptors.

It’s not clear to scientists just how much of an evolutionary leap would be needed for this strain of bird flu to spread among humans, or whether mink are the host that would allow that leap to happen.

How this ultimately plays out “really depends on what happens with the virus,” said Meghan Davis, an epidemiologist at Johns Hopkins University.

“We might get lucky, and a response that is not robust may not have dire consequences,” she said. “But we may get unlucky.”

There’s also a risk of something called “reassortment,” where another flu virus could infect the same cell as a bird flu virus and swap wide swaths of genetic code already adapted to humans.

When the outbreak in the mink farm started, there was very little seasonal flu it could mix with, but September saw one of the earliest starts to flu season on record, compared with November, when flu normally takes off.

“The most likely scenario for a pandemic is that we have a virus that reassorts with another virus,” said Jennifer Nuzzo, director of the Pandemic Center at the Brown University School of Public Health. “Obviously, having this circulate amid human flu season is not great.”

Utah officials were far more responsive to the threat of spillover in 2020, when outbreaks of COVID-19 spread explosively on mink farms – including this very farm. They invited federal officials to the state to research how the virus adapted and moved.

Given mink’s reputation as escape artists, wildlife experts set up traps around infected farms. The traps frequently caught mink carrying COVID-19 that had originated in the nearby barns. The virus infected both escaped mink and wild mink they had come in contact with. Mink and farmworkers were frequently tested, tracking the way that the virus was spreading and mutating.

As other states and countries took the same measures, they identified humans infected by the mink with COVID-19 and, ultimately, human-to-human transmission.

There are no similar efforts now in Utah with bird flu.

A USDA spokesperson told ProPublica that it was more involved in 2020 because the virus that causes COVID-19 had “significant” potential to transmit between humans and animals and vice versa.

Scientists with the USDA sequenced and published bird flu genomic data from the first samples of dead mink. Those sequences didn’t show any particularly concerning mutations, experts and the CDC told ProPublica.

But in the past two months, experts said, the virus is sure to have continued mutating as it spread – it’s only a question of whether those mutations could confer some advantage in infecting humans as well.

“As long as it continues to go mink to mink, then it becomes more and more likely that it could jump to a human,” Hansen said.

Fenn said new samples were taken two weeks ago. Price said she is still waiting for the results.

John Korslund, a retired USDA official who helped coordinate the response to COVID-19 on mink farms several years prior, was one of several experts who said officials need to look closely at newer genetic samples to see how the virus is evolving.

“I really want to know what the virus has done, from when it started to today,” he said. “Are we getting accumulated mutations making the virus more dangerous?”

ProPublica began reporting on the outbreak late this summer after the USDA updated a bird flu dashboard that showed seven captive mink had tested positive in Utah. But neither the USDA nor state officials had said anything publicly, and, when asked, they wouldn’t discuss what was actually happening on the farm.

Communications obtained under the state’s public records law — first published by the Animal Welfare Institute and reported on by the Salt Lake Tribune — show Utah officials took pains to protect the farm’s privacy amid the outbreak. The state did not declare an emergency, because doing so, officials said, “may alert animal rights groups,” according to the messages.

Willy Lanier, a public health veterinarian with the state health department, told colleagues to keep the outbreak under “closer hold” than outbreaks of bird flu on poultry and dairy farms. Keeping the information confidential was consistent with state protocol, a spokesperson in his department said, adding that it was still shared with local health officials. But when ProPublica reached out to the spokesperson for the Utah County Health Department about a possible bird flu outbreak, she said, “That would be news to me.”

Price is so deferential to the family that owns the farm that she has not set foot on the property.

“We might get lucky, and a response that is not robust may not have dire consequences. But we may get unlucky.”

“I’m really the only one here at this facility that’s been dealing with the producer. They’re a very private bunch,” Price said. “A lot of what I’m going off of is what I’ve seen from Google Maps, what I’ve talked to the producer about, what I’ve talked to the vet about. I haven’t felt the need to go out there based on those conversations.”

She said she “became very comfortable” in conversations with the owner that the virus wouldn’t spread off the farm. It doesn’t have animals moving on and off over the course of the year like on a dairy or poultry farm; mink leave only when they’re slaughtered and skinned for pelts at the end of the year. She also said that she was confident the owner would recapture any escaped mink.

Short of a formal quarantine, Price gave the farm a “verbal hold” to not move any mink, or manure or carcasses, off the farm. “We haven’t seen spread off of this farm. We have not had additional cases in Utah that are linked to this, so thus far the verbal hold orders and the measures that have been taken look to be successful: There is not spread happening from this farm from this case.”

Price’s assertion didn’t allay the concerns of experts that ProPublica spoke with. When poultry test positive for this strain of bird flu, all nearby farms are quarantined and tested for the virus. But the approach in Utah relies on farms reporting cases or the public calling in reports of dead birds in the area.

“If you’re not looking for something, you won’t find it,” Sorrell said. “Unless you’re doing active surveillance, you can’t claim that you haven’t had cases.”

Fenn told ProPublica his conversations with state officials made him less worried about any kind of spread. When the outbreak started, Fenn said, he asked whether the bird flu could infect people.

“Right off the bat, the state people we reported to, they said you don’t need to worry too much,” Fenn said. “My guys wear masks every day. They have glasses they can wear,” Fenn said. “ Me? I don’t wear it at all. Never have, never will, because I’ve been doing it for 40 years.”

Fenn said he wished he could vaccinate the mink for bird flu to keep the rest of his animals from catching it and dying; Price asked the USDA about a vaccine that had been used to save endangered wild species but said she got nowhere with that request. The USDA told ProPublica its division responsible for protecting animal health has no “species-specific regulatory authority or dedicated programs” for vaccinating mink, depopulating them or paying owners back for such a culling.

Fenn told ProPublica he thinks concerns about mink as a particularly risky bird flu host are trumped up by anti-fur activists, the same forces that he believed were behind the reactions in Spain and Finland, which depopulated mink farms hit with bird flu. “Europeans are a little scared of everything,” he said. “They’re going to do what they’re going to do, and here in the United States, we’re going to do what we’re going to do.”

Price said she was aware that Finland and Spain had depopulated their fur farms when the same strain infected them. “I didn’t really look much further into it,” she said, explaining that she was interested in those cases only for lessons about how to handle the outbreak, but not the particular reasons those countries chose to depopulate the farms.

A USDA spokesperson said Congress would have to specifically fund a program to depopulate mink. (The program to cull poultry to stop the spread of bird flu has cost well over $1 billion since 2022.)

The events in Utah are a “clear warning sign” about the dangers of mink farms, said Neil Vora, executive director for the Preventing Pandemics at the Source Coalition.

“Fur farms are to produce pelts purely for luxury goods, to make fancy coats,” he said. “It’s absurd that we’re willing to gamble with the lives of millions of people just to keep an industry alive.”

The industry has struggled as American consumers increasingly reject fur. Though domestic farms peaked about a decade ago, selling luxury pelts that often ended up in China, foreign farms have sprung up, threatening the business.

While animal rights advocates have pushed for Congress to adopt legislation that would phase out the fur industry, buying out farmers over the course of a year to end the practice once and for all, winds in Congress are blowing the other direction. The Senate will soon weigh in on the latest Farm Bill, which now seeks to expand market opportunities for mink farmers by undoing a prohibition in federal law on subsidizing the fur trade.

The virus continues to spread on the Utah farm, said Fenn, though at a slower pace. Experts told ProPublica it still poses a risk.

“I would want a full on hazmat suit if I were on that farm,” Peacock said. “Or, preferably, I’d rather not to be on that farm.”

That perfect petri dish exists right now, a ProPublica investigation found. For over two months, an outbreak of bird flu has raged uncontrolled on a Utah farm that raises thousands of mink for fur. Mink have lungs and airways that on a cellular level are remarkably similar to those of people; they have caught and spread other flus and infected some humans with COVID-19.

Instead of trying to stop the outbreak and prevent it from spilling into humans, state and federal officials have done little except try to keep its existence quiet, our investigation shows. The failure to act amounts to a cascade of missteps flu experts called “irresponsible” and “puzzling.” One called it “playing with fire.”

Utah agriculture officials learned in mid-August that mink were dying of bird flu on C&T Fenn Farms, about an hour’s drive south from Salt Lake City, according to communications released under the state’s public records law.

Had poultry been infected, the playbook would have been clear: The strain is so virulent that if a single chicken tests positive, the U.S. government requires farmers to kill the whole flock to protect other farms as well as public health. Poultry farmers are reimbursed for the loss.

But there are no federal rules for how to deal with sick mink.

Handling Utah’s response was state veterinarian Amanda Price, who sought help from the U.S. Department of Agriculture, which takes the lead on bird flu outbreaks involving poultry and cows.

USDA officials said they didn’t regulate mink or have a vaccine that was approved to give them. And though officials with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention spoke with Price’s health department counterparts who were less involved with the outbreak, they never talked to her, and she never reached out.

The threat of spillover has been far from the minds of Utah officials, records and interviews show. Instead, front and center have been the interests of a business raising animals to skin for luxury pelts to ship overseas.

“It has been devastating,” Price told ProPublica of the outbreak and its impact on the farm’s owner. “This is his life’s work that he’s seeing be destroyed. I really hope that you understand the humanity behind this situation.”

Flu experts were unequivocal that the government should have ordered the farm to cull its mink, as other countries have done.

But Utah never seriously considered it. A fur trade representative warned Price that doing so would kill the business and, ultimately, the industry. Farms spend decades breeding mink to develop unique genetic lineages.

“There’s just not a lot of options out there other than kind of sitting and waiting and seeing what happens,” Price said, noting that USDA officials did not urge her to kill the mink or make her feel like bird flu in mink was any riskier than infections in other animals that have caught it, like ducks and cows.

Flu experts found that response surprising.

“Every other country that I’m aware of that has had H5N1 in fur farms, depopulation has happened pretty quickly,” said Tom Peacock, an avian influenza researcher at the Pirbright Institute. “The fact that the first instinct wasn’t just to depopulate and figure stuff out later is very concerning to me.”

And all the while, officials didn’t monitor the mink workers for symptoms, as guidance from the CDC urges.

Their exposure is significant: Mink sneeze. They escape. They attack. “The mink are fierce,” said a Spanish-speaking “minkero” who works on a nearby farm, showing the scars he got from the aggressive, solitary creatures. “You get scared because the fucking thing bites, and it claws at you.”

Right now in Washington state, officials are working to track down the contacts of a symptomatic person exposed to dairy cattle infected with bird flu. The patient is isolating at home. Utah public health officials used to do similar, proactive monitoring. But they stopped after complaints from their colleagues in the agriculture department that farmers were annoyed by it.

“Us doing active monitoring was making it so that farms didn’t want to work with them,” said Pamela Gomez Pinedo, a respiratory disease epidemiologist for Utah’s health department. As a result, public health workers had a “pretty difficult time” getting details from agriculture officials, she said.

As part of a 2025 agreement, the Utah Department of Agriculture and Food agreed to share information about bird flu outbreaks on farms — so long as the health officials didn’t do anything to track workers’ health.

“We will only do active monitoring in the case of evidence of human-to-human transmission,” Pinedo said in emails with a CDC epidemiologist. As the outbreak stretched to a month, CDC officials expressed concerns, asking how anyone would know if a worker had been infected if they weren’t getting tested.

Price said the state’s policy not to monitor workers is meant to allow farmers to decide whether to contact the health department, rather than “forcing it on them.” “If we started seeing people actively dying from this, obviously that would change how we respond to this,” she said.

A telltale sign of bird flu infection in humans is eye redness. It is present in over 90% of cases and is often the sole symptom a person displays.

Last Saturday, a ProPublica reporter tracked down two people who worked at the Utah mink farm. One had a visibly red eye.

The CDC explicitly urges testing for the symptom.

After ProPublica reached out to the farm, its owner, Chad Fenn, contacted the Utah County Health Department about the red eye, which had cleared up by Monday, he told ProPublica. He said the worker got dust in his eye; the department didn’t recommend testing.

“They’re not going to do anything,” Fenn said. “They said they don’t see that there’s a need.” He said the department recommended testing only if other symptoms, like a cough or sore throat, developed.

A half dozen flu experts had no shortage of adjectives to describe their disbelief at the government decisions not to cull the mink or monitor and test the workers.

“It’s amazing to me,” said Gail Hansen, a former Kansas state public health veterinarian and epidemiologist. “Utah doesn’t want to be the epicenter of the next human flu outbreak, right?”

Most of the new viruses that have shaken humanity in the past century have spilled over from animals. Ebolas likely came from fruit bats; HIV, from chimpanzees. The leading theory for COVID-19 is that it jumped from bats to humans via an intermediate host mammal such as a raccoon dog. And all the modern flu pandemics have been bird flu viruses that mutated to spread in humans.

ProPublica reported extensively on spillover in a 2023 series called “Roots of an Outbreak.” To prevent more deadly spillovers, experts worldwide urge governments to take a “one health” approach to viral threats, treating dangers to animals as dangers to humans.

That’s not happening in Utah, experts said after reviewing ProPublica’s findings. Both the state and federal government are to blame.

“There’s no leader of this ‘one health’ team to take charge,” said Erin Sorrell, a senior scholar at the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security. “That is going to allow the virus to continue to circulate, potentially adapt and potentially spill over into another host, whether it’s avian, mammalian or human.”

This strain of bird flu, which began ripping through American poultry farms in early 2022, has proved adept at jumping to other species. It took hold in cows in 2024 and since then has run rampant through dairies.

Yet federal guidance to prevent the spread into mink farms hasn’t considered cows a threat. Farmers are encouraged to keep wild birds from infiltrating their barns and avoid feeding them raw poultry. It has said nothing about feeding beef parts to mink, which farmers commonly do in summer months to encourage the animals to put on weight and grow.

Dairy cows, which are not “depopulated” if exposed to bird flu, can still be slaughtered with the meat sold to farms and humans even though researchers have found the virus in the tissues of sick cows. USDA officials have insisted that beef for human consumption is free of bird flu, in part because the virus concentrates itself in sections of the cow people don’t usually eat. Fenn said that the mink are routinely fed raw organs of livestock, including cows.

The genomic data from the samples of mink lungs and brains sent from Utah to a USDA lab shows that the mink were stricken with the same strain of the virus that has spread in dairy herds. Officials said they didn’t know whether contaminated beef was the way the mink got infected.

In mink, the dairy strain of bird flu may have found a much better host for mutating into a human flu, because mink airways have an abundance of the type of cell receptors in people’s throats and noses that are a common pathway for infection.

A 2024 study of the dairy strain of the bird flu found that a single mutation could switch the virus from preferring the bird-type receptors to human-type receptors. And in studies using ferrets, a close relative of mink, the virus acquired mutations that let it attack human-type cell receptors.

It’s not clear to scientists just how much of an evolutionary leap would be needed for this strain of bird flu to spread among humans, or whether mink are the host that would allow that leap to happen.

How this ultimately plays out “really depends on what happens with the virus,” said Meghan Davis, an epidemiologist at Johns Hopkins University.

“We might get lucky, and a response that is not robust may not have dire consequences,” she said. “But we may get unlucky.”

There’s also a risk of something called “reassortment,” where another flu virus could infect the same cell as a bird flu virus and swap wide swaths of genetic code already adapted to humans.

When the outbreak in the mink farm started, there was very little seasonal flu it could mix with, but September saw one of the earliest starts to flu season on record, compared with November, when flu normally takes off.

“The most likely scenario for a pandemic is that we have a virus that reassorts with another virus,” said Jennifer Nuzzo, director of the Pandemic Center at the Brown University School of Public Health. “Obviously, having this circulate amid human flu season is not great.”

Utah officials were far more responsive to the threat of spillover in 2020, when outbreaks of COVID-19 spread explosively on mink farms – including this very farm. They invited federal officials to the state to research how the virus adapted and moved.

Given mink’s reputation as escape artists, wildlife experts set up traps around infected farms. The traps frequently caught mink carrying COVID-19 that had originated in the nearby barns. The virus infected both escaped mink and wild mink they had come in contact with. Mink and farmworkers were frequently tested, tracking the way that the virus was spreading and mutating.

As other states and countries took the same measures, they identified humans infected by the mink with COVID-19 and, ultimately, human-to-human transmission.

There are no similar efforts now in Utah with bird flu.

A USDA spokesperson told ProPublica that it was more involved in 2020 because the virus that causes COVID-19 had “significant” potential to transmit between humans and animals and vice versa.

Scientists with the USDA sequenced and published bird flu genomic data from the first samples of dead mink. Those sequences didn’t show any particularly concerning mutations, experts and the CDC told ProPublica.

But in the past two months, experts said, the virus is sure to have continued mutating as it spread – it’s only a question of whether those mutations could confer some advantage in infecting humans as well.

“As long as it continues to go mink to mink, then it becomes more and more likely that it could jump to a human,” Hansen said.

Fenn said new samples were taken two weeks ago. Price said she is still waiting for the results.

John Korslund, a retired USDA official who helped coordinate the response to COVID-19 on mink farms several years prior, was one of several experts who said officials need to look closely at newer genetic samples to see how the virus is evolving.

“I really want to know what the virus has done, from when it started to today,” he said. “Are we getting accumulated mutations making the virus more dangerous?”

ProPublica began reporting on the outbreak late this summer after the USDA updated a bird flu dashboard that showed seven captive mink had tested positive in Utah. But neither the USDA nor state officials had said anything publicly, and, when asked, they wouldn’t discuss what was actually happening on the farm.

Communications obtained under the state’s public records law — first published by the Animal Welfare Institute and reported on by the Salt Lake Tribune — show Utah officials took pains to protect the farm’s privacy amid the outbreak. The state did not declare an emergency, because doing so, officials said, “may alert animal rights groups,” according to the messages.

Willy Lanier, a public health veterinarian with the state health department, told colleagues to keep the outbreak under “closer hold” than outbreaks of bird flu on poultry and dairy farms. Keeping the information confidential was consistent with state protocol, a spokesperson in his department said, adding that it was still shared with local health officials. But when ProPublica reached out to the spokesperson for the Utah County Health Department about a possible bird flu outbreak, she said, “That would be news to me.”

Price is so deferential to the family that owns the farm that she has not set foot on the property.

“We might get lucky, and a response that is not robust may not have dire consequences. But we may get unlucky.”

“I’m really the only one here at this facility that’s been dealing with the producer. They’re a very private bunch,” Price said. “A lot of what I’m going off of is what I’ve seen from Google Maps, what I’ve talked to the producer about, what I’ve talked to the vet about. I haven’t felt the need to go out there based on those conversations.”

She said she “became very comfortable” in conversations with the owner that the virus wouldn’t spread off the farm. It doesn’t have animals moving on and off over the course of the year like on a dairy or poultry farm; mink leave only when they’re slaughtered and skinned for pelts at the end of the year. She also said that she was confident the owner would recapture any escaped mink.

Short of a formal quarantine, Price gave the farm a “verbal hold” to not move any mink, or manure or carcasses, off the farm. “We haven’t seen spread off of this farm. We have not had additional cases in Utah that are linked to this, so thus far the verbal hold orders and the measures that have been taken look to be successful: There is not spread happening from this farm from this case.”

Price’s assertion didn’t allay the concerns of experts that ProPublica spoke with. When poultry test positive for this strain of bird flu, all nearby farms are quarantined and tested for the virus. But the approach in Utah relies on farms reporting cases or the public calling in reports of dead birds in the area.

“If you’re not looking for something, you won’t find it,” Sorrell said. “Unless you’re doing active surveillance, you can’t claim that you haven’t had cases.”

Fenn told ProPublica his conversations with state officials made him less worried about any kind of spread. When the outbreak started, Fenn said, he asked whether the bird flu could infect people.

“Right off the bat, the state people we reported to, they said you don’t need to worry too much,” Fenn said. “My guys wear masks every day. They have glasses they can wear,” Fenn said. “ Me? I don’t wear it at all. Never have, never will, because I’ve been doing it for 40 years.”

Fenn said he wished he could vaccinate the mink for bird flu to keep the rest of his animals from catching it and dying; Price asked the USDA about a vaccine that had been used to save endangered wild species but said she got nowhere with that request. The USDA told ProPublica its division responsible for protecting animal health has no “species-specific regulatory authority or dedicated programs” for vaccinating mink, depopulating them or paying owners back for such a culling.

Fenn told ProPublica he thinks concerns about mink as a particularly risky bird flu host are trumped up by anti-fur activists, the same forces that he believed were behind the reactions in Spain and Finland, which depopulated mink farms hit with bird flu. “Europeans are a little scared of everything,” he said. “They’re going to do what they’re going to do, and here in the United States, we’re going to do what we’re going to do.”

Price said she was aware that Finland and Spain had depopulated their fur farms when the same strain infected them. “I didn’t really look much further into it,” she said, explaining that she was interested in those cases only for lessons about how to handle the outbreak, but not the particular reasons those countries chose to depopulate the farms.

A USDA spokesperson said Congress would have to specifically fund a program to depopulate mink. (The program to cull poultry to stop the spread of bird flu has cost well over $1 billion since 2022.)

The events in Utah are a “clear warning sign” about the dangers of mink farms, said Neil Vora, executive director for the Preventing Pandemics at the Source Coalition.

“Fur farms are to produce pelts purely for luxury goods, to make fancy coats,” he said. “It’s absurd that we’re willing to gamble with the lives of millions of people just to keep an industry alive.”

The industry has struggled as American consumers increasingly reject fur. Though domestic farms peaked about a decade ago, selling luxury pelts that often ended up in China, foreign farms have sprung up, threatening the business.

While animal rights advocates have pushed for Congress to adopt legislation that would phase out the fur industry, buying out farmers over the course of a year to end the practice once and for all, winds in Congress are blowing the other direction. The Senate will soon weigh in on the latest Farm Bill, which now seeks to expand market opportunities for mink farmers by undoing a prohibition in federal law on subsidizing the fur trade.

The virus continues to spread on the Utah farm, said Fenn, though at a slower pace. Experts told ProPublica it still poses a risk.

“I would want a full on hazmat suit if I were on that farm,” Peacock said. “Or, preferably, I’d rather not to be on that farm.”

#4112. At Scotland’s ancient Cladh Hallan, homes were full of human bones

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

Published Oct 23, 2026 8:56 AM EDT

In 2001, archaeologists were painstakingly excavating a dwelling at the Bronze-Age site of Cladh Hallan in  the Outer Hebrides islands off of Scotland’s northwestern coast. The house had been oval in shape, with a low door leading to an interior hearth. Other conveniences included sunken storage areas and stone-lined water storage tanks. Under the floors of these houses, though, and under the feet of the occupants, was a fascinating feature that evokes the opening scenes of a classic horror film.

As the archaeologists slowly dug through the floor of the structure, they came unexpectedly upon a human skeleton, legs tucked up in the fetal position. Another skeleton, similarly arranged, was found beneath a different roundhouse’s floor. These people had clearly been laid to rest with care, with stones laid out around them.

When archaeologists began to study the remains, though, they were staggered to learn that each body’s parts had not all come from the same person. These were composite mummies, assembled with the remains of at least six people who had died over the span of two hundred years. But this wasn’t a jumbled-together macabre collection or evidence of a Bronze-Age serial killer. These bodies had been treated carefully and buried in stone-lined graves. So what were the people of Cladh Hallan doing with the dug-up remains of their ancestors?

More than four thousand years ago, early farmers began to work the soil on the Outer Hebrides islands off of Scotland’s northwestern coast. At the time, the Roman Empire and even ancient Greece were specks on the chronological horizon. Along the Aegean coast, Minoans and Mycenaeans were building palaces and cities. Farther north, Germanic tribes were exporting amber and expertly forging bronze and gold artifacts.

At Cladh Hallan, early farmers grew barley, herded sheep, and foraged the abundant resources on the island’s beaches. Their descendants continued to thrive on the island for centuries, the population grew, and so did the need for land.

Nearly a thousand years into Cladh Hallan’s occupation, with living space on the small island becoming harder to find, people started building over the burial grounds of their ancestors. But the islanders were definitely aware of the graves beneath their feet—in fact, they went out of their way to acknowledge the people buried in them.

Residents of Cladh Hallan dug down into these older burials containing both cremated and unburnt remains, removing some parts and leaving the rest undisturbed. Then they assembled the exhumed remains into the requisite anatomical combinations that modern archaeologists have found centuries later. Once this was done, the composite bodies were once again laid to rest under the foundations of the roundhouses.

The two best-known adult skeletons from Cladh Hallan are the most unusual. Each was found under a roundhouse. One skeleton was identified as male, the other female, based on their pelvic bones. The bodies date to between 1300 and 1500 BCE…mostly.

The skeleton originally identified as a woman had a man’s skull where her own head used to be. Two of the teeth were missing from the skull, and those had been placed in each of the woman’s hands. By radiocarbon dating the bones, researchers found that the skull actually belonged to a man who had died at least a century earlier, perhaps a relative from an older generation.

The body originally identified as male was put together from three different people—all confirmed as male through DNA testing. The burial was made up of a man’s torso, a second man’s skull, and a third man’s jawbone. Each of these men had died at different times over the span of about 150 years. Again, ancestry and family identity seem to play a big part in these re-burials.

It isn’t terribly uncommon for cultures to include their deceased loved ones in celebratory, ritual, or even daily activities. Every few years, families in the Toraja region of Sulawesi, Indonesia exhume the remains of loved ones, clean them, dress them in fresh garments, and honor them with festivals.

South Korean “death beads” are made from the cremated remains of family members and displayed in the home.

Pre-colonial Inca religious tradition included regular ceremonial processions featuring the remains of people “buried” in natural alcoves in the Andes mountains and mummified by the dry, cold mountain air. The Cladh Hallan skeletons had once been mummified as well, though this was not mummification as it was practiced in ancient Egypt, nor like the mountainside Inca tradition.

First, the bodies were tightly wrapped in fabric, which kept the bones and soft tissue flexed into the fetal position. This is why the limbs remained so tightly curled towards the torso, and why the bodies were so well-preserved. Archaeologists were able to identify this type of preservation based on microscopic changes to the surfaces of the bones from the acidic environment of the peat bogs in which the bodies were submerged after wrapping.

Peat is a dense, wet accumulation of centuries of decaying plant matter, and is a feature of lots of landscapes, particularly in the northern latitudes. People have used dried peat as fuel for thousands of years, since it burns well and slowly, and is plentiful in areas where there aren’t a lot of trees for firewood. Peat from the Scottish isles, notably, is responsible for the smoky, earthy taste characteristic of Scotch whiskey.

Peat bogs are so packed with plant material that there is almost no oxygen present, which is great for preservation, as is the typically cool temperature beneath the peat’s surface. The lack of oxygen keeps microbes and other critters from consuming the body’s soft tissues, and the tannic acid from all the decaying plants in the bog turns skin dark and leathery.

By first burying the bodies of family members under layers of peat, the people at Cladh Hallan ensured that the remains would be as intact as possible when later generations exhumed them. This not only means that the Bronze Age inhabitants of the island were well aware of peat’s preservative properties, but that the entire process was a deliberate sequence of events.

Archaeologists interpret the pieced-together reburials as a part of a social or religious tradition that emphasized family lines and reverence for ancestors. Stones clearly marked the graves under the roundhouses, so the homes’ placement was definitely on purpose.

The initial bog burials were deliberately used as a source of family remains, and the parts that composed the buried skeletons seem to have been deliberately chosen, though we may never know why or how. It seems as though ancestor-recycling may have been a regular process at Cladh Hallan in the later part of the Bronze Age, as older generations gave way to new.

Archaeologists also surmise that the exhumed mummified remains were initially kept above ground, maybe in a special place in the house, and included as a part of daily life. These multi-ancestor figures might have been consulted when important decisions were made, or they may have been seen as household protectors.

Archaeology can never really uncover the full story of what people thought and believed thousands of years ago. Thoughts do not fossilize, and decay and weathering affect even the best-preserved archaeological sites.

We will probably never know exactly what it was like to live at Cladh Hallan when it was a busy, thriving settlement. We may never understand how the people that lived there conceived of their world, or how they thought about spirituality. Thanks to the burials at Cladh Hallan, though, we have a tantalizing glimpse of how people may have seen death in their universe as a continuous, pieced-together chain of lives lived over generations.

#4212. A 250-Million-Year-Old Habit May Explain How Crocodiles Outlasted the Dinosaurs

📅 23 Oct 2026 | technology | Source

It was their preference for warmer climates.

Some 66 million years ago, an asteroid slammed into Earth, bringing the age of giants to a catastrophic end. T. rex, Triceratops, and all other non-avian dinosaurs vanished. Only one branch of the dinosaur family tree survived—birds.

But what about another great prehistoric lineage? How on Earth did crocodiles survive?

After all, the broader crocodile lineage had already existed for roughly 180 million years when the asteroid struck. Its evolutionary family tree included colossal predators that could attack dinosaurs, armored plant eaters, and strange creatures that looked nothing like the crocodiles we know today.

A new study offers a surprising explanation. Despite their extraordinary diversity, crocodiles and their ancient relatives spent roughly 250 million years following a remarkably consistent rule: follow the warmth.

That seemingly simple preference may have put some of them in exactly the right places when disaster struck.

Today’s crocodilians are variations on an exceptionally effective ambush-predator design. Their armored bodies lurk unseen beneath the waterline, while eyes and nostrils perched high on their skulls remain exposed. Powerful tails propel them forward, exploding into action with muscular jaws seizing unsuspecting prey.

It’s a successful design, but for most of their evolutionary history, crocodile relatives were anything but predictable.

More than 200 million years ago, phytosaurs patrolled Triassic rivers looking uncannily like modern crocodiles, complete with long snouts and armored bodies. Their nostrils, however, sat much farther back, near their eyes. Whether phytosaurs belonged to the crocodile evolutionary branch or were more distant relatives remains debated. Either way, they were an extraordinary example of evolution producing similar solutions to similar problems.

But the crocodile family tree was never limited to this aquatic ambush-predator blueprint.

Some crocodile relatives were powerful land hunters, evolving body plans that looked more like dinosaurs than modern crocodiles. Poposaurus stalked prey on two legs, with feet remarkably similar to those of bipedal dinosaurs. Meanwhile, Fasolasuchus was a colossal four-legged predator that may have reached 9 meters (30 feet) in length, potentially ranking among the largest land predators outside the dinosaurs.

Other branches took an entirely different evolutionary path and became herbivores. Simosuchus, a small crocodile relative from Madagascar, had a short, pug-like snout and unusual leaf-shearing teeth adapted for eating vegetation.

And in the Triassic, aetosaurs like Desmatosuchus roamed the landscape covered in bony armor, with enormous spikes protruding from their shoulders. They looked more like miniature tanks than crocodiles.

Metriorhynchus evolved into an ocean-going predator, complete with flippers and a powerful tail adapted for swimming. Meanwhile, enormous semiaquatic creatures such as Deinosuchus stalked Cretaceous waterways, with some estimates putting the largest individuals at around 10 to 12 meters long. These giants were capable of preying on dinosaurs that ventured too close to the water.

“They have occupied almost every conceivable ecological role,” Alessandro Chiarenza, a paleontologist at University College London and the study’s lead author, told Reuters.

Today, only a fraction of that diversity remains. Almost everything about crocodile relatives changed over the centuries, from what they ate to how they moved.

But something else barely changed at all.

To investigate, Chiarenza and his colleagues assembled an evolutionary tree containing more than 400 living and extinct species, drawing on over 7,000 fossil records and extensive modern observations.

They compared these records with 52 reconstructions of Earth’s ancient climates, stretching back 245 million years. Their habitat models focused on nonmarine crocodile-line animals, rather than every ocean-going offshoot.

The results revealed that despite evolving radically different bodies, diets, and lifestyles, crocodile-line animals largely retained their ancestral preference for warmth.

This wasn’t simply a matter of crocodiles living in the tropics. For much of their history, the tropics weren’t their only option.

Around 50 million years ago, when Earth was approximately 10° Celsius warmer than today, crocodile relatives inhabited high-latitude regions of North America, Europe, and even Antarctica.

As the planet cooled, suitable habitats shifted toward the equator, and crocodile populations largely followed. In other words, rather than evolving to withstand increasingly cold conditions, they generally moved to places that still offered the warmth they needed.

Their climatic preferences weren’t completely fixed. The researchers identified changes in temperature preferences around 94 million years ago and a shift toward drier conditions among certain Australian crocodile relatives around 34 million years ago.

But the broader pattern remained remarkably consistent. Rather than continually evolving new tolerances, crocodiles generally followed the climates they already preferred.

Scientists call this climatic niche conservatism: the tendency of a lineage to retain its inherited environmental preferences over evolutionary time.

It’s a strategy that sounds almost stubborn. But 66 million years ago, it became a lifeline.

The Chicxulub impact didn’t just kill animals in the immediate vicinity of the collision. It sent enormous quantities of dust and other particles into the atmosphere, reducing the sunlight reaching Earth’s surface.

Temperatures plummeted, photosynthesis was disrupted, and food webs began to collapse. For animals dependent on stable temperatures and abundant food, the consequences could be devastating.

With sunlight reduced by 5% or 10%, tropical and subtropical regions retained climates suitable for crocodile relatives. Only the most extreme scenario, involving a 15% reduction, eliminated suitable habitats globally. The researchers noted that this particular scenario is considered unlikely.

The results contrast with an earlier 2020 study involving several of the same researchers, which found that an asteroid-induced impact winter drastically reduced suitable habitats for non-avian dinosaurs. The new findings suggest that the same global catastrophe could have affected two ancient lineages very differently, partly because of where suitable habitats remained.

Living crocodilians are cold-blooded, requiring far less energy than comparably sized warm-blooded animals. They can endure long periods without food, and some enter dormant states when conditions deteriorate. These characteristics would have been especially useful after the impact, when the collapse of plant productivity disrupted food supplies.

Smaller body sizes, semiaquatic lifestyles, and flexible diets probably helped some surviving crocodile relatives, too.

The simulations don’t prove that tropical refuges saved crocodiles, nor do they explain why particular species survived while others disappeared. But they reveal how geography and inherited climatic preferences could have tipped the odds.

There’s also a sobering lesson for modern crocodilians. Their ancestors often survived changing climates by moving to places that remained suitable. But today’s crocodilians face a world increasingly fragmented by roads, farmland, cities, and other human barriers. The habitats they need aren’t always connected, and moving to a better location may no longer be so simple.

Crocodile relatives spent 250 million years experimenting with extraordinary ways to live, becoming giants, herbivores, land hunters, and marine predators. Yet one of the traits that may have helped their lineage endure was something they changed surprisingly little: their preference for a warm place to live.

The study was published in the journal Science Advances.
