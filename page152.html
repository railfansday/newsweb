#639. Scientists reach the ultimate quantum limit on a surface

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Scientists at the Max Planck Institute for the Science of Light (MPL) have achieved a breakthrough in quantum physics, developing a way to study individual molecules on a surface with extraordinary precision. For the first time, researchers have demonstrated that molecules attached to a surface can reach the ultimate quantum limit of optical coherence, overcoming a major obstacle that has long restricted molecular quantum research.

The findings, published in Science, could open new possibilities for investigating how molecules interact with surfaces and developing advanced quantum technologies.

Why Surfaces Have Been a Major Obstacle in Quantum Physics

Many emerging quantum technologies depend on extremely small objects, such as individual atoms and molecules, that interact with light. Known as quantum emitters, these tiny systems can produce single particles of light called photons, store quantum information, and help establish quantum connections between distant systems. These capabilities are important for future applications in quantum computing and secure quantum communication.

To study a single quantum emitter, scientists must keep it stable and confined to one location for extended periods. Traditionally, researchers have accomplished this by trapping atoms or molecules in a vacuum or embedding them inside solid materials.

Placing quantum emitters directly on a surface, however, offers an intriguing advantage. Scientists could potentially manipulate individual molecules by "touching them" with an extremely sharp probe. Techniques such as scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) already use tips with atomic precision to investigate matter at incredibly small scales.

The difficulty is that surfaces are rarely as clean and stable as quantum experiments require. Contaminants from the surrounding environment can accumulate on them, creating disturbances that interfere with the delicate quantum behavior of nearby atoms and molecules. Until now, researchers had struggled to preserve the quantum optical properties of individual emitters attached to surfaces.

Scientists in MPL's Nano-Optics Division have now found a way around this problem.

A Clever Crystal Cleaning Method Solves the Problem

The research team, led by Prof. Vahid Sandoghdar, director at MPL and head of the "Nano-Optics" Division, developed an unusual cleaning method that takes advantage of a natural property of organic crystals.

At room temperature, the crystal material gradually evaporates through a process known as sublimation, in which a solid transforms directly into a gas without first becoming a liquid.

The researchers placed a small crystal inside a vacuum chamber equipped for extremely low temperatures, called a cryostat. As the crystal's uppermost layers slowly evaporated, they carried away unwanted contaminants, leaving behind a remarkably clean surface.

The team then cooled the crystal to just a few degrees Kelvin above absolute zero, the lowest theoretically possible temperature. This extreme cooling halted further sublimation and helped maintain the pristine surface.

Finally, the scientists used a tiny, specially fabricated oven to evaporate molecules onto the cold crystal, allowing them to settle on the clean surface.

Molecules Reach the Ultimate Quantum Limit

With this exceptionally stable environment in place, the researchers could investigate whether the molecules retained their fragile quantum properties.

Dr. Alexey Shkarin, a researcher in the Nano-Optics Division at MPL, explained: "The quality of quantum emitters can be evaluated by their coherence times, which indicate how long they keep their quantumness."

Coherence time describes how long a quantum system can maintain the properties needed for quantum behavior. The longer this coherence persists, the better the emitter can perform in applications that depend on precise interactions with light.

However, there is a fundamental physical restriction on how long coherence can last. Known as the Fourier limit, this boundary is determined by the time required for a quantum emitter to release its energy into its surroundings.

In an unstable or 'noisy' environment, disturbances can cause coherence to disappear hundreds or even thousands of times faster than this theoretical limit allows.

The scientists discovered that molecules deposited on their exceptionally clean crystal surface consistently reached the Fourier limit. This demonstrated that the surrounding environment was sufficiently quiet and stable to preserve the molecules' quantum coherence for the maximum physically permitted duration.

It is the first time researchers have achieved this fundamental limit with molecules attached to a surface.

New Possibilities for Controlling Individual Molecules

The breakthrough also allowed the team to investigate how a crystal surface influences the molecules resting on it.

Further experiments revealed that interactions with the surface can cause molecules to align in particular directions and alter their energy levels. The researchers also found indications that the surface might influence the molecules' shapes or the ways they vibrate.

These effects could provide valuable clues about how matter behaves at the molecular scale, while also offering new opportunities to control quantum systems.

"Our future work will focus on combining this method with AFM and STM to gain local nanometer control over individual quantum emitters," says Vahid Sandoghdar.

By combining their ultra-clean surface technique with powerful microscopy methods, the researchers hope to manipulate individual quantum emitters with precision measured in billionths of a meter.

Such capabilities could reveal previously inaccessible details about interactions between molecules and surfaces, while laying the groundwork for engineering new quantum states of matter and advancing future molecular quantum technologies.

#638. Jensen Huang’s Vision for Europe Runs on Plug-In Data Centers

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Jensen Huang outlined his vision of Europe’s future at Nvidia’s global conference in Berlin on Wednesday. It consists of robotics, AI agents, and modular data centers, with Nvidia unsurprisingly powering it all.

“To be part of this AI economy, you don’t want to just be consuming the AI. You want to be producing the AI,” he told the 4,000-strong crowd at Nvidia’s GTC. “We can do all of that here in Europe.”

The last GTC, held in San Jose, California, in March, was all about AI inference and more efficient compute. Seven months later, the focus has switched to power, agents, and physical AI. There were even some cute little Microduck robots—made by recent Nvidia acquisition Hugging Face—doing Huang’s bidding at the end of his 90-minute pitch.

Huang’s first big announcement was a modular “AI factory” called the DSX Pod. It is a “plug-and-play” option for building AI data centers, with everything arriving already assembled in a large metal container and packed with Nvidia’s latest Vera Rubin compute racks. It can be brought online in 14 days, instead of taking months or years to construct. That may come in especially handy in Europe, where infrastructure projects are notoriously slow. Huang said the DSX Pod would also slash the amount of time compute sits idle in a data center as it will come online quicker. “This is a complete transformation,” he said.

The speech, delivered at Berlin’s Tempodrom, a venue inspired by a circus tent with the lighting of a gamer’s den, veered between selling laptops and sweeping predictions, with Nvidia presented as the fundamental technology provider to every industry. At one point, two Greenpeace protesters stood up and unfurled a banner reading “Clean up your dirty business.” Huang told his security team to “Go easy” as they were marched out.

Humanoid robotics will be the “next great industry,” Huang said, arguing that Europe was well-placed to lead on it due to its manufacturing heritage. But physical AI goes way beyond robotics, Huang pointed out, explaining that even telecom operators are adding Nvidia GPUs to their cell towers. He also announced partnerships with European carmakers VW and Mercedes on AVs.

Nvidia is making a similar move in humanoid robotics, with Huang announcing the launch of a full-stack robotics platform to standardize configuration. It also plans to release a preconfigured reference robot, Agile One, available in the first quarter of next year, to help developers start building physical AI systems. “This is your great opportunity,” he said.

On model usage, Huang predicted: “Every company in the future will use both closed models as well as open models that you customize.” He showcased Nvidia’s own open-source models as hitting frontier performance once customized on an organization’s proprietary data.

He also predicted that agents would mean there will be more users of software tools than ever before, and said agents’ reasoning, iteration, and tool use are pushing token consumption and demand for computing—and Nvidia GPUs—higher.

In case the 90-minute speech was too technical, it ended with an AI-generated Eurovision song summing up what Huang had just announced. The speech was easier to follow, to be fair.

On Oct. 20, 1970, the Soviet Union launched the Zond 8 spacecraft on a mission to the moon. This was the last mission in the Zond program.

After the first three Zond missions explored Venus and Mars, the next ones were circumlunar missions that flew around the moon before returning to Earth. Although there was no crew on board, the Zond spacecraft was designed with cosmonauts in mind.

The Zond missions served as test flights for the Soviet Union's plans to send cosmonauts to the moon. But those plans for crewed flights never came to fruition. This was partly because the U.S. had already put astronauts on the moon, but also because the Zond missions had a lot of technical problems.

Zond 8 was considered a success — even though its guidance system malfunctioned on the way back to Earth. The spacecraft still returned to Earth safely and splashed down in the Indian Ocean one week after it launched.

While the Zond 1 and Zond 2 missions were launched toward Venus and Mars, respectively, the primary target of most Zond missions was the moon. At the time, the Soviet Union was embroiled in an ongoing space race with the United States to be the first on the moon with a human crew.

By the time Zond 8 launched on Oct. 20, 1970, that space race was lost to the Soviet Union. NASA and the U.S. had already landed astronauts on the moon twice with Apollo 11 and Apollo 12 (in July and November of 1969, respectively), and even peformed the first space rescue fo the Apollo 13 astronauts in April 1970.

While Zond 8 was uncrewed, it was a test flight of the Soviet Union's L1 spacecraft that was designed to fly a cosmonaut around the moon. It looped around the moon in a lunar flyby, approaching within 1,120 kilometers of the moon's surface at its closest point, according to RussianSpaceWeb.com.

Cameras on Zond 8 captured stunning views of the Earth and moon, recreating the iconic Earthrise views seen by Apollo 8 astronauts two years earlier in 1968.

Zond 8 ultimately splashed down in the Indian Ocean on Oct. 27, 1970 after a solar attitude sensor failed, sending it on a shorter, steeper reentry path during landing, according to RussianSpaceWeb.com. It was retrieved by a recovery ship, marking the end of the Soviet moon landing program. Russian would go on to develop a series of Salyut space stations, culminating in the long-lived Mir space station.

After decades without a moon probe, Russia did launch a new uncrewed moon lander in 2023. That Luna 25 mission failed and crashed into the lunar surface after an onboard engine controller malfunctioned. The country has pledged a return to the moon in 2027.

#624. Scientists find a way to fight Alzheimer’s brain damage without removing toxic tau

📅 26 Oct 2026 | other | Source

Protecting brain cells from destruction remains one of the biggest challenges in treating Alzheimer's disease and related neurodegenerative conditions. These disorders, known as tauopathies, involve the buildup of abnormal tangles of a protein called tau inside the brain. As the tangles accumulate, nearby neurons begin to die. However, growing evidence suggests that tau itself may not be responsible for most of the damage. Instead, the immune system's reaction to the protein appears to play a major role in the loss of brain cells, memory, and independence.

Scientists at Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis have now identified a promising strategy for interrupting this damaging immune response. In mice with tau accumulation similar to that seen in Alzheimer's, researchers used an antibody to block a protein called CXCR3, which helps immune cells known as T cells enter the brain. After several months of treatment, the number of T cells inside the brain had fallen by approximately half.

The results were striking. Compared with untreated mice, animals receiving the antibody retained roughly 40% more tissue in brain regions involved in memory and performed better on a memory test. These improvements occurred even though the amount of accumulated tau remained unchanged.

The findings were published September 28 in the journal Neuron.

"In tauopathies, including Alzheimer's disease, there's no treatment right now that actually decreases neurodegeneration," said David M. Holtzman, MD, senior author of the study and the Barbara Burton and Reuben M. Morriss III Distinguished Professor in the Department of Neurology at WashU Medicine. "If we can show that we're really decreasing brain cell death, it's certainly worth trying to pursue that pathway to prevent the most devastating consequences of these diseases."

Why Immune Cells May Be a Key to Alzheimer's Brain Damage

Current Alzheimer's treatments primarily focus on removing amyloid, another protein associated with the disease. Two approved medications, lecanemab and donanemab, target amyloid plaques that develop between brain cells during the early stages of Alzheimer's. These deposits interfere with communication between neurons, contribute to the formation of tau tangles inside cells, and ultimately lead to neuronal death.

Although these medications can slow cognitive decline, they have not been shown to prevent brain cells from dying. They also offer no treatment for primary tauopathies, conditions in which tau accumulates without the presence of amyloid. Alzheimer's is classified as a secondary tauopathy because both abnormal amyloid and tau are present.

Holtzman's laboratory has been investigating whether the immune system offers another way to address these diseases. In a 2023 study published in Nature, the researchers discovered that T cells enter the brains of mice with abnormal tau accumulation, concentrating in regions containing the most tau and contributing to neurodegeneration.

A related study published earlier this month in Nature Neuroscience provided another important clue. It showed that the signals directing these T cells originate in lymph nodes located outside the brain.

"For this therapeutic approach, if it is safe, you wouldn't have to design the drug to get into the brain -- which is a big deal since most molecules don't cross the blood-brain barrier well -- and you don't have to get rid of the tau to get this therapeutic effect."

Scientists Identify the Pathway That Guides Immune Cells Into the Brain

Despite these earlier discoveries, researchers still needed to understand how activated T cells found their way into the brain.

T cells navigate through the body by following chemical signals known as chemokines. Earlier experiments in Holtzman's laboratory had identified unusually high levels of one such signal, CXCL10, in mice with tau accumulation. Other researchers had also reported elevated CXCL10 levels in people with Alzheimer's disease.

Activated T cells carry a surface receptor called CXCR3 that recognizes CXCL10, allowing the cells to follow its chemical signals. This relationship suggested that the CXCL10 and CXCR3 pathway might be responsible for guiding harmful T cells into the brain.

To investigate, the researchers studied mice that developed abnormal tau deposits resembling those associated with Alzheimer's. The team included co-senior author Jason Ulrich, PhD, a professor of neurology at WashU Medicine, and first author Joshua T. Emmerson, PhD, a postdoctoral researcher in Holtzman's laboratory.

The experiments revealed that T cells could not infiltrate the brain when mice lacked either CXCL10 or its matching receptor, CXCR3, on T cells. This protection persisted even when the scientists deliberately triggered inflammation to stimulate an immune response.

Experimental Treatment Preserves Brain Tissue and Memory

With the pathway identified, the team tested whether blocking CXCR3 with an antibody could reduce brain damage.

The researchers selected young mice that already had tau deposits in their brains but had not yet experienced substantial loss of brain cells. They administered the antibody once every five days for three and a half months.

By the end of the experiment, treated mice had approximately half as many T cells in their brains as untreated animals. They also showed less evidence of neuronal injury and retained substantially more brain tissue, including roughly 40% more tissue in memory-related regions. Their performance on a memory test was also better.

Importantly, these protective effects occurred without any detectable reduction in tau tangles. The treated and untreated mice had similar amounts of abnormal tau, suggesting that limiting the immune response could reduce neurodegeneration even when the underlying protein deposits remain.

Another finding could have major implications for future drug development. The antibody reached the boundary of the brain but did not enter the brain tissue itself.

This suggests that it may be possible to reduce neurodegeneration by targeting immune activity from outside the brain. Such an approach could avoid one of the major difficulties in developing neurological treatments: getting drugs across the blood-brain barrier, which prevents many substances from entering brain tissue.

A Potential New Direction for Alzheimer's Treatment

Although the results are encouraging, the treatment has so far been tested only in mice. Additional research will be needed to determine whether the approach is safe and effective enough to evaluate in people.

Holtzman suggested that medications already used to control T cells in other diseases could provide a starting point. Certain treatments for multiple sclerosis and other autoimmune conditions, in which the immune system mistakenly attacks healthy tissue, might be worth investigating for their potential to reduce Alzheimer's-related brain damage.

This would represent a significant shift in treatment strategy. Rather than concentrating exclusively on the abnormal proteins associated with neurodegeneration, researchers could also target the immune responses that contribute to the destruction of neurons.

"Tauopathies aren't thought of as autoimmune disorders, so they haven't been treated the same way, but this study shows for the first time in an animal model that these diseases respond to a specific T-cell therapy," said Holtzman, who also directs WashU's Hope Center for Neurological Disorders and the Knight Alzheimer Disease Research Center at WashU Medicine.

"For this therapeutic approach, if it is safe, you wouldn't have to design the drug to get into the brain -- which is a big deal since most molecules don't cross the blood-brain barrier well -- and you don't have to get rid of the tau to get this therapeutic effect."

The findings raise the possibility that protecting the brain from harmful immune activity, rather than removing tau itself, could become an important strategy for treating Alzheimer's and other tauopathies.

The ability to create protein scaffolds that impart enantioselectivity to simple, readily available metal catalysts could redefine the landscape of asymmetric catalysis. Artificial metalloenzymes (ArMs), hybrid catalysts that combine a synthetic metal cofactor with a protein scaffold, offer a potential means to achieve this goal, but scaffold discovery has remained constrained by trial-and-error approaches and the functional limits of extant proteins.1–5 This challenge is acute for metal polypyridine photocatalysts, which are widely used in visible-light chemistry but have not enabled highly enantioselective photocatalysis within protein scaffolds. These findings establish de novo protein design as a route to functional scaffolds for synthetic metal complexes that lack natural protein partners and provide a strategy for combining protein design and evolution to control non-natural catalytic chemistry.

A Russian satellite just took a very close look at an American spacecraft in low Earth orbit.

A few days ago, the snooping satellite Kosmos 2612 rendezvoused with ICEYE-X36, a commercial craft that observes our planet using synthetic aperture radar (SAR).

The close encounter, which occurred between Oct. 15 and Oct. 19, was reported by Marco Langbroek and other satellite trackers, with Langbroek calling it "a worrisome cat-and-mouse game" in orbit. The rendezvous has garnered a lot of attention in space circles, because it was non-consensual and startlingly close; at one point, the two spacecraft may have been separated by just a few meters. Before tackling that question, we need to set the stage a bit. For example, it's important to note that inspections aren't unheard of in the final frontier. Russia, the United States and China have all maneuvered satellites into close proximity of other nations' hardware in recent years.

Most of this activity has taken place in geostationary orbit, which lies 22,236 miles (35,786 kilometers) above Earth. At this altitude, orbital velocity matches our planet's rotational speed, allowing spacecraft to "hover" over one patch of Earth continuously. GEO is therefore a popular destination for spy, communications and weather satellites — and for spacecraft designed to shadow and assess them.

Kosmos 2612 is operating in low Earth orbit (LEO), which is part of the reason its activities have created such a stir. Neither China nor the U.S. has conducted an uncooperative rendezvous in LEO, as far as we know. But Russia has, according to Victoria Samson, chief director of space security and stability for the nonprofit Secure World Foundation.

"They've done a few," she told Space.com. "Kosmos 2543 got within about 30 kilometers [19 miles] of USA 245 in January 2020, and then Kosmos 2558 got within about 70 kilometers [43 miles] of USA 326 in August 2022. And they've done repeated approaches to 326."

Kosmos 2612 got much closer to its target than those other inspectors did, of course. And there's another big difference: USA 245 and USA 326 are classified spy satellites managed by the U.S. National Reconnaissance Office, while ICEYE-X36 is a private Earth-observing craft.

There are about 80 ICEYE spacecraft in LEO at the moment. They image our planet using SAR, which can peer through clouds and gather data both day and night. Some of these satellites are operated by ICEYE, a company based in Finland, while others are managed by its American subsidiary, ICEYE US. ICEYE-X36 is one of the latter.

At least some ICEYE satellites provide imagery to Ukraine, which Russia invaded in February 2022 and continues to assault with ground troops and missile and drone attacks. Indeed, ICEYE signed a contract this past January firming up this data-sharing relationship with Ukraine.

"The agreement ensures that the Ministry of Defence continues to receive a high volume of high-resolution satellite imagery through ICEYE's world-leading SAR constellation, supporting the Ukrainian Armed Forces with persistent situational awareness on tactical timelines," ICEYE representatives said in a January statement.

That likely explains Russia's interest in ICEYE satellites, which is extensive and longstanding. For example, Kosmos 2612 launched in April of this year with five other spacecraft. In May, four members of this fleet, including Kosmos 2612, conducted maneuvers to get into a co-planar orbit with ICEYE-X36. Such maneuvers use a lot of fuel and are therefore not performed lightly. (It's unclear if ICEYE-X36 is dedicated to capturing imagery of Ukraine or Russia, but Russia certainly seems to think it is.)

However, we still don't know the purpose of Kosmos 2612's close approach (which is apparently over, at least for now), Samson stressed.

There are a few possibilities. For example, Russia may want to get a better idea of ICEYE satellites' capabilities, and/or figure out what exactly they're studying on Earth below. Or maybe Kosmos 2612 was actively interfering with ICEYE-X36 in some way — messing with its sensors, perhaps, or blocking or intercepting its communications with the ground.

However, Samson leans toward another explanation.

"I think they're getting frustrated," she said of the Russians.

"They've been kind of in a stalemate, particularly for the past six months or so. And so I think it's just more a matter of demonstrating military prowess in a manner that the Ukrainians cannot respond to," she added. "The Ukrainians are just as good, perhaps even better than, the Russians on drones. But when it comes to space stuff, they're not peers, and this is just a way to remind everyone that that's the case."

As Kosmos 2612's activities show, space is becoming an increasingly competitive and contested domain. The U.S. has long held the advantage above Earth, fielding the most capable reconnaissance and communications craft, but Russia and China are challenging that dominance.

This drama is unfolding above our heads now, and it affects everyone on the planet. Samson therefore wishes that governments around the world were more forthcoming about their space activities — for example, that we learned about Kosmos 2612's shenanigans from the U.S .Space Force rather than from private companies and civilians who pore over government tracking data.

"I think it just speaks to the importance of transparency, and sharing information about these sort of things," she said. "As much as you may want to, you cannot keep the existence of satellites classified, because people can see them."
